Democrats eye state supreme court races — this year and beyond

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Liberal groups have spent millions in a handful of battleground state supreme court races ahead of Tuesday’s elections, looking to lay the groundwork for Democrats to win control of more courts ahead of a crucial redistricting cycle.

At the center of the fight are races for Republican-majority state supreme courts in North Carolina and Ohio, where this year’s results could set the stage for Democrats to flip the makeup of the benches in 2028. National groups are also spending to defend the liberal majority on Michigan’s court and on an open seat in Montana, where voters will also decide whether judicial elections remain nonpartisan.

The investments are yet another reflection of how state supreme court elections have become national political battlegrounds. The high court justices have become arbiters of major disputes, from abortion rights to election integrity and redistricting. Those who win in the coming cycles will likely preside over challenges to congressional and legislative maps that are drawn in a handful of states after the midterm election — and then nationwide after the 2030 census.

Nowhere are the contests more heated than in North Carolina.

Democrats’ defense of a seat currently held by Anita Earls is “step No. 2” to flip the North Carolina Supreme Court, said Anderson Clayton, the state’s Democratic Party chair. “In 2028, we’re looking at being able to take three Republican seats on that court.”

Democrats lost four seats on the state supreme court in the 2020 and 2022 elections, and the Republican majority cleared the way for a Republican-led gerrymandering in 2023.

Clayton said her group has spent more than $1 million on the state supreme court race. Earls has also drawn support from national Democrats like Pete Buttigieg and Maryland Gov. Wes Moore.

“If we had won a state Supreme Court race then in 2020 in our state, Hakeem Jeffries would be speaker of the House right now,” said Clayton, adding that her group is trying to build the foundation for congressional majorities “in 2032 and beyond.”

The ACLU is among the groups making long-term investments in state supreme court races. It has poured $8 million into state supreme court races in North Carolina, Michigan and Montana this year, in addition to Georgia and Wisconsin races earlier this year, said Deirdre Schifeling, the group’s chief political and advocacy officer, making it one of the top spenders in these contests.

The ACLU’s multi-cycle strategy to build “pro-civil rights, civil liberties majorities” is also targeting Arizona, Pennsylvania, Kentucky and Ohio, Schifeling said. The group already set aside resources for supreme court races in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania in 2027 and will begin fundraising for the 2028 races early next year.

More than $14 million has been spent on television ads in North Carolina, Michigan, Ohio and Montana, most of it benefiting Democratic and liberal-leaning candidates, according to estimates from the liberal-leaning Brennan Center for Justice. Most of the liberal candidates have also outraised their conservative competitors.

By contrast, the electoral headwinds have forced Republicans to defend statewide offices and state legislatures they currently control, leaving fewer resources for the judicial races.

Republican-aligned groups like the Republican State Leadership Committee — the party’s chief downballot campaign organization — have largely stayed on the sidelines this cycle after warning for months they were being swamped by Democrats. The organization’s judicial affiliate reported more than $5 million in expenditures across 2024 and 2025.

“Democrats understand what is at stake, and have dumped massive amounts of resources into these contests,” said RSLC President Edith Jorge-Tuñón in a statement. “The RSLC is fighting back with smart and targeted spending because the investments we make today will determine whether we are positioned to protect our majorities, compete in the next redistricting cycle, and secure Republican victories for years to come.”

The National Democratic Redistricting Committee — the party’s main redistricting-focused group — declined to discuss this year’s total spending in judicial races this year, but NDRC President John Bisognano said in a statement that “the upcoming 2026 and 2028 state supreme court elections are critical to the long-term fight to protect democracy.”

And those involved in supreme court fights said they believed spending will only increase down the road, particularly from major industry players.

“I think that the money flowing into those races from external interests tells the tale of what big money, big pharma, big data recognize about the next areas that they can seek to leverage and control to their benefit,” said Tish Gotell Faulks, the state courts director at the liberal Alliance for Justice Action. “Those interests are now turning to the state courts and seeking to capture those courts as well.”

Floating scarecrows could save thousands of seabirds from deadly fishing nets

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A scarecrow usually stands in a field. During a trial off the coast of southwest England, fishers floated two kinds on the sea instead, and both made their nets much less likely to catch diving seabirds.

One was a buoy topped with a spinning head of large, fake eyes. The other was a kite shaped like a bird of prey. With kites over a net, the odds of it catching any guillemots or razorbills fell by 71%, and with the buoys they fell by 59%.

Until now, evidence that anything keeps diving seabirds out of fishing nets has been limited.

Tom Horton of the University of Exeter led the analysis.

The trial itself was run by Fishtek Marine, a Devon company that makes the buoy, with Cornish fishers who had cameras fitted to their boats.

It covered one stretch of coast and one kind of fishing, so it can’t yet say how the devices would do anywhere else.

Diving birds drown in gillnets

A gillnet is a wall of fine mesh set in the water to catch fish. Seabirds that chase fish underwater, such as guillemots and razorbills, can swim into the same mesh and drown. Fishers call any animal caught by accident bycatch.

Researchers estimated in 2024 that about 31,000 common guillemots die this way each year in European waters alone. Fishers and scientists have tried fixes, from lighting up nets to changing the gear itself.

Even so, the authors describe the tools for protecting seabirds in gillnets as poorly developed.

The buoy came out of eight years of work by Fishtek Marine and the RSPB, Britain’s bird conservation charity.

In Estonia it cut the number of sea ducks nearby, but its record since then has been mixed. A 2023 trial found it didn’t reduce seabird bycatch in Iceland’s lumpfish fishery.

Cameras recorded every haul

The Cornish trial ran from October 2023 to March 2025. Ten fishers took part, all working close to shore, and each one set nets in matched groups.

In each group, one net had kites and another had buoys. A third had nothing. All three went into the same fishing grounds, so they could be compared fairly.

Over that stretch the boats set 1,057 nets, about 80 miles (129 kilometers) of net in all.

A camera system on every boat recorded each haul, and reviewers logged every animal that came up. The fishers were paid for taking part.

Three of the paper’s co-authors work for Fishtek Marine, which manufactures the buoy, and the paper declares that link. The kite is an off-the-shelf product.

Both devices cut the catch

The nets brought up 191 animals that nobody was trying to catch, and most of them were birds.

Guillemots and razorbills alone made up 138. Almost every animal was dead by the time it reached the boat.

The remaining animals included seals and cetaceans, such as dolphins and porpoises.

Both devices lowered the odds that a net would catch any guillemots or razorbills at all, compared with plain nets. On that measure, the kite and the buoy did about equally well.

When birds were caught, nets with buoys held 68% fewer of them for the same amount of fishing. Kite nets held fewer as well, but that gap was too uncertain to rule out chance.

In an interview with Earth.com, Horton said the size of the effect was what stood out. “The clearest surprise was just how strong and consistent the results were,” he said.

Timing and spacing both matter

GPS on each boat logged where along the net every animal turned up, so the team could measure how far each one was from the nearest device. Close in, the kites did better.

Only five birds were caught within about 80 feet (24 meters) of a kite.

Near the buoys, five birds had been caught within about 23 feet (7 meters). The buoys offered moderate protection over a wider area instead.

Where fishers put the devices may be just as important as which one they choose.

Birds can still be caught in the gaps between them, and the authors suggest kites placed every 200 to 260 feet (60 to 80 meters) could suit coastal gillnet fisheries.

Fishers caught the most birds for their effort in January and February.

Fishers targeting sea bass and mackerel caught birds at the highest rates, and so did those using mesh about 4 inches (100 to 110 millimeters) wide.

The authors wrote that deterrents aimed at those winter nets could protect many birds in a way fishers are likely to accept.

Fishers shaped the devices

The fishers generally found the buoys easy to handle and store. The kites performed worse in very light winds and were harder to manage in strong ones.

Setting extra devices along longer nets was the hardest part of the job, and several of the fishers’ suggestions have already gone into new prototypes.

Horton said one of the most encouraging parts of the study was how willing the fishers were to work together on testing new ideas.

“These are simple, low-cost tools that can help address the important issue of seabird bycatch in gillnet fisheries,” he told Earth.com.

Micky Bacon, a fisherman from Mousehole who took part, saw the trial as a chance to show what his industry wants.

“Fishermen don’t just want to go and catch fish, we also want to be part and parcel of improving the environment,” he said.

Questions beyond the trial

Seals are one open question. Fifteen seals turned up in nets with buoys, against six in plain ones. Kite nets held four.

Horton and his colleagues read that as a hint that the buoys might attract seals, and said more testing would be needed to confirm it.

They also noted that the trial spaced its devices to suit the buoy, which probably left gaps for the kites and may have affected the results.

Nor did the team measure water depth or wind, or whether birds stop reacting to the devices over time. Nearly a third of the nets stayed out overnight, and deterrents that birds have to see probably work best in daylight.

Horton said the next step is other waters. “We are keen to explore how these approaches perform in other fisheries and environmental settings, and whether they can be adapted to reduce bycatch more broadly,” he added.

The buoy deterred ducks in Estonia and then made no difference in Iceland, so one good result settles little.

Further trials will show whether these simple devices can protect seabirds across different fisheries, potentially preventing thousands of accidental deaths.

The full study was published in the journal Conservation Letters.
