A federal judge on Sunday dismissed the Justice Department’s lawsuit seeking to delay certification of Alaska’s Senate election amid the "Decoy Dan" controversy, rejecting the Trump administration’s bid two days before the midterm elections.

The DOJ said it would appeal.

The lawsuit centered on two candidates with similar names: Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan and challenger Daniel J. Sullivan Jr. The DOJ lawsuit sought to delay or block certification while investigating whether voter confusion may have affected the outcome.

U.S. District Judge Sharon Gleason said the government had not shown how the requested investigation could identify voters’ intent while protecting ballot secrecy. She also said the relief sought would intrude on Alaska’s responsibility to run its elections.

The DOJ said it would appeal, arguing the judge dismissed the case on procedural grounds without addressing its claim that Sullivan Jr. entered the race to confuse voters. The case now heads to the 9th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals.

The dispute has roiled the campaign in recent days. The New York Times reported that people connected to Democratic nominee Mary Peltola’s campaign helped recruit Sullivan Jr. to run as a spoiler. Peltola has denied involvement, and Sullivan Jr. has denied coordinating with her campaign.

Democrats eye state supreme court races — this year and beyond

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Liberal groups have spent millions in a handful of battleground state supreme court races ahead of Tuesday’s elections, looking to lay the groundwork for Democrats to win control of more courts ahead of a crucial redistricting cycle.

At the center of the fight are races for Republican-majority state supreme courts in North Carolina and Ohio, where this year’s results could set the stage for Democrats to flip the makeup of the benches in 2028. National groups are also spending to defend the liberal majority on Michigan’s court and on an open seat in Montana, where voters will also decide whether judicial elections remain nonpartisan.

The investments are yet another reflection of how state supreme court elections have become national political battlegrounds. The high court justices have become arbiters of major disputes, from abortion rights to election integrity and redistricting. Those who win in the coming cycles will likely preside over challenges to congressional and legislative maps that are drawn in a handful of states after the midterm election — and then nationwide after the 2030 census.

Nowhere are the contests more heated than in North Carolina.

Democrats’ defense of a seat currently held by Anita Earls is “step No. 2” to flip the North Carolina Supreme Court, said Anderson Clayton, the state’s Democratic Party chair. “In 2028, we’re looking at being able to take three Republican seats on that court.”

Democrats lost four seats on the state supreme court in the 2020 and 2022 elections, and the Republican majority cleared the way for a Republican-led gerrymandering in 2023.

Clayton said her group has spent more than $1 million on the state supreme court race. Earls has also drawn support from national Democrats like Pete Buttigieg and Maryland Gov. Wes Moore.

“If we had won a state Supreme Court race then in 2020 in our state, Hakeem Jeffries would be speaker of the House right now,” said Clayton, adding that her group is trying to build the foundation for congressional majorities “in 2032 and beyond.”

The ACLU is among the groups making long-term investments in state supreme court races. It has poured $8 million into state supreme court races in North Carolina, Michigan and Montana this year, in addition to Georgia and Wisconsin races earlier this year, said Deirdre Schifeling, the group’s chief political and advocacy officer, making it one of the top spenders in these contests.

The ACLU’s multi-cycle strategy to build “pro-civil rights, civil liberties majorities” is also targeting Arizona, Pennsylvania, Kentucky and Ohio, Schifeling said. The group already set aside resources for supreme court races in Wisconsin and Pennsylvania in 2027 and will begin fundraising for the 2028 races early next year.

More than $14 million has been spent on television ads in North Carolina, Michigan, Ohio and Montana, most of it benefiting Democratic and liberal-leaning candidates, according to estimates from the liberal-leaning Brennan Center for Justice. Most of the liberal candidates have also outraised their conservative competitors.

By contrast, the electoral headwinds have forced Republicans to defend statewide offices and state legislatures they currently control, leaving fewer resources for the judicial races.

Republican-aligned groups like the Republican State Leadership Committee — the party’s chief downballot campaign organization — have largely stayed on the sidelines this cycle after warning for months they were being swamped by Democrats. The organization’s judicial affiliate reported more than $5 million in expenditures across 2024 and 2025.

“Democrats understand what is at stake, and have dumped massive amounts of resources into these contests,” said RSLC President Edith Jorge-Tuñón in a statement. “The RSLC is fighting back with smart and targeted spending because the investments we make today will determine whether we are positioned to protect our majorities, compete in the next redistricting cycle, and secure Republican victories for years to come.”

The National Democratic Redistricting Committee — the party’s main redistricting-focused group — declined to discuss this year’s total spending in judicial races this year, but NDRC President John Bisognano said in a statement that “the upcoming 2026 and 2028 state supreme court elections are critical to the long-term fight to protect democracy.”

And those involved in supreme court fights said they believed spending will only increase down the road, particularly from major industry players.

“I think that the money flowing into those races from external interests tells the tale of what big money, big pharma, big data recognize about the next areas that they can seek to leverage and control to their benefit,” said Tish Gotell Faulks, the state courts director at the liberal Alliance for Justice Action. “Those interests are now turning to the state courts and seeking to capture those courts as well.”

Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens endorses Republican Mike Rogers for Michigan Senate

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Democratic Rep. Haley Stevens endorsed Republican Mike Rogers in the Michigan Senate race on Sunday — a stunning break with her party that derails Democrats’ attempts to create a united front for Abdul El-Sayed.

Stevens’ seismic step across party lines comes just days after she insisted she was still going to support El-Sayed despite their escalating public feud.

Tensions crescendoed through the weekend as Stevens attempted to condition a Sunday joint appearance with El-Sayed and Rep. Jim Clyburn (D-S.C.) on El-Sayed’s rejection of an ad from one of his allies that was critical of Israel. El-Sayed’s team then asked Stevens to stay away from his events, as POLITICO first reported.

“Abdul said that I was no longer welcome in his campaign and that I should stay away. And he’s right,” Stevens said in avideo posted to social media on Sunday after she appeared at a Detroit church alongside Rogers.

“And that’s why I am endorsing Mike Rogers for the United States Senate today,” she said. “Who we are as Michiganders is not hate, and it is not what we are getting out from our Democratic nominee right now.”

Her cross-party endorsement of the man she’d once hoped to defeat for a Senate seat, just two days out from a critical Senate election, underscores how fragile her public truce with El-Sayed was after a bitter August primary that deeply divided the party. And their infighting is splashing across social media at the most inopportune moment possible, as Democrats race to hold a competitive seat that could decide control of the chamber.

Rogers said he was “honored to have Haley Stevens’ support” and that they had spoken by phone earlier in the morning about her endorsement.

“We talked about America, what was better for Americans, what was better for the future of our state by bringing people together,” Rogers said while speaking to reporters alongside Stevens after the church service. “What we decided is: Sometimes the hate grows so loud that it jeopardizes some of our citizens in this state. … This is more a statement about standing together against hate and the future of this state and the future of this country than it is anything else.”

Stevens insisted she’s “not trying to harm anyone” and that she’s “not turning my back on Democrats.”

Representatives for El-Sayed did not immediately respond to requests for comment. But the Democratic nominee told reporters Sunday, "I actually don't think about Haley Stevens."

"I'm just less interested in the politics of it all than I am in the policy of it all. And so, look, if you're somebody who believes that we ought to have more rights, not less, then I would think you would support me in this race," El-Sayed said.

Stevens’ endorsement sparked immediate, scorching backlash from various corners of the Democratic Party. Several members of her team, including her chief of staff for eight years Justin German, resigned Sunday morning and said they will continue to work to elect El-Sayed on Nov. 3.

“Many amazing staffers went to work for Haley Stevens to fight for shared democratic values and keep this Senate seat blue. Some of those incredible people are now working their asses off to elect Abdul,” Stevens’ former campaign communications director, Arik Wolk, posted on X. “There are two Supreme Court seats and so many other values Haley claimed to care about on the line. By endorsing Mike Rogers she has betrayed all those values.”

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer and Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee Chair Kirsten Gillibrand reiterated their support for El-Sayed in statements Sunday.

"This is a despicable betrayal from Rep. Stevens," Schumer wrote on social media. "Senate Democrats are united in our support for Abdul El-Sayed, because we know he will fight for the economic interests of working families in Michigan, unlike Mike Rogers, who is a tool of Donald Trump and billionaire donors."

Stevens had denied she would endorse Rogers or go to church with him to senior Michigan and top national Democrats as recently as Saturday night, according to a Michigan Democratic official briefed on the matter, granted anonymity to share private details.

Earlier Sunday morning, Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-Md.) told NBC that he spoke to Stevens the night before and she would be “voting straight down the line on the Democratic ticket, and she’s going to be celebrating with Democrats across the country when we win on Tuesday night.”

Stevens also told a Michigan Democratic Party official Saturday afternoon that she planned to vote for El-Sayed, according to screenshots of text messages obtained by POLITICO.

“I am voting straight Dem!!!!,” she wrote. “I had a dream last night we won,” she added. “And I believe he will win! P.S. it was a happy dream.”

Her reversal just hours later confounded many Democrats.

“Michiganders are worried about how they are going to pay their mortgage and fill up their gas tanks … That’s what they’re talking about, not Congresswoman Stevens’ continued efforts to get attention,” Rep. Debbie Dingell (D-Mich.), who had been attempting to broker peace and helped organize the joint appearance between the primary rivals, said in a statement. “We must stay focused on electing Democrats up and down the ballot, and that includes electing Abdul El-Sayed as Michigan’s next Senator.”

Michigan Democratic Party Chair Curtis Hertel said in a statement — shortly before Stevens officially endorsed Rogers — that her decision to campaign with the Republican “shows that she has abandoned everything she claims to care about.”

“[El-Sayed] will still win. Haley swings no votes at this point. She just lit her career on fire for no reason,” said one senior Senate Democratic aide, who was granted anonymity to speak candidly.

In addition to their fury at Stevens, some Democrats are livid with El-Sayed for appearing to escalate the back and forth with Stevens in the final days.

“He couldn't leave it alone,” said another senior Democratic aide, who was granted anonymity to speak candidly.

Stevens said in the video that she does “not stand with Hasan Piker,” the far-left streamer who El-Sayed campaigned with ahead of the Democratic primary. (El-Sayed has since distanced himself from the streamer amid Piker’s controversial remarks about Sept. 11.)

She said remains a “proud Democrat.”

Republicans latched onto the endorsement quickly. Some believe this will strengthen their messaging that Democrats are a party of socialists and radicals in the final days of the election.

"When Congresswoman Haley Stevens, who ran against Abdul El-Sayed and knows him better than anyone, stands with Mike Rogers, Michigan should take notice. Democrats who feel Abdul has abandoned them have a home with Mike,” RNC spokesperson Hunter Lovell said in a statement.

The news is blowing up in some House GOP group chats, said Rep. Jack Bergman (R-Mich.).

“We always talk about the October surprise. It can be good or bad. Well, I think this is a bit of a positive surprise here 2 days before the before the polls finally close," he said.

Conservative activist James O’Keefe set out to expose alleged vulnerabilities in Minnesota’s election system. Now, he faces felony charges stemming from the undercover operation.

O’Keefe, formerly of Project Veritas and current CEO of the O’Keefe Media Group, and conservative influencer Cameron Higby were charged Wednesday with unlawful voting and identity theft after allegedly impersonating registered voters at Minneapolis polling places during the state’s Aug. 11 primary.

According to Hennepin County prosecutors, the men wore disguises, used fake accents and provided election workers with the names of registered voters without their knowledge or permission. They filmed the encounters and later posted videos claiming to expose weaknesses in Minnesota’s voter registration procedures.

The operation targeted the state’s *vouching* system, which allows registered voters to verify the residency of people seeking to register on Election Day. The process requires sworn statements and identifying information from prospective voters.

Neither O’Keefe nor Higby cast a ballot or signed the required vouching oath. Prosecutors contend that misrepresenting their identities while beginning the voting process was itself unlawful.

O’Keefe defended the operation as investigative journalism, calling the prosecution an attack on First Amendment rights. Higby also denied breaking the law, saying the pair intended to expose election vulnerabilities.

Each rung launders the one below it. The correction, when it comes, travels in the opposite direction and arrives nowhere.

Only 32% of Americans have high confidence that news orgs will report midterm election results accurately, survey finds

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

About six in 10 Americans said they have at least “some” confidence that news organizations will report the upcoming midterm election results accurately, according to a new national survey. But only 32% said they had “quite a bit” or “a great deal” of confidence that journalists would do the job.

In the survey from Medill Local News Initiative and the Alliance for Trust in Media, 37% said they had either “not too much confidence” or “no confidence at all” that news outlets would accurately report the election results.

The survey was completed by a nationally representative sample of 1,057 U.S. adults either online or by telephone earlier this month. The findings echo other national surveys, including a Gallup survey that showed trust in media to report “fully, accurately, and fairly” at 33% — an increase from an all-time low last year.

The Medill/Alliance survey found about half of Americans said they had “not too much confidence” or “no confidence at all” in news outlets to investigate voter fraud claims thoroughly. U.S. President Donald Trump has cast doubt on elections on 70% of days since returning to office — starting with false claims about voter fraud on the first day of his second term.

In another bleak result from the same poll, slightly more survey respondents said journalists manipulate the news for political or commercial purposes than report it accurately. Across the survey, Democrats reported being more confident in (and trusting of) news organizations than Republicans. Nearly 80% of Republicans said journalists try to manipulate the news compared to 38% of independents and 24% of Democrats.

Jefferson Fisher Has The Secret to Getting Along Better With Absolutely Everyone

📅 Jul 27, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

If being a parent, a husband, a man, a human has taught me anything at all it’s that communication is everything. Also, most of us suck at communication.

For once, our poor communication is not a product of big external forces — neither isolationist politics nor overcooked capitalism nor a failing of modern masculinity. We can only blame ourselves because, hey, communicating is hard. Getting your point across to a group, your nuclear family, or one single person takes practice and discipline, mistakes and learning. I don’t think we ever get it entirely right.

Well, most of us don’t. There are some communicators in this world that are … blessed. Jefferson Fisher is one such individual. Fisher is a Texan, dad, husband, and personal injury attorney who has made a name for himself through social videos that teach us all how to communicate better.

The videos offer pithy lessons like this: Instead of saying I disagree, “tweak it a little and say, I see things differently.” Why? “When you talk in terms of perspective, you find more connection.” Here’s another: “How I talk to my kids will affect how they talk to their kids.”

Simple, pointed, and poignant. You can see why millions tune in.

Fisher’s goal is to make people talk more and argue less. It says just that on the cover of his fantastic, practical, wonderfully written book The Next Conversation — a must read for all of us who strive to get along a little better with those around us.

We caught up with Fisher to talk about his new book and, naturally, anoint him as the latest Cool Dad. Here are some excerpts from our talk, including extras from the Cool Dad Questionnaire and his advice for the most important conversations you’ll ever have.

What’s one thing you wish your kids had today from your childhood?

Probably more of a reckless abandon about what they ate. I grew up in just every Little Debbie from Cosmic Brownies to ... everything you could think of that was probably terrible for us, but we didn't know any different.

What does your partner love about you?

I mean, this is my guess ... I would say what Sierra loves about me is my steadiness. When things don't go to plan, I'm pretty rock steady. So when there is something unexpected or the kids go into what we call their midnight madness, when it's right there at bedtime and they're absolutely losing their minds, that's when I'm able to step in and I think I'm able to really notice when she's overwhelmed, and take over.

And what do you think they maybe find annoying about you?

I mean, there's lots. I'm trying to find a really good one like she finds the most annoying about me is sometimes I can be a little bit occasionally of like a party-pooper where the kids are just ... all going nuts and I'm like, "Can we just not yell so much in our house?" So there's that.

Probably the bigger thing she gets more annoyed about is my relationship with money. Its just money. So I'm the spender and so she is the saver. If it's something that's like, "Hey, but this would be fun, even though it's not something we exactly need right now, but it would be a cool memory," I think there's a lot of that.

“Fatherhood... is a daily devotion to putting your wife, your spouse, your partner, your kids before you.”

What does fatherhood mean to you in our current world?

Fatherhood, with the caveat of doing it well to be a good father, I think, is knowing it's a discipline and not a destination. It's a daily devotion to putting your wife, your spouse, your partner, your kids before you. And it's this idea of ... are you inconveniencing them for your comfort or is it the opposite, are you willing to inconvenience yourself for their comfort?

There are times where it's going to be uncomfortable. I don't want to be the one carrying all the bags all the time, but is it what my family needs in that moment in the airport? Yeah, that's what they need. Can I do it? Yeah.

I got to say that the carrying bags all the time is a wonderful metaphor.

Yeah. You're the one that is supposed to shoulder the heavy. So you're supposed to carry what they can't. I'm sure there's a similarity to this phrase of to be what others cannot, you must be willing to suffer what others will not, and so it's like that. To be the dad that others can't, you got to be able to do what others won't.

How do you better communicate with your partner in parenting? Do you think there's ways that you, something that don't apply, or do you follow the advice in your book and have great arguments and conversations with your spouse?

I wouldn't say there are things that don't apply. I would say what it would need to go deeper on is the theme of repair specifically. So you could have the mindset of arguments and that's something that's very helpful. There's the idea of saying things with control, meaning be regulated and knowing when you need to take that timeout, and using the pauses. But the turn the book doesn't make with regard to marital relationships is repair.

We are talking about words of affirmation, affirming somebody's feelings, being able to take ownership of your words and what that actually sounds like ... how to truly use words of empathy and affirm how they feel, how to say things that are going to bring. We hit on connection in the book, but more in the way of framing non-relationship conversations.

In repair, there is a lot of something as simple as, "Yes, I said that. Yes, I did that. I absolutely can see how that would have frustrated you or upset you," or "I think it's totally reasonable for you to feel this way. I don't blame you for being upset about that. I could have done that better." And then how to actually, words of apology, how to repair after a rupture, that to me is, that's a whole different ballgame, whole different set of skills.

“There's a difference between [that and] saying, ‘How are you and how's your heart?’”

What do you think about checking in? I'm wondering where that falls in the communication line. It's maybe a little bit of the repair, but is that an important thing to do or how do you feel about just what a felt of unspoken understanding between family?

There's an element that my wife and I do and that's check-ins and check-outs. I mean, it's what we call them, things that we do in the morning and things that we do in the evening. Otherwise, there are times you may never talk to your spouse in the day and you know what that's like. Before you know it, you're just two people, somehow there's a woman who lives with me. I don't know who she is, but she's here and you can be roommates that way if you're not checking in with each other. The other is that unspokenness of knowing when something is wrong and that you feel like you might be approaching things from a different vantage. I like asking the phrase, "What's coming up?" "What's coming up for you?"

Instead of me saying, "What's wrong with you" or even, "What's up with you?" That tends to get a pretty defensive response. But if I say something as simple as what's coming up, there's a difference between [that and] saying, "How are you and how's your heart?"
