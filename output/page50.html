Subaru Thinks It Can Steal More American Buyers From Its Rivals

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Subaru Sees More Room to Grow in America

Subaru has carved out a loyal following in the US, one of its biggest strongholds outside Japan. But the brand clearly thinks it’s just getting started.

According to its 2026 Integrated Report, Subaru is eyeing a 5% share of the US car market. That’s a big leap from 3.9% at the end of 2025, and it’ll take more than just keeping the current fanbase happy. Subaru wants to plug the holes in its lineup and win over drivers of all ages, coast to coast. It’s a bold move for a brand that’s usually stuck to its niche. The automaker’s challenge now is to cast a wider net without losing the quirky charm that made it a household name in the first place.

Subaru

A Bigger Lineup and More Flexible Production

Subaru’s game plan is to roll out more models and make its factories quick on their feet. The idea is to fill in the lineup gaps and give shoppers more reasons to take a second look. Subaru’s also got its sights set on selling over 1.2 million cars a year worldwide by the early 2030s. That’s a big jump from the 896,000 it managed during the fiscal year that ended March 2026.

According to the company, flexible production is key here. Subaru’s Yajima plant in Japan started cranking out battery-electric cars in early 2026, and now the same line can build Toyota EVs right next to gas-powered Foresters.

Come fall 2026, that plant will also be churning out Subaru Forester SUVs for the US, backing up Subaru’s Indiana factory. That should help Subaru keep up when demand spikes or the market throws a curveball.

Subaru’s also sharpening its pencil with the Cost Innovation 20-30 plan, aiming to slash about ¥200 billion ($1.26 billion) a year from its expenses by 2030. That extra cash could help cushion the blow from rising costs and those pesky US tariffs.

Subaru Needs More Than Its Existing Reputation

Subaru’s already got a strong identity in America, but snagging 5% of the market means luring in folks who’ve never given the brand a second thought. That might mean jumping into segments where the competition is already throwing elbows. At this point, Subaru’s been making changes to its pricing, with base models being reintroduced for the 2027 model, lowering the price of entry.

Subaru has to walk a fine line: attract new fans without alienating the loyalists. Just tossing more models into the mix won’t cut it if they don’t bring something special to the table. Its production changes and cost-cutting efforts are sensible steps, particularly as automakers face pressure from tariffs and shifting demand. However, the bigger challenge will be making the lineup relevant to a wider audience.

Subaru knows what it wants and has a roadmap to get there. Whether it can actually poach buyers from the big names will come down to the cars it rolls out and how well they click with American drivers.

Saving water in the West could come with a hidden wildfire risk

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Letting a thirsty field go dry in a drought looks like a clean trade: less water pumped, more left in the river. The field doesn’t stay empty, though.

Across most of the West where farmland has gone out of production, grass and brush grew back in its place, and both burn more readily than a crop does.

Farmland, meanwhile, often acts as a barrier to advancing wildfires.

Retiring farmland therefore involves another trade-off: water saved at the potential cost of a natural firebreak.

Until now, nobody had measured the second half of that bargain across the region.

Austen Lorenz of Aythya Analytics and Beatrice Gordon of the Desert Research Institute in Reno co-led the work, alongside a fire manager from the National Interagency Fire Center.

They mapped 9,948 wildfires across 11 western states between 1985 and 2024, each one at least 247 acres (100 hectares).

Then they compared the land just inside each fire’s final edge with the land just outside.

Fires tend to stop at field edges

Each fire perimeter came from Monitoring Trends in Burn Severity, a federal satellite record of large fires. The team drew a ring about 300 feet (90 meters) wide on either side of each edge.

Everything inside the ring was sorted into forest, grass, scrub, wetland, bare ground, developed land, or cropland.

Cropland was about 2.8 times as likely to lie in the outer ring as in the inner one. Grass was the only cover found more often inside a fire than outside it.

Of the 2,452 fires with cropland within 300 feet of the edge, 2,242 had more of it outside than inside, which is 91.4%. Only bare ground did as well at that distance.

When the team widened the rings to about 3,300 feet (1,000 meters), cropland outperformed bare ground as well.

Cropland stayed outside fires in every year of the record. It still did when the team looked only at valley bottoms, where most farms sit, so the low ground alone doesn’t explain it.

Why wet fields don’t burn

Crops hold more water in their tissue than wild grass does. Irrigation keeps them wet through the fire season in California’s Central Valley, Washington’s Columbia Basin, and Idaho’s Snake River Plain.

A harvested or tilled field is also low and even, with no tall fuel to carry flames upward.

Firefighters add a third reason. They use irrigated fields as safety zones and as anchor points for control lines. A field’s edge is often where a crew built its control line.

Gordon is a hydrologist, and she said her field has asked how much water crops use far more often than what the land becomes afterward.

“After working with wildfire managers, it became clear that drought adaptation strategies that alter cropland can influence how wildfire moves through the landscape,” Gordon said.

“It’s a reminder that out here in the West, we don’t have the luxury of thinking of just one hazard anymore.”

Where the farmland went

The reasons to retire a field are real. Irrigated farms are the region’s largest water user, and the snowpack and groundwater they draw on are both shrinking.

In the Central Valley, taking an acre out of production saves between half an acre-foot of water a year for grain or hay and 2.3 acre-feet for almonds.

A typical American household uses about 0.45 acre-feet a year. One fallowed almond acre, then, frees up roughly what five households use.

In the 477 watersheds where farms and fires have met since 1985, about 10.6 million acres (4.29 million hectares) of cropland have become something else.

In 77% of those watersheds, most of that land went to grass or scrub. That shift runs across the Pacific Northwest, the Inland Northwest, the Intermountain West, and the Great Basin.

Grass was about one-fifth as likely as cropland to lie outside a fire line, and scrub about half as likely. Much of that land is old farmland left fallow on purpose, which the authors call a practical and widely used way to cut farm water use.

The water savings are real. But losing farmland can also mean losing a valuable firebreak.

Houses replace the fields

In the other 19% of watersheds, 91 in all, the biggest change was cropland turning into housing.

Those watersheds cluster around growing metro areas in California, the Pacific Northwest, Utah’s Wasatch Front, and Colorado’s Front Range. They account for about 1.9 million acres (774,000 hectares) of lost cropland.

The loss works out to roughly 130 acres a day for 39 years, close to a hundred football fields every day. Developed land was about half as likely as cropland to lie outside a fire’s edge.

Building on a field removes the buffer and adds the things that start fires and make them harder to fight.

Houses bring ignition sources, structures to protect, and people to evacuate while the fire is still growing.

Balancing water and wildfire risk

A fire’s final edge is set by weather, terrain, fuel, and how hard crews fought it, and this study can’t pull those apart. It shows that cropland lies where fires stop, not how much of that is the crop itself.

The authors argue the distinction matters less for planning than it sounds, though. Wet plants, open ground, or a place for crews to work all leave with the cropland, whichever one matters most.

The authors also did not estimate how much wildfire risk any one field’s retirement adds, or how that compares with the water it saves.

The team wants to build that calculation next, so a water district weighing a fallowing program can set the water saved against the fire risk added.

They also point to the USDA’s Conservation Reserve Program, which already pays landowners to idle sensitive land and could add fire buffering to its criteria.

“We want to stress that there’s no binary answer here – the complexity can’t be reduced to simply ‘fire is bad’ or ‘cropland is water intensive.’ Hopefully, this can help us evaluate where the trade-offs are steepest when we’re looking at compounding hazards,” Gordon said.

The full study was published in the journal Environmental Research Letters.

Scientists give AI chatbots a new way to read, with surprising results

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An AI chatbot can write a whole essay about a word and still struggle to spell that word backward. That’s because most AI language models never see individual letters.

Before reading anything, they break text into chunks of whole words or pieces of words.

Researchers have now converted several existing models to read text in bytes, the small units computers use to represent text. Unlike word-based chunks, bytes allow models to process text at a much finer level.

The conversion took less than 1% of the training such a model normally needs. Until now, a model that read text this way had to be built from scratch, and those models lagged behind.

Benjamin Minixhofer of the Allen Institute for AI and the University of Cambridge led the work.

Valentin Hofmann of the Allen Institute and LMU Munich was the senior author, with co-authors at the University of Washington and Imperial College London.

The team tested openly available models with up to about 8 billion parameters, the adjustable values a model learns in training.

Why chatbots miss letters

Before a language model reads a sentence, a separate program splits it into tokens. A common word usually becomes one token, and a rarer word gets broken into a few pieces.

The model learns from those pieces and never directly sees the letters inside them.

Chunking makes models efficient, but it has costs.

Models built this way have trouble with tasks that depend on single characters, which matters for computer code and for DNA and other biological sequences.

A fixed vocabulary of chunks also tends to favor English.

Models that read raw bytes avoid those problems. But byte-level models trained from scratch have trailed the chunk-based kind, and every leading chatbot model still reads in chunks.

Converting a model, not building one

Minixhofer’s team didn’t start over. They kept the core of each finished model and added new, smaller parts that read bytes and group them into chunks the core can work with. They call the process byteification.

In the first stage, only the new parts were trained, with the goal of making the converted model behave exactly like the original. In the second stage, the whole model learned to use the letter-level detail.

The whole process used about 49 billion tokens of text, less than 1% of the training data typically used to build a comparable model from scratch.

The team converted three openly available models this way: Qwen3, Llama 3 and Olmo 3. The converted versions got new names, Bwen, Blama and Bolmo.

The converted models kept up

On a set of tests covering science, math, coding, reading and general knowledge, the converted models landed close to the models they came from.

Bolmo averaged 62.5, against 62.3 for the original Olmo 3. Bwen averaged 69.8, against 70.3 for Qwen3.

In other words, the converted models retained nearly all of their general abilities.

The converted models also beat every earlier byte-reading model of similar size.

On science, technology, engineering and math questions, Bolmo scored 16.5 points higher than one such model trained from scratch.

The team could also give a converted model new skills without more training.

Copying over the changes that had taught Olmo 3 to follow instructions raised Bolmo’s instruction-following score by 36 percentage points, roughly matching the instruction-trained Olmo 3.

In speed, the converted models came close to the originals. They can also be trained to read bigger chunks at once, which makes them faster at some cost in accuracy.

Letters became a strength

The biggest gains came on letter-level tasks, such as spelling out a word, reversing it, or swapping and deleting characters.

On those tests, Bolmo scored 78.1 against 54.3 for the original Olmo 3, and Blama scored 81.0 against 57.2 for Llama 3.

Hofmann, a junior professor at LMU Munich who studies language processing with AI, described the gap directly.

“Traditional LLMs struggle with tasks that require character-level capabilities, such as spelling a word backwards,” Hofmann said. “Our byteified models are substantively better at this.”

Part of that gain came from practice rather than from reading bytes. The team added about 75 million tokens of training text built around letter tasks, roughly 0.04% of the training data.

An unconverted copy of Olmo 3 trained on the same mix reached 70.7, so the byte-reading design added the rest.

Limits of the conversion

Not every score went up. The converted models were less likely than the originals to write working computer code on the first try. Given many tries, though, they more often produced at least one answer that worked.

A split result like that could mean the converted models write more varied code, but differences in the settings used to generate answers could also explain it.

The letter-practice text was all in English, and the models tested stopped at about 8 billion parameters.

Whether the conversion works as well on much larger models hasn’t been tested. Neither has the idea that reading bytes would help with languages other than English, which the team lists as a potential benefit.

The team has released its models, code and training data, so other groups can run those checks themselves.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.

United went big on credit cards in 2026. More changes are coming, MileagePlus boss says

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Before he ran United Airlines' MileagePlus program, Jarad Fisher was the type of traveler who went "all-in" for elite status.

He read TPG, collected miles and occasionally went on ambitious end-of-year "status runs."

"My wife thought I was nuts," he joked, recalling an epic out-and-back trip to Hawaii 12 years ago — where he never even left the airport — in order to qualify.

Now, Fisher is in charge of United's loyalty program as it undergoes one of its biggest transformations in recent memory.

Ironically, one of his first orders of business was eliminating flight segments as a status metric.

United Airlines Premier entrance at Newark Liberty International Airport (EWR). SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

Starting in 2027, "Premier Qualifying Flights" are out. That means United loyalists will no longer be "graded" on how many actual flights they rack up.

Instead, the carrier will rely exclusively on PQPs, which are based on a combination of members' spending, both on flights and on their United credit cards.

United has framed the change as a way to make qualifying for status simpler — though it could make it more challenging for some United flyers.

It will also end the need for eleventh-hour "segment runs" that once had frequent flyers (like Fisher) crisscrossing the country in December to rack up enough elite-qualifying flights.

"That was an old relic," he said, speaking about United's recent changes in an exclusive interview with TPG.

"You're taking seats away from people who actually want to get to a certain destination. It's not great for the environment. It's not a great use of time," he explained. "We thought it made more sense for them to have one, consistent metric."

United Airlines Airbus A319. SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

That's not the only change United has made to MileagePlus in 2026.

United's 2026 MileagePlus changes

Earlier this year, the carrier upended several of its loyalty policies, heavily tilting its program toward members who carry one of its credit cards.

  • United's card-carrying flyers now earn more miles on flights than they used to.
  • Non-cardholders now earn fewer miles than before.
  • Customers with a United card now also have access to the lowest award redemptions.
  • Cardholders skirt some of the tightest restrictions when booking one of the airline's basic economy fares.

Together, these moves were among the most aggressive any U.S. airline has made to nudge flyers toward its cobranded credit cards.

It's part of a larger strategy Fisher often sums up with this mantra:

"It's good to fly United, it's great to be a MileagePlus member," he said. "But the best way to engage with us is to be a MileagePlus member and actually have one of our cards."

Expect more changes along those lines.

United's loyalty shake-up isn't done

Speaking publicly this year, United's chief commercial officer, Andrew Nocella, has hinted that United's loyalty shakeup isn't done.

SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

It appears some changes may have to wait until the airline can re-negotiate its credit card deal with banking partner Chase.

"While we continue to work under a long-term cobrand contract," Nocella said on an earnings call in April, "We're making changes to what we can control today."

United's current Chase deal is set to run through 2029, according to past TPG reporting.

For now, the carrier has significantly upped the incentives for customers to carry one of its cards in the first place: Over the past year, United's cobranded credit card holders have clearly gained the upper hand in its program.

Fisher said the airline plans to continue adapting the program in ways "that will be more consistent" with that ethos in the coming years.

"And so I think as a function of that, we're looking at not only the core loyalty program, we're looking at our partnerships," he said. "And we're trying to make it so that we will continue to be — not just the best airline loyalty program — but we're really aspiring to be the best loyalty program in the world."

SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

What could be next for MileagePlus?

What future changes could MileagePlus members ultimately see when they go to redeem miles, earn on their Lyft rides or enter a United Club?

All of that remains to be seen.

And Fisher will play a big part in those decisions, after taking the helm of MileagePlus this year with an intriguing pedigree.

Seven years ago, he was heavily involved in the launch of Apple Card.

Before that, he spent time at top rival Delta Air Lines, helping turbocharge its partnership with American Express — which today is the industry's most lucrative tie-up, set to bring Delta $9 billion this year.

That resume has Fisher thinking beyond earn rates and status benchmarks as he looks to build on changes that gave United flyers plenty to digest in 2026.

"We're really trying to think about what will truly help us break away from the pack," he said of the carrier's future plans.

Delta Air Lines Boeing 757-200. SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

Of course, that's easier said than done in the airline world.

Flight delays are inevitable. Loyal flyers can grow frustrated when they go to book a redemption and are shocked by how many miles they'll need.

Not to mention, complimentary status upgrades don't clear as often as they used to, as first-class seats are increasingly filled by customers who actually paid for their tickets. By the way, balancing paid first-class seats with those long upgrade queues is a dilemma Fisher said the airline is "consistently looking at."

"More folks are paying for [premium seats], which of course is putting pressure on the system," he acknowledged.

United's first-class seats on an Airbus A321neo. SEAN CUDAHY/THE POINTS GUY

But for an airline that has made plenty of waves in recent years — from its on-board internet to its international routes — it's safe to say United won't shy away from trying new things on the loyalty front, either.

And it's a likely bet that customers who carry its credit cards will benefit most.

"If you think about the amount of time people spend with us, and on board our aircraft, we want them to feel known. We want them to feel recognized," he said. "You will continue to see us become even more focused and deliberate about that approach."
