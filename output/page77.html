Could AI give older creatives a second act?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Advertising is made for the young by the young. 

That’s conventional wisdom backed up by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, which in 2025 reported the median age of ad industry workers was 39, compared to 42 for the broader workforce. Meanwhile, a report from the World Advertising Research Center estimates that only 5% to 10% of budgets are directed toward older consumers.

Today, brands focus on Gen Z customers and ad companies target Gen Z hires, all on newer platforms like TikTok. As the new guard sweeps in, the people who built the previous era creep toward irrelevance. As AI is impacting every aspect of the industry, it’s only natural to assume that it, too, will usher in a new era of ad makers and consumers. 

It doesn’t have to. Some of the most compelling artists who incorporate AI I encounter are not necessarily very young workers coming straight out of school. They are older, established game designers, VFX artists, concept artists, animators, photographers, and directors who have spent decades devising concepts and developing characters, images, and entire imaginary worlds that have earned fandoms.  

These professionals have played with light, composition, performance, movement, texture, and narrative. They know how to make the moment big and cinematic and, more importantly, they pick up on when it feels wrong.

AI can generate campaigns, but it cannot replicate genuine experience. And if these experienced workers can leverage the technology, it could, potentially, revitalize their careers.

The Last Generation of Analog Talent

The common assumption is that AI belongs to digital natives. But knowing how to run a new model is not the same as knowing what to create with it. 

Those who came up in the analog era can immediately discern when an image lacks tension or a lens choice feels off. They know when a character could go viral and move them to the front of the scene. That’s judgment that comes with experience, not with AI.

Great things happen when proven talents are unleashed in new areas: Think of editors generating the images they once had to search for, or a game designer taking their vision to directing a commercial shoot. 

It’s understandable, though, that agencies recruit youth for AI. After all, the algorithm on career sites seeks AI talent based on specific tool fluency, and doesn’t delve into broader AI exposure and career experience.

But those who are looking find a technologically attuned older workforce. A new report by job training nonprofit CWI Works and IPSOS found that 90% of people 55 and older who have used AI for work were somewhat comfortable with AI, and 50% were extremely comfortable using it. Among Gen X respondents, 71% have used generative AI, tracking right alongside the 74% of millennials. 

This is not a closed-minded cohort. Just because someone isn’t a 22-year-old grad doesn’t mean they’re not curious about this tool. That curiosity, combined with decades of experience, is a powerful thing.

Prompting is not the profession

AI skills matter, but new tools expire quickly. Today’s specialization is tomorrow’s button click. Taste, however, can never become a button.

I have worked as a copywriter and creative director at agencies for a decade. When I started Dolsten & Co. 18 months ago as an AI-first creative agency and production company, I was building something new but was inspired by the stellar talent I’d learned from through my career. 

In recent months, Dolsten & Co. brought in an experienced copywriter, game designer, and storyboard artist with decades of experience to work on board. The copywriter built an immersive digital experience for us, while we enlisted the game designer to build a world for a TV commercial, and the storyboard artist shaped a beautiful narrative for a brand film. Their careers had been displaced by industry upheaval and hiring biases—consolidations, cost cutting, and a push for offices to “look” younger—but talent remains, and the three are shaping brand narratives and AI-driven content for leading brands using their rich experience. 

I knew that the AI skills would come, and that they’d enable these kinds of valuable, second-act experts to bring incredible ideas to life. Our hiring philosophy has brought incredibly diverse talent.

Experience is never obsolete

Advertising is notoriously dismissive of older creative talent. Experience is expensive, and senior people are pushed toward smaller management pools. Specialists can find their craft devalued when the industry adopts a different production model. 

These are valid reasons for the talent drain, but AI creates the possibility of a different trajectory. This doesn’t mean younger creators are less important. The most interesting teams combine youth’s speed, experimentation, and cultural fluency with the judgment and discipline of experienced artists.

AI will continue to automate certain tasks and compress workflows. As the creative industry dwells on what AI is replacing, the more provocative question is who it will allow to begin again.

How HomeView foreshadows Apple’s post-app, Siri-first future

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As the iPhone grew into an unprecedented success, Apple was marching its users toward millions of downloads to the drumbeat of “There’s an app for that.” Meanwhile, Amazon and Google were flooding homes with waves of TV platforms, smart speakers, and cameras. These products and their underlying connectivity standards formed a deep foundation to support a new generation of AI-driven services far surpassing those of Google Assistant and the original Alexa.

At its home product launch on October 13, Apple updated its Apple TV and HomePod mini speakers. But all eyes were on HomeView. The touchscreen family hub is spinning and tilting the company toward a new direction led by a reinvented Siri and away from a dedicated app-first ecosystem.

If the iPad was originally dismissed as a large iPod Touch, the HomeView—with its 1:1 aspect ratio and widget-driven display—may at first evoke a large Apple Watch. But not for long. The device can be mounted on a wall or tilted on a domed aluminum speaker base that has the metallic texture of a Mac mini as opposed to the cozier cloth stylings of a HomePod mini. And if the frenetic 3-minute family morning tableau Apple produced to showcase the device proves accurate, we can expect it to serve up a whirlwind of multimodal interactions as opposed to the tap-and-takeaway interactions of Apple’s wrist computer.

On the other hand, HomeView’s platform philosophy borrows from another Apple experience: CarPlay, which presents apps installed on the connected iPhone on automakers’ dashboard displays. While HomeView is an independent device, it still has strong ties to the iPhone, which provides its personalization and app engine.

Amazon and Google smart screens have long featured cameras and personalized their experiences for recognized individuals. But Apple has enabled much richer personalization on the fly by using iPhone proximity to determine who’s using HomeView. It’s a best-of-both-worlds approach that Google should absolutely adopt with Android should it revive its Nest Home displays. The approach would also benefit both the Apple TV box and service, the latter of which trails Netflix, Disney+, Peacock, and others in supporting viewer profiles.

No App Store, but all Apple

Though HomeView offers far more of a standalone experience than CarPlay, it doesn’t have its own app store. Nor does it introduce a “homeOS” (Apple says it’s powered by “HomeView software”) to complement Apple’s family of operating systems for its computers, phones, watches, and TV devices. It‘s Apple’s first “post-app” device, similar to the way the iPhone was its first major “post-PC” platform.

Of course, that doesn’t mean that apps have vanished, any more than PCs (including Macs) did in the post-PC era. But as it created a device that serves as a primary interface for smart homes, Apple—like Google and Amazon before it— has recognized that an app store is not the ideal approach.

Developers can indirectly introduce apps onto HomeView by including support for the device in their iPhone apps, the same way CarPlay works. In fact, third-party iPhone audio and video apps that support CarPlay, such as Spotify and Audible, should work out of the gate on HomeView.

However, this approach can lead to relevant apps not getting noticed by consumers, if they’re not as compelling on the iPhone. Conversely, less relevant HomeView apps could also show up on the device simply because they’re companion components of an iPhone app.

In any case, while the HomeView may not have as rich an app ecosystem as the iPad, it’s still relatively app-centric compared to its Amazon and Google counterparts. Moreover, customers will appreciate that there isn’t an ad in sight, in contrast to Amazon’s Echo Show.

Home hubs such as HomeView pose a unique privacy challenge because they are far more likely to capture information that some family members might want to keep private from other family members than, say, a streaming device like Apple TV. As major ecosystem vendors feed far more intimate knowledge about us into AI than was ever captured for advertising personalization, the stakes for privacy will rise even further.

HomeView provides an exceptionally good platform for Apple to promote its commitment to privacy protection. In addition to the iPhone-driven personalization, communications are protected by Apple’s Private Cloud Compute. The device’s rollout also marked the debut of cameras and thermostats from LG and Schneider that feature high-end design and adhere to Apple’s privacy standards.

Despite the appeal of the privacy pitch, it’s unlikely that HomeView will be much of a beachhead into homes that aren’t already deep into Apple’s ecosystem. It’s enmeshed in a web of Apple services and technologies beyond Siri, including Apple’s cloud, video and music services, FaceTime, Home, Safari, Handoff, and even Find My. (Despite the focus on touchscreen interactions, there’s no support for Apple Arcade or iOS games in general.)

Striving with Siri

Like other smart screens, HomeView has a rich touch interface. However, as Apple’s first post-app device, it is also the company’s first Siri-first device. The past few iPhone launches have leaned heavily into spotlighting Apple Intelligence and showing how its revitalized agent is creating and improving new experiences, but smart screens are a new twist, and are often accessed from across the room. The new Siri’s more natural conversation and far greater knowledge and skills make HomeView a much more compelling proposition, one that stands to improve more rapidly because it learns consistently from iPhone usage.

This year has seen Apple jump into categories where competitors had a longstanding presence. Samsung introduced the first Galaxy Z Fold in 2019 and Amazon’s Echo Show debuted two years before that. Functionally, Siri closes the gap with devices that only recently got access to Gemini Home and Alexa+. And true to form, Apple has leveraged late market entry to shake up some category assumptions.

HomeView’s mix of closeup and distant functionality has raised the bar for the incumbent devices from Amazon and Google. And its departure from Apple’s standard practice of dedicating an operating system and app store to each major device category could presage a new era for the company’s devices.
