Guarding the Gates of Knowledge

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


A recent scroll through social media got me thinking about trust. About half my feed was filled with AI slop that astute users would call out as being machine-generated, often to the embarrassment of the rube who unknowingly spread it and sometimes to the delight of the poster who shared it deliberately because they found it amusing or useful for sowing confusion.

Remember the good old days of GPT-3 when we could ask the chatbot anything and it would almost instantly and very confidently spit back a plausible but false answer? That’s when the word “hallucination” as applied to AI answers entered the lexicon. Six years and several versions of a few different chatbots later, I still can’t trust the answers I get, even though Claude or Gemini often do useful work for me. That’s the thing about current AI: Trust but verify. And verify you must.

Apply that maxim at scale to maintain the integrity of the scientific record and you get an idea of the challenge facing publishers of peer-reviewed scholarship. In “I Used AI to Fake a Scientific Paper,” freelance writer and doctor of applied mathematics Benjamin Skuse tells the story of the human gatekeepers trying to beat back the rising tide of AI slop by attempting to get his own AI-generated papers past IEEE’s defenses.

“He basically hit ‘run experiment’ and ‘write paper.’ And then he didn’t do a damn thing to it.” —Eliza Strickland

As features and AI beat editor Eliza Strickland told me, Skuse was surprised at just how effortless it was to generate a paper that was more or less plausible and more or less science. For one of his experiments, “he basically hit ‘run experiment’ and ‘write paper,’” she said. “And then he didn’t do a damn thing to it. He just launched it into the maw of the publishing engine.”

Skuse discovered that the combination of IEEE’s human-review processes and research-integrity checker programs are currently keeping pace in what amounts to a cat-and-mouse game against people using free tools. But the more advanced LLMs are no longer making the same dumb mistakes the free versions do. That leaves publishers locked in an arms race between increasingly plausible slop and the systems aimed at detecting it.

Scholarly publishers aren’t the only ones trying to hold the line against slop. So is that global depository of human-generated knowledge, Wikipedia. For this month’s Five Questions, Strickland interviewed Ellia Lebleu, a founding member of WikiProject AI Cleanup, a volunteer effort to detect and delete AI-generated material from the platform. Lebleu’s team is confronting the same problem as commercial publishers, but with fewer people and fewer tools.

“Wikipedia is known as a repository of human-generated information, and they want to hold on to that,” says Strickland. “Who knows how long they’ll be able to do that.”

If we can’t collectively assume that the scientific record is trustworthy or that basic facts about the world are accurate, then our understanding of what is real unspools into uncertainty. Perhaps that delights quantum scientists and the grifters who thrive in an information environment where there is no shared reality. But that uncertainty ultimately undermines trust in the institutions that exist to curate the corpus of human knowledge and to promote the advancement of science and technology for the benefit of humanity.

Or maybe in a world where AI is doing the science and writing the papers, it won’t matter whether or not humans trust the outputs. As Strickland put it, “Doesn’t it just feel crazy that we’ll have AI writing for other AIs at some point? We’ll just get left out of the loop.”

Facing a Trump backlash, New York Republicans blame blue state incumbents

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

ALBANY, New York — New York Republicans have a closing argument for voters unhappy with the cost of living: Blame the incumbent Democrats who run Albany.

President Donald Trump’s deep unpopularity in his native state — made worse by high gas prices and a Canadian tariff war — has created a nightmare environment for the GOP in this deep blue state. Yet Republican candidates, strategists and former officeholders believe their party can leverage New Yorkers’ vexation with high taxes, housing costs and skyrocketing utility bills to offset the midterm agony their party is poised to suffer on Tuesday.

Gubernatorial nominee Bruce Blakeman’s final TV ad warns voters that Democratic Gov. Kathy Hochul’s reelection will result in a tax hike at the behest of her political ally, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani. On the trail, he blasts the sharp increase in energy costs and pledges to reverse the state’s 12-year-old ban on natural gas drilling along the Pennsylvania border. And he’s pledging to bolster public safety by repealing a package of criminal justice law changes.

Republicans hope those attacks will offset Democrats’ efforts to make the election a referendum on Trump. GOP candidates running in down-ballot House and state legislative contests have used Hochul as a foil — a reflection of her middling poll numbers for much of the year.

Rep. Mike Lawler, running in a district Trump lost by one point two years ago, has gleefully tied Democrats to Hochul.

“If Democrats in New York complain about affordability, we have the highest state taxes in the country, income and property taxes. We have chronically poor educational performance. We have a housing market that’s stultified,” said former Rep. John Faso, a Republican who ran for governor in 2006. “Since the Democrats have been running New York state at the statewide level for many years — it’s hard to blame all of that on Donald Trump.”

Hochul’s campaign insists New York voters will positively affirm her five years in office.


“From the beginning, this race has been about records: Governor Hochul has put money back in New Yorkers’ pockets, invested $4 billion in public safety, and fought back against Trump’s attacks – all Bruce Blakeman has done is raise taxes, stick his neck out for ICE, and defend everything Trump does,” Hochul campaign spokesperson Ryan Radulovacki said in a statement. "New Yorkers don’t want that and they never have.”


The rhetoric reflects a broader bet about voters’ collective mood in an intensely disruptive political era and is a test of whether increasingly nationalized elections can still hinge on local concerns. Skyrocketing gas and grocery costs, along with the perception among younger people that their generation will not be as financially secure as their parents, has fueled insurgent primary candidates’ successful bids against their establishment foes.

New York Republicans are making this anti-incumbent case eight years after Democrats won the state Senate, giving them control of both legislative chambers and the governor’s office. GOP officials contend that Democratic control in Albany has made the Empire State less safe and more expensive. They hope the argument will appeal to swing voters, including the growing number of people who aren’t enrolled in a party, and disaffected Democrats concerned with the party’s leftward lurch — especially after Mamdani’s election.

The stakes for Republicans extend beyond Blakeman’s longshot bid for governor. The GOP is trying to retain control of the closely divided House, and New York is home to several swing districts.

Republicans are fretting about the political fate of Lawler, who occupies one of those swing seats. They are also nervously watching the outcome of a race for a deep red House seat being vacated by Rep. Elise Stefanik — an election that was never expected to be competitive.

Polls have shown their entire statewide ticket, which is led by Blakeman and includes state attorney general and comptroller candidates, down by double digits against the incumbent Democrats. One survey also showed the limits of both candidates’ affordability pitches: 35% of likely voters said neither would make life more affordable for average New Yorkers, compared with 30% who chose Hochul and 27% who chose Blakeman.

Voters across the country are enraged by the cost of living, and Hochul’s allies expect they will take it out on Trump.

“When you have a level of anger at the government or life, sometimes that spills out on people who aren’t to blame,” said Democratic former New York City Council Speaker Christine Quinn. “But in New York as it relates to Gov. Hochul, that misplaced anger is easily dispelled when voters are reminded of everything she’s gotten done.”

Some Republicans acknowledge that voters may be in a sour enough mood to reject GOP incumbents like Lawler along with Democrats. That’s added another layer of uncertainty for a party that’s desperate to regain statewide relevance.

“This is the craziest and weirdest election cycle that I've ever seen,” said Chris Tague, a state lawmaker and local GOP party chair. “It’s been very tough to gauge what’s going to happen on Tuesday night.”

Republicans are nevertheless trying to find ways of returning to power by casting this midterm fight as a change election.

GOP state attorney general candidate Saritha Komatireddy, running against two-term Democrat Letitia James, has framed her race entirely around public safety as her campaign makes an overt appeal to voters across the political spectrum concerned with crime.

“New Yorkers are tired of the way things are going,” Komatireddy said. “They understand things are going in the wrong direction. They want change.”

The state’s notoriety for high taxes has also given Republicans ammunition.

Blakeman has warned that Hochul’s second term will include the tax hikes being sought by Mamdani, her democratic socialist ally in City Hall. The mayor and his political base — which Hochul needs to run up the score in the five boroughs — are expected to pressure the governor next year to raise income tax rates on rich people and large corporations. Hochul has insisted Mamdani’s campaigning for her is not linked to a promise to back an increase.

In one of his final TV spots, Blakeman’s campaign contends Hochul is to blame for voters’ affordability woes.

“Kathy Hochul made New York unaffordable — taxing paychecks, toll and fare hikes. The nation’s highest taxes,” the ad’s narrator says.

While Hochul has not definitively ruled out a tax increase next year, the Republican nominee has proposed eliminating taxes for most New Yorkers — though he has not spelled out the details.

A party that in the 1990s simultaneously held the governor’s mansion, New York City Hall and a U.S. Senate seat has witnessed its power rapidly erode this century. A New York Republican last won a statewide race in 2002, when George Pataki secured a third term. Trump’s first midterm in 2018 saw the final toehold of GOP power erased when the party lost the state Senate.

Republicans have seen their statewide enrollment steadily dwindle as well, with more voters now registered as independents. Redoubts of Republican power are confined to some upstate counties and on bellwether Long Island, where Blakeman is a top official.

Four years ago, then-Rep. Lee Zeldin made it within 6 points of unseating Hochul — the closest a Republican has come in a gubernatorial election since the GOP’s losing streak began in 2006.

Even that race highlighted the difficulty of a GOP victory in New York. Hochul had taken office only a year earlier and was not as well known as her predecessor, Andrew Cuomo. Democratic President Joe Biden’s popularity was starting to wane. And Republicans benefitted from the deep pockets of cosmetics heir Ronald Lauder, who funded a super PAC to boost their nominee.

Zeldin still lost. And Hochul has used the race as an example of how her party can’t take anything for granted.

“We have major pockets of truly MAGA Republicans,” she told podcaster Ben Max recently. “It’s a much more purple state than people would think."

Still, Democrats have enjoyed the trappings of incumbency that make it difficult for Republicans to break through as New York’s political establishment remains behind Hochul.

The governor, a powerhouse fundraiser, has handily outspent Blakeman on advertising by more than $10 million, according to data from the ad tracking firm AdImpact. New York’s deep-pocketed private sector — including business leaders who need a powerful governor on their side in Albany — have largely stuck with Hochul. Labor organizations that once endorsed incumbent Republicans like Pataki are firmly within her camp.

“They play a role in this,” said New York Conservative Party Chair Gerard Kassar. “They definitely play a role in keeping incumbents in.”

Pulling focus: a few new video features don’t mean camera makers gave up on photography

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Video is key to some of the niches camera makers are looking to expand into, but that doesn’t mean they’re solely focused on video, now.

Richard Butler

I keep reading comments, across various platforms, that “[cameramaker x] is only interested in video, now.” What I’ve found really interesting is that you can substitute almost any company name into that sentence and find someone convinced that ‘their’ camera maker has lost interest in photography entirely. And I just don’t think it’s true.

Every camera company has become interested in video in addition to photography, but that’s not the same thing as instead of it.

Yet it’s got to the point where some people react as if any little improvement in video is a rejection of photography. Or sometimes just changes that they personally interpret as favoring video.

Every camera company has become interested in video in addition to photography, but that’s not the same thing as instead of it.

For instance, the provision of a flip-out screen is not proof that camera companies don’t care about photography. We’re told by most manufacturers that there are as many photographers that like fully articulated screens as that don’t. And this is supported, anecdotally at least, by the comments we’ve read on our articles and forums over the years.

A fully articulating rear screen isn’t evidence of a manufacturer’s focus on video, just because the style isn’t to your taste. Plenty of stills-centric and stills-only models have had them. And have done so for decades. The Panasonic G1 shown here didn’t even shoot video.DPReview

Canon has been including fully-articulating screens on its enthusiast cameras for as long as this site has existed: the first camera we ever reviewed had one. Likewise its PowerShot G series, aimed at dedicated photographers long before stills/video hybrids became feasible, tended to have them. And it’s not just Canon that adopted fully-articulating screens (though I think I remember reading that it held a key patent, at one point) on its photo-focused models. Panasonic’s first Micro Four Thirds camera, the G1, had a fully articulating screen. But no video capability at all.

A question of optics

Yes, Nikon has bought the Red cinema camera company to further enhance its video credentials and, like its rivals, has introduced some explicitly video-focused models…Mitchell Clark

It’s not necessarily a question of manufacturers’ priorities that have led to video advances seeming more prevalent in recent releases.

Photographic features are relatively mature: sensors have been photographically excellent (and hard to improve on) for some time now, single-image processing is almost universally good and ergonomics, in many cases, are the culmination of decades of iterative improvements. It’s quite difficult to look at modern cameras and say “this camera lacks this important photo feature.”

…but it’s also the company that produces the Zf, a statement of commitment to its photo heritage in a language that even those of us born in the last century can understand.Richard Butler

Autofocus is probably the biggest area in which photographic improvements are being made, as all the major companies look to refine the relatively modern on-sensor phase detection-based focusing systems and expand the capabilities of the subject recognition systems they’ve been developing.

By comparison, there’s been much work to be done on the video side of things: 10-bit video to make Log more usable, the addition of features such as waveforms and false color and, more recently, the ability to capture 32-bit float audio (though it’s notable only the explicitly video-focused Nikon ZR that has all the hardware for this included in the cost of the camera body, so far).

The existence of video-specific models, and the absence of the more photo-focused models we wanted them to be, isn’t proof that camera makers aren’t interested in photography, too.DPReview

So it shouldn’t be surprising that each new model has more additional video features than its predecessor but not many radical advances on the photography side. It’s not that camera makers have lost interest in photography or photographers, it’s just that there’s more obvious room for improvement on the video site.

Also, a great many of the more photo-focused mainstream camera models have been updated relatively recently, so it’s the more niche products and varied attempts to address the vlogging/enthusiast video/cinema markets that are currently being released. Pause your focus on video for a moment and you’ll also see the likes of the Sony RX1R III, Ricoh GR IV, Sigma BF and Fujifilm X half being launched: none of which is particularly video focused.

A question of optics

It’s also true that every new lens announcement talks about minimized focus breathing, to make it work well for video. But this is usually mentioned in addition to all the exciting features that make it the best thing ever for photographers. Personally, I don’t give this any more credence than the ubiquitous press release claims that the number and shape of the aperture blades deliver attractive bokeh (at best they can help one aspect of bokeh quality).

It’s only when a lens has a smooth-rotating aperture ring or, worse still, a powered zoom, that I worry about video considerations having any real impact on its use for photography.

It’s rarely a zero-sum game

Adding video features to cameras rarely comes at the expense of photo capabilities. Or at cost to the users. Expanding the audience to include video users (and photographers that like to shoot video) subsidizes camera development. Omitting those features risks making a product uncompetitive or making it a niche proposition that then has to be sold at a higher price.

Oh no! Fujifilm has clearly given up on enthusiast cameras and will never make another APS-C model.Richard Butler

As someone who primarily thinks of themselves as a photographer but who has enjoyed dabbling in video when I’ve had the chance, I don’t understand why there’s so much angst about the addition of video features. Indeed it’s just as often I see requests for video features such as zebras, waveforms or support for LUTs to be expanded across to the photo side of the camera: features most of us wouldn’t have encountered, were it not for the expansion of video capabilities.

To me, the main thing worth complaining about is the recent trend for making the stills/video switch part of the ‘On’ switch. And that’s mainly because I’ve just spent a week shooting with the EOS R7 during which I repeatedly tried to grab a photo and found myself thinking “why is it 16:9?” just as the red frame appeared to tell me I hadn’t taken a photo but instead had just started recording a video clip.

If you want to protest that decision, you’ll find me at the barricades.
