I was going to write a post about why, in this election, the right thing for libertarians to do is vote for Democrats for all federal offices, unless either 1) the Dem candidate is DSA, or 2) the GOP candidate is one of those very rare Republicans who oppose all or most of Trump's awful policies. But libertarian political philosopher Dan Shapiro beat me to it, in an excellent post at the Bleeding Heart Libertarian substack. I agree with the vast bulk of what he says. But I have a few additions and caveats that I will append after quoting a few key passages from Dan's post. Before getting into detail, I will note that I generally believe in voting for the lesser evil among those candidates with a realistic chance to win. Thus, unlike Dan, I don't usually vote for third party candidates, even in more normal times. If you're opposed to "lesser evil" voting on principle, we have a disagreement that goes  far beyond the present election. But I would urge you to check out my defense of that practice, where I explain why you should even vote for the evil Queen Cersei over the even more evil Sauron, if those are the only available choices.

I don't expect this post or Dan's will change the outcome of the election. They almost certainly won't. But they might still be helpful in thinking through big issues we face in the current political moment.

Here's an excerpt from Dan's post:

Most of my voting life I voted for the Libertarian Party when they advocated classical liberal/libertarian policies. Now those who care about and prioritize liberty and a free society—however they label themselves—should vote Democratic unless the candidate is a Democratic Socialist….

There's been so much criminality and misdeeds in the Trump administration that it's exhausting to cover it all, but keeping "the land of the free and the home of the brave" means remembering the essentials. I provide links when the points I make are not common knowledge and/or might be controversial.

Violating the norm of peaceful transfer of political power

January 6: Trump is the only presidential candidate in the US who refused to concede that he lost. He told a crowd to "fight like hell," and the mob then stormed the Capitol to try to overturn the results. The House impeached him for "incitement of insurrection," and 57 senators, including seven Republicans, voted to convict—short of the two-thirds required. After his campaign of lies and deception and pressuring election officials to falsify the results failed to overturn the election, he later pardoned all the perpetrators of the violence.

Violating the rule of law, shredding due process, torture

ICE: masked secret police who kidnap people and send them to hellholes in El Salvador, where they were subject to constant beatings, sexual abuse, and torture; to the Central African Republic, in the midst of a brutal civil war with severe human rights abuses; to Florida's "Alligator Alcatraz," where large numbers of detainees were held in cages the size of phone booths; and to Fort Bliss, Texas, where guards grabbed detainees' testicles while beating them. There've been zero consequences for this; indeed, Trump says they are "immune."

Not only were the deportations done without any notification to families or lawyers, but ICE has disappeared from databases those they seized so lawyers and families can't find them.

ICE and CBP have rammed people in their cars, dragged the occupants out, and rammed into houses without warrants. They have murdered, wounded, and assaulted protestors and ICE monitors—Alex Pretti and Renee Good, during the ICE occupation of Minneapolis, being the most publicized—but there have been many others.

There is now "expedited removal" for anyone undocumented, which means no hearing before a judge. (And this is even though immigration judges are—wrongly—a part of, and not independent of, the executive branch.) Indeed, Trump fired any immigration judge who disagreed with him or whom he perceived as not being deportation-friendly enough, and replaced the Board of Immigration Appeals, which has denied appeals for almost all deportation orders.

The administration removed TPS (temporary protected status) for immigrants fleeing violence and is deporting them to the very countries the State Department has declared unsafe—e.g., Haiti.

ICE apprehended immigrant parents after they dropped their kids off at school (see here, here, and here), as well as apprehending children and toddlers and using them as bait (the Liam Conejo Ramos incident)….

Violating the ban on using the military for policing

 

Trump sent Marines to LA, and National Guard troops to Chicago and DC. This violates a basic norm since the Founding and violates Posse Comitatus, a statute banning the military from policing.

Assaults on free speech and free assembly

Trump and FCC chairman Carr threatened talk shows and networks for dissenting political opinions.

As mentioned, ICE murdered, assaulted, and wounded protestors and ICE monitors.

The administration targeted people for their political views and for writing op-eds. Rochester resident David Streever sent a critical email to the director of ICE, and five months later, federal officers tracked his movements and staked out his New York City hotel as he returned from an overseas trip….

There is much more, including explanations of why the Democrats (with the notable exception of DSA types) are far less bad than this, despite their considerable flaws. I myself have written about the evils of the DSA here, explaining why they are comparable to MAGA nationalist Republicans.  In addition to the harm individual DSA members might cause if elected to office, it is important to reduce their numbers as much as possible, so as to minimize the chances that socialists could take over the Democratic Party, as MAGA nationalists have done with the GOP.

I have a few additions to Dan's arguments.

First, it is essential to consider the enormous scale of Trump's harmful and unjust immigration and trade policies. When it comes to setting issue priorities, scale matters a great deal. Even under normal administrations, immigration restrictions threaten the economic and civil liberties of American citizens (to say nothing of those of immigrants themselves) more than any other federal policy. Trump's vast expansion of exclusion and mass deportation has made things a lot worse. The liberty and well-being of millions of people are at stake.

Trump's trade policies seek to impose the highest tariff levels since those that gravely exacerbated the Great Depression, and also are a grave menace to the rule of law. Here too, we're talking about massive effects on the liberty and welfare of millions. Judicial review can and has helped curb these abuses (more on the tariff front than immigration). But there is much Congress can do to help, including curbing funding to ICE and other agencies complicit in wrongdoing. Congressional Democrats also plan to cut back executive tariff authority. That may not succeed while Trump remains in office, but can help set the stage for later progress.

Dan raises the issue of whether non-DSA Democrats might be worse than the Trump-era GOP on economic liberty issues. This becomes an easy issue if you recognize that immigration restrictions and tariffs are also violations of economic liberty. Due to their massive scale, Trump's actions in this area easily outweigh the very real economic policy flaws of non-DSA Dems. And then we have such things as his acquisition of federal government stakes in numerous private firms, something that even the most left-wing recent Democratic presidents didn't attempt. It's a dramatic example of how MAGA nationalists have dangerous economic policies similar to those of socialists.

It is also important to remember that a Democratic Senate could block Trump's horrible executive and judicial nominees. I don't want to see any more Pete Hegseths and RFK, Jrs. In Trump's first term, he had some good judicial nominees. In the second, he has mostly  bad ones (Emil Bove is a particularly egregious example). From a libertarian perspective, I'd rather have a standard liberal nominee fill the next Supreme Court vacancy (despite all the flaws such jurists have) than the type of right-winger Trump II is all too likely to nominate. Such a person would probably be selected for his willingness to turn a blind eye to executive abuses of power, trample civil liberties, and bulldoze constitutional obstacles to MAGA nationalist policies, such as federalism and separation of powers. There may well not be any Supreme Court vacancies, of course. But lower court nominations will arise, and they matter too.

Finally, I'm more hawkish than the average libertarian, and I think it's important to counter Trump's policies of undermining the Western alliance, and coddling mass-murdering dictators like Vladimir Putin and (to a lesser extent) Xi Jinping. We should care about defending liberty worldwide, not just in one country, especially since the two are interconnected. The Democrats (except for a few far-leftists) oppose this awfulness and could use control of Congress to help curb it.

In these and other areas, a Democratic-controlled Congress could not fully stop Trump's abuses. The executive has accumulated too much power for that, often in constitutionally dubious ways. But congressional control of nominations and purse strings can do much to constrain the executive at the margin. And electoral repudiation of Trump's abuses can reduce the risk that future presidents will imitate them.

I do have a few caveats to attach to Dan's otherwise compelling points:

First, Dan doesn't differentiate between elections for federal offices and those for state and local ones. I do. Beating the GOP in federal races is vital for countering Trump's awful agenda. At the state and local level, other issues are dominant, and Republicans are often better than Dems on the crucial issues of housing, state-level taxation, and state and local economic regulation. Which party is better on these and other state and local issues will vary from state to state. For example, red state Republicans are much better on the crucial issue of housing deregulation than blue state ones (who often back exclusionary zoning against Democratic reform efforts). Thus, it might make sense to vote for Republican candidates in some state and local races, but not others, depending on the issues involved.

Second, when it comes to the DSA problem, there can be difficult questions about what to do with Dem candidates who are not actual DSA members, but seem closely adjacent to them. Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed is a notable example. He says he's not a socialist, but also is closely aligned with socialists like Bernie Sanders, and "influencer" Hasan Piker, the guy who - among other things - praises communist mass murderers like Mao Zedong.

In this post, I'm not going to try to adjudicate each individual candidate and race. I will merely say that if you're trying to figure out whether a non-DSA member is actually a socialist, two noteworthy indicators are 1) whether he or she supports government control of all or most of the economy and 2) whether they praise socialist despots abroad, such as Mao, Fidel Castro, and others. If the answers to one or both questions are "yes," that's a strong indication they might be socialists, and therefore not worth voting for, except perhaps against the very worst Republicans.

There is a similar calculation in identifying the few Republicans who have opposed Trump's worst policies. Here, key indicators are whether they have opposed his tariffs, his mass deportations, his kowtowing to Vladimir Putin, and his attacks on civil liberties. Sadly, Republican candidates with consistent strong records on all or most of these issues, are few and far between, though Republican criticism of the tariffs and policies towards Russia have picked up in recent weeks.

Finally, as Dan would agree, voting for Democratic candidates does not imply supporting everything the Democratic Party does, or abstaining from criticizing Democrats on the many occasions when they do bad things.

Elsewhere, I have argued that libertarians, in the current political moment, should seek an alliance with "abundance liberals." But alliance is not the same thing as total agreement. And the Democratic Party obviously includes various factions who are much worse than the abundance types. We should keep that in mind, even as we recognize that right now the Republicans are much worse.

A Dutch still life, “Beast Jesus,” several esoteric Goya characters, and not one but two Marina Abramović/Ulay references (and not even the most obvious ones!) walk into a bar ... That’s a Hyperallergic Halloween, baby!

On Wednesday evening, October 28, almost 200 of our members, readers, artists, supporters, and their enthusiastic friends rolled into Oberon in Brooklyn for our annual art costume party. Winners in several categories ranging from “Best in Show” to “Most Likely to Get Censored in Florida” brought out the art-history nerd within them, materializing into both immediately recognizable icons — Roy Lichtenstein's “Drowning Girls” and Johannes Vermeer’s “Girl with a Pearl Earring” — and admirably obscure references (oh, she was an Eva Hesse rope sculpture?!).

We’re so grateful to our absurdly smart, creative, and crafty community for their commitment to the bit. And a big shout-out to Zac Thompson for their incredible photographs, which will help us relive the night for many moons, or at least as we wait impatiently for next year ... 🌝

Best in Show winners Emilio Rojas and Chico Raro as Pedro Lemebel and Francisco Casa Silva of Las Yeguas del Apocalipsis’s “Las Dos Fridas” (1989). No bribing was necessary to convince the panel.

Most Esoteric

Zoë Lescaze and Michael Rossi made every part of their costumes from scratch — yes, including the chair — to dress as lesser-known characters from Francisco Goya's Los caprichos (1797–98) series. The couple was among the winners of the "Most Esoteric" award, naturally.

Most Likely to Get Censored in Florida

Richard Prince's "Wayward Nurse" (2005), part of the artist's series critiquing the sexualization of nurses on pulp-fiction book covers, would not fly in the Sunshine State in 2026!

Most Likely to Get Acquired by a Museum Unironically

Curators and staff from the Heckscher Museum of Art in Huntington, New York, recreated a painting from the institution's own collection: George Grosz's antifascist "Eclipse of the Sun" (1926).

For a genuine fright this Halloween, you can turn your gaze to the southern border, where ravenous, flesh-eating maggots are festering and have taken to eating people's pets.

This week, the country's New World Screwworm (NWS) infestation spread to a third state, Arizona, which joins New Mexico and Texas in being plagued by the horrifying parasites. To date, 74 animal cases have been identified in the US since the flies re-invaded the country in June.

The flies lay hundreds of eggs in wounds and moist orifices of animals, and the resulting voracious maggots feast on their victims. The maggots resemble screws and tortuously bore and twist into living flesh, causing severe, sometimes fatal wounds, before dropping to the ground to develop into adults.

Alberta Ski Resort To Open Days Earlier Than Planned as 2026-27 Season Kicks Off

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Out West, another resort contender is joining the early-season shuffle: Lake Louise, Alberta, will kick off winter earlier than planned on Sunday, November 1, per a recent announcement.

The resort originally planned to open on November 6.

This marks Lake Louise’s second-earliest opening in history and, according to the resort, comes courtesy of cold October weather and its hardworking team. 

At Lake Louise—as at most other ski resorts that open this time of year—the digs will be modest to start, with access to the Easy Street trail via the Juniper Express. But Lake Louise will offer two terrain parks with jumps and rails. The resort’s set to be open from 9:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. Lake Louise’s webcams show solid fall snow coverage.

At the time of writing this, Lake Louise’s neighbor, Banff Sunshine Village, hasn’t shared any news about opening. But both resorts tend to open around the same time. You can check Sunshine Village’s Instagram page for the latest news. 

Lake Louise’s planned opening comes after two regular frontrunners, Arapahoe Basin and Keystone Resort, Colorado, kicked off the North American season this week. Another ski area in the state, Wolf Creek, plans to open on Halloween.

Beyond that, many resorts aren’t slated to open until late November. Even if you’re too far away to stop by Alberta or Colorado, though, it’s worth seeing these openings as a sign we’re all headed in the right direction. Before too long, it’ll be time to click in.

#763: Acorns CEO: The Scariest Part Of Investing Is the First Step, with Noah Kerner

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Noah Kerner was a bank teller at 17 and interned on a municipal bond trading floor in college. By every measure, he understood money. But his fear of investing kept him from buying so much as a certificate of deposit for most of his twenties, and it cost him a decade of compounding.

Today he’s the CEO of Acorns, which has helped everyday people save and invest more than $35 billion, often starting with spare change. He knows firsthand that the fear of investing rarely comes from a lack of knowledge. It comes from stress, shame, and the sense that money is a club you were never invited into.

Noah joins the show to talk about why so many people avoid looking at their money, and why most parents would rather talk to their kids about sex and drugs than about finances. He explains how Acorns tackles the fear of investing by making the first step so small that it’s hard to say no.

He also draws a sharp line between patience and prediction. He explains why prediction markets, sports betting, and buy now, pay later keep pulling people toward quick hits, and why the best thing to do with your money is often to be boring.

Then the conversation turns personal: the vestibular migraines that forced him to step away from work, how he lives with the risk that most companies fail, and the trait he values most in the people he hires.

Key Takeaways

The biggest ideas from the conversation +
  • Knowing about money doesn’t make investing easier: Noah worked as a bank teller and interned on a bond trading floor, yet his fear of investing still kept him on the sidelines for a decade. Avoidance is usually emotional, driven by stress and shame, not a knowledge gap.
  • Make the first step too small to refuse: Rounding a $12.87 purchase up to $13 and investing the 13 cents is hard to object to. Starting tiny gets people past the fear of investing, and watching the balance grow builds the confidence to add recurring contributions.
  • Automation beats willpower for long-term investing: Recurring investments, such as $5 a day or $25 a week, are the most popular feature at Acorns. Automating the habit means it runs in the background for decades, without relying on daily discipline or decision-making.
  • Choose patience over prediction with your money: Prediction is thinking you can guess your way to rich quickly. Patience means a diversified portfolio and a 40-year horizon. Noah says it’s fine to play with about 10% of your money, but problems start when people confuse betting with a money strategy.
  • The app you use shapes how you behave: The same $10,000 can be handled very differently depending on the platform. Products built around trading reward impulsivity, while Acorns saw low withdrawals and steady investing even during market shocks like Liberation Day in April 2025.
  • A company’s business model reveals its real incentives: Acorns charges a subscription instead of profiting from trading, borrowing, or overspending. Noah argues that how a company makes money determines what it rewards, so it’s worth asking how any financial app earns its revenue before trusting it with yours.

Madrona Prize goes to UW research on agents that shrink AI models to run on small chips

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Madrona Prize honorees with representatives from Madrona at the Allen School Research Showcase. From left: Karan Mehandru, Chris Picardo and Joe Horseman of Madrona; Zhihan Zhang, Madrona Prize winner; Tanush Yadav, first runner-up; Jacob Morrison of Madrona and the Allen School; and Phoebe Riser and Baxter Black of Madrona. (Photo by Kristine White)

A University of Washington project that uses AI agents to squeeze AI models onto tiny devices — such as a solar-powered camera that spots elk in the wild — won the Madrona Prize at the Allen School’s annual Research Showcase on Thursday.

Seattle venture capital firm Madrona has awarded the prize since 2006 to Allen School student projects that combine strong research with commercial potential. The winners were chosen from among the student posters presented at the evening event on the UW campus.

Zhihan Zhang explains his Madrona Prize-winning research on using AI agents to fit AI models onto small chips. (Photo by Kristine White)

Zhihan Zhang, a PhD student in the Allen School of Computer Science & Engineering, presented the winning project. It tests whether AI agents can do a job that experts now do by hand: shrinking AI models and tuning device software to run on small, low-power chips with tight limits on memory, power and heat.

In the team’s tests, leading AI models failed at the task without feedback from real hardware. The team built a system that let the agent load its work onto real hardware, measure the results and try again. With that feedback, the agent beat human experts within seven rounds.

The researchers used the approach on two real-world projects: an elk-detection camera trap, which needed its vision model shrunk by 250 times, and a wearable device that records children’s speech for child development research.

The team released its benchmark, called Embedded Arena, as open-source code. Allen School faculty members Shwetak Patel, Vikram Iyer, Natasha Jaques and Kurtis Heimerl also worked on the project.

Other collaborators: Alexander Metzger, Jiuyang Lyu, Chun-Cheng Chang, Jiayi Shao, Yujia Liu, Emmanuel Azuh Mensah, Edward Wang and Gregory D. Abowd.

Keisuke Kamahori with his VibeSys poster, winner of the People’s Choice award. (Photo by Kristine White)

The People’s Choice award went to Keisuke Kamahori for VibeSys, a project that uses AI agents to write custom software for a specific chip and task, then keep improving it, instead of relying on general-purpose tools built for common setups.

Its first application, VibeServe, writes the behind-the-scenes software that takes requests from users and runs them through an AI model. On Amazon’s Trainium2 chips, the software it built handled up to 2.7 times as much work as an existing system. Shihang (Vic) Li and Allen School faculty members Simon Peter and Baris Kasikci also worked on the project.

Tanush Yadav, first runner-up for the Madrona Prize, with his VideoNet poster. (Photo by Kristine White)

The first runner-up for the Madrona Prize, Tanush Yadav, presented VideoNet, a test of how well AI models recognize specific actions in video, such as a particular bouldering move or a type of surgical stitch. It covers more than 1,000 actions across 38 fields.

Leading AI models struggle with it compared to ordinary people. Shown three examples, people improved their accuracy by about 14 percentage points, while AI models improved by about 3 percentage points.

Allen School faculty members Ali Farhadi, Hanna Hajishirzi and Ranjay Krishna, and former Allen School faculty member Yejin Choi, now at Stanford University, also worked on the project.

Other collaborators: Mohammadreza Salehi, Jae Sung Park, Vivek Ramanujan and Rohun Tripathi.

Henry Wong, second runner-up for the Madrona Prize, with his poster on securing DNA synthesizers. (Photo by Kristine White)

The second runner-up, Henry Wong, presented work on securing benchtop DNA synthesizers, machines that let users print synthetic DNA.

His project focuses on protecting the screening software meant to stop someone from printing a dangerous pathogen, and concludes that even perfectly secure hardware wouldn’t fully solve the problem.

Allen School faculty member Jeff Nivala and former Allen School faculty member Tadayoshi Kohno, now at Georgetown University, also worked on the project.

All of the presenters are PhD students in the Allen School.

Most backpackers memorialize their trips through photographs. But when LA-based hiker Tom Kelly hit the PCT in 2023, he wanted to document a different, more sensory experience of the trail. Kelly, a nature sound field recordist, composer, and musician, toted 40 ounces of audio recording equipment during his 2,650-mile thru-hike, pausing along the trail to capture the acoustics of the journey. Kelly set up his microphones along burbling creeks and amid chirping insects; he recorded the rustling of grass in the wind, rain on a tent fly, or the chatter of passing hikers. Upon completing his thru-hike, Kelly spent two years compiling his recordings into an interactive and freely accessible sound map.

Reporter Amelia Arvesen spoke to Kelly about his project, the challenges of capturing sound in the backcountry, and what he hopes listeners can gain from the map.

Transcript

Tom Kelly: It was beautifully calm, really nice day. And we all went to bed, and I woke up to the sound of a great horned owl calling across the lake. And so, the last thing I want to do is wake up and do something, you know? It’s hard enough to wake up to go to the bathroom, much less take your recording equipment, go hike, set up, and you know, do the whole recording process.

I’m a nature sound field recordist, I’m a composer, and music performer based in Los Angeles, California.
