The Only Time I Felt Allowed To Be Overweight Was When I Was Pregnant

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Westend61/Westend61/Getty Images

I'd never gone 24 hours without thinking about my weight. Never a day where I didn’t feel guilty for overeating and what the scale would look like the next morning. Forever overweight and forever ashamed.

Then I got pregnant. I arrived at my new high-risk OB-GYN, the one I had to search for because my BMI alone made me automatically high risk. No preexisting medical conditions, no concerning family history… just FAT. And before I could even wrap my head around the fact that I was growing an actual human being, my new doctor told me that because of my BMI, I should only gain 11 pounds over the next nine months.

Eleven. Pounds.

I was supposed to grow a whole ass baby, and only gain 11 pounds? Historically, I could gain 11 pounds overnight if I put my mind to it! I thought pregnancy was the one time I could finally embrace getting bigger. I actually envisioned stepping on the scale at the doctor's office without fear, maybe even getting a little golf clap from my doctor for gaining a few pounds. Like the prize pig at the county fair.

I understood there were medical reasons behind the recommendation, but I couldn't help feeling defeated. Here I was, finally experiencing something I had dreamed about, and somehow, the conversation had already come back to my weight. Even when my body was literally supposed to get bigger, there was a limit.

I felt ashamed. And that shame quickly turned into anxiety. I'd spent my entire life fearing weight gain for myself, but now I was terrified it could hurt someone else: my baby. Instead of embracing my growing body, I spent my first trimester afraid of what would happen if it grew too much.

By week 12, stress and morning sickness had caused me to lose 10 pounds. I stepped on the scale at my next ultrasound, secretly excited and doing some quick math. Did that mean I could gain 21 pounds now? Did the math carry over? I was already calculating how to earn more weight gain. But when I spoke with my doctor, he didn't mention the weight loss at all. He wasn't proud, he wasn't mad. Not a peep.

And somehow, this felt like a green light to me. Like actually, maybe he isn’t THAT focused on the scale and more focused on the baby (duh). After that appointment, I felt my jaw unclench, and something in my brain shifted. I had made it through the first trimester, the baby was healthy and I had lost weight. For thatI was not punished nor rewarded. It was finally, just a number.

So I announced my pregnancy to family and friends and a sudden weight was lifted (and soon gained). Eventually, even the morning sickness let up and I reverted back to the old me. And just like that, I was back in my safe space, scrolling DoorDash.

I started eating whatever I wanted and felt no shame. I ate mozzarella sticks for a snack at 10am. Bagels before bed. I would end every meal with a “treat” of Phish Food ice cream or a couple peanut butter cups for good measure. It’s called palate cleansing.

It felt like FREEDOM. I didn't have to suck in my stomach or hide my rolls. No worrying about posing for a picture to make myself look smaller or hide my sins. I didn't have to wonder if someone was looking at what was on my plate, because honestly I DARE YOU. I didn't have to promise myself I'd “be good tomorrow.” My belly was supposed to get bigger. My clothes were supposed to stop fitting. I was supposed to gain weight. It was like a fun topic of conversation with my girlfriends and people who were already moms… “eating myself through house and home, you know how it goes!”

Well, spoiler alert, I saw their 11 pounds and ended up raising them 50. And honestly? I wasn't that scared. I'd been that weight before. I knew what it was like to live in that body. The number I was supposed to fear reaching was a number I'd already seen on my own scale. But more than that, for the first time in my life, my growing body came with an explanation.

The craziest part? My doctor didn’t bring up my weight again until after I had the baby. Sure, it still made the occasional cameo, like when I needed an anesthesia consultation before birth because of my BMI. But it made my weight felt more like a supporting character instead of the entire plot. It felt like a huge relief.

Pregnancy didn't make me comfortable gaining weight. It made my weight explainable. Because the reality is, I didn't suddenly stop being plus-size. For once in my life I felt normal…like maybe this is how regular people go about their day. Like I was in a new tier of women, one where people were going to celebrate my growing belly. My dad would make us dinner on Sundays and say “Mary gets extra, she’s eating for two!” This is what dreams were made of.

Before I knew it, nine months had flown by and I'd given birth to my baby boy. Less than 12 hours postpartum, a visiting doctor looked at my legs and asked, “Do your legs typically look like this, or are you really swollen?”

And just like that, we were back, baby!

Nope, that's just what my legs look like. I'd barely had a minute to process giving birth before I was back to feeling self-conscious about my body. Nine months of freedom, gone in one sentence.

And that was just the beginning. Soon, doctor's appointments included questions like, “So, what's your plan for weight loss?” and suggestions about GLP-1 medications. My health was otherwise good, but once again, my weight was the conversation.

I remember thinking, how am I supposed to diet on no sleep? I'm just trying to keep a newborn alive, recover from childbirth, and make it through the day. Could my body just exist for a minute without needing to be fixed?

But still, I felt the pressure to lose weight, like my body was a project that was never finished. I even caught myself feeling relieved on days when I was so busy caring for my baby that I barely had time to eat.

I've spent my entire life trying to make my body smaller, and for most of my pregnancy, I finally got to celebrate getting bigger. I wish I could say that experience changed the way I see myself forever, but the truth is, I still think about my weight every day. I still feel the guilt, still do the mental math, still wonder what the scale will say tomorrow. My brain is programmed this way. But pregnancy gave me a taste of freedom I never knew was missing. For once, I got to exist in my body without feeling like I owed anyone an explanation.

Sometimes I wonder if I will ever feel that free again. After giving birth to two beautiful boys, I don’t long for another pregnancy, though I have to admit, nine more months of not obsessing over the scale sounds pretty damn appealing.

I don’t want another excuse to love my body, I just want to stop feeling like I need one.

Assassin’s Creed Franchise Update Reveals Codename Hexe News Coming Next Year, New Game Plus for Valhalla, And Confirms No New Remakes

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ubisoft has released a fall update on the Assassin’s Creed franchise, providing news big and small on its flagship series. The news includes updates for existing games and smaller but significant insights on the various upcoming Assassin’s Creed games previously announced.

Assassin's Creed Valhalla

In terms of current games, Assassin’s Creed Valhalla is getting a long-requested New Game Plus thanks to a new update coming on November 5. This means you can start a fresh game while bringing over your progression items/level from your previous playthrough. This update will also introduce the following features:

  • An optional progression mode where Gear, Runes, and Masteries all contribute to Eivor's overall Power
  • New runes, achievements, a stash system, and new rewards, including a Master Assassin outfit inspired by Naoe's look in Assassin's Creed Shadows
  • Mithril, a new resource to upgrade Divine Gear and strengthen runes
  • Divine Gear quality for all items

This news comes a few days after New Game Plus arrived in Assassin's Creed Black Flag Resynced. 

Assassin's Creed Codename Invictis

Codname Invictis, a PvP multiplayer game, remains in development as Ubisoft shares it has incorporated feedback from players who, according to Ubisoft, “saw great potential in the foundations but wanted more of the pillars that define Assassin's Creed.” To this end, Ubisoft has spent months reworking Invictis to reflect this vague feedback and say the game is “now evolving in an exciting direction that better reflects those pillars while staying true to its multiplayer ambitions.”

Assassin's Creed Codename Hexe

As for the next single-player Assassin’s Creed, Codename Hexe, Ubisoft says it will share the next major update on this game in 2027. First announced in 2022, Codename Hexe is set during the 17th-century reign of the Holy Roman Empire, and the era's witch hunts will play a significant role.

Future Assassin's Creed Remakes 

With the release of Assassin’s Creed Black Flag Resynced earlier this year, Ubisoft has given fans what they'll likely see as a disappointing update on potential future remakes: “One thing we want to be transparent about today is that we currently have no other Assassin's Creed remake actively in development. Our teams are already focused on the next chapter of Assassin's Creed titles, including projects you haven't heard about yet, featuring new settings and stories.”

However, Ubisoft adds that this door isn’t closed for good. “Like many of you, we love the idea of revisiting our classic titles, and we'll keep exploring opportunities to bring older titles back,” says Ubisoft.

Assassin's Creed Netflix Series

Ubisoft’s next update concerns the live-action Assassin's Creed series in production for Netflix. They say the show is “nearing completion” in Rome and that award-winning composer Hannah Peel (Insomnia, Rogue Agent, Game of Thrones: The Last Watch) is composing the score. 

Lastly, Ubisoft teases big celebrations are planned for 2027, which marks the 20th anniversary of the Assassin's Creed franchise. It's keeping details to itself for now, but states it's "preparing a year of reveals, updates, rewards, and community moments. "

The two-paragraph Sept. 18 news release from Montana Fish, Wildlife & Parks raised more questions than it may have intended.

The department “is seeking help from the public to locate a hybridized female rocky mountain bighorn sheep that went missing from a private alternative livestock facility near Vaughn in early September,” the release from FWP’s Region 4 office in Great Falls stated. “The bighorn ewe is described as weighing about 120 pounds and is primarily white in color with a red ear tag.”

The news release stressed wildlife officials’ concern that the ewe could come into contact with wild bighorn sheep in the vicinity. Domestic sheep are often vectors for pathogens that can fatally infect wild sheep.

What the news release didn’t disclose is that the “alternative livestock facility” in question is owned by Arthur “Jack” Schubarth, who was convicted in 2024 of violating the federal Lacey Act in his pursuit to create giant hybrid bighorn rams. Schubarth pleaded guilty to helping smuggle genetic material from Marco Polo argali sheep from Kyrgyzstan into the United States in order to clone the ram and create a “super sheep” with huge spiraling horns and resistance to the pathogens that infect North American wild sheep. His quest was enabled by Montana hunting outfitter John Lewton, who lopped off the testicles of his clients’ trophy wild bighorns, kept the gonads on ice, and later sold them to Schubarth.

The idea, Schubarth later told court officials, was to use the genetic material to incubate hybrid sheep with horns larger than any North American rams.

Lewton was sentenced last week in federal court in Billings and received a $20,000 fine and a year of probation for his part in the genetic alchemy. As a condition of his federal-court sentence, Lewton is prohibited from outfitting or guiding hunters for a full year.

‘Trailer Park Chemistry’

The notion that a “Frankenram” could be produced with a combination of cloned embryos and artificial insemination isn’t an abstraction. In 2017, Schubarth actually produced what he called the Montana Mountain King, a pure cloned Marco Polo ram birthed to a surrogate bighorn ewe. Schubarth subsequently bred offspring of the Montana Mountain King to captive bighorn ewes on his farm west of Great Falls. It’s one of these ewes that has now gone missing, according to Fish, Wildlife & Parks.

Schubarth’s genetic hermetics has both baffled and impressed wildlife investigators, who told Outdoor Life that the sort of reproductive science that Schubarth practiced is normally limited to hospitals, universities, and other science centers with state-of-the-art facilities.

“The best way to describe his operation is trailer-park chemistry,” said a source familiar with the case who spoke on background. “He’s pulled off a pretty high-level genetics program with hardware-store gear and limited resources.”

Schubarth received probation for his guilty plea to the Lacey Act violations. In his petition to the federal court for leniency, Schubarth’s attorney, Jason Holden, noted his client’s contribution to the science of wildlife propagation.

“Jack is an 81-year-old non-violent man,” Holden wrote in 2024. “His crime, the cloning of a Marco Polo Sheep from Kyrgyzstan, has ruined his life, reputation and family. Jack did something no one else could, or has ever done. On a ranch, in a barn in Montana, he created Montana Mountain King. MMK is an extraordinary animal, born of science, and from a man who, if he could rewrite history, would have left the challenge of cloning a Marco Polo to only the imagination of Michael Crichton.”

Was the Sheep Sold?

The department’s all-points-bulletin asking the public to report a lone and roaming ewe may not produce any sightings. That’s because there’s a possibility that the ewe isn’t actually on the lam. She may have been sold to an out-of-state game farm, said sources who spoke to Outdoor Life on the condition that they wouldn’t be identified based on the speculative nature of their hunches.

Schubarth, who is on probation from the 2024 federal wildlife-trafficking charges, has been charged by FWP with failure to report the escape of an animal permitted in his commercial wildlife operation, according to charging documents at the Cascade County District Court in Montana.

The “game farm” permit requires that if anything escapes the permitted facility, owners must report within 48 hours to both FWP and the Montana Department of Livestock.

“According to our records, the ewe in question reportedly escaped on Sept. 2, and our game wardens heard about it the next week,” says Dave Hagengruber, FWP’s public information officer in the Great Falls regional office. “Wardens went out to talk with Schubarth in mid-September, and he basically said, ‘Oh yeah, I meant to tell you.’”

Schubarth told wardens the ewe likely escaped either over a fence damaged by a wind storm or through a gate accidentally left open at the end of his property, beyond lines of kennels of dogs that produce puppies sold in Schubarth’s pet store in Great Falls.

Assuming the ewe did leave Schubarth’s facility, it could pose a risk to the region’s wild sheep.

“Cascade is 30 miles from his farm, and there’s a sizeable band of [wild] bighorns there,” says Hagengruber. “The Birdtail Hills are 20 to 25 miles to the west, and there are bighorns in those hills. The Rocky Mountain Front, home to Montana’s most treasured bighorn sheep herd, is 60 or 70 miles west. Fortunately, this is not a ram, so it’s less likely to roam widely, but the threat of disease transmission is a significant concern. That’s why we want to hear of any sightings. It’s Jack’s property and his responsibility to locate and return to his facility, but we can’t have this hybrid sheep potentially contacting wild bands.”

Fate of a Freak

Tilek, the pure cloned Marco Polo ram taken in by the
Rosamond Gifford Zoo. Photo credit: Rosamond Gifford Zoo

Schubarth insists he did not sell the ewe, and suspects the fugitive sheep was killed by predators within a few miles of his facility in the Sun River Valley.

“I haven’t heard anything about her, and to my knowledge Fish and Game hasn’t heard anything for five weeks now,” Schubarth said in an interview. “With all the people out looking, we haven’t seen it and no one’s called me. I’m assuming, with her lack of experience with predators, that a coyote got her.”

Schubarth confirmed the ewe is a hybrid.

“She’s mainly bighorn and probably three or four domestic crosses from the original cross that was 50 percent bighorn,” Schubarth said. “I added some more bighorn from that original [parentage]. So they’re probably 62.5 percent bighorn. They don’t have any experience of being wild. I’m pretty sure she would have watched coyotes walk right up to her” without trying to evade them.

Schubarth further confirmed that he still has “around 45 head of sheep, is all” remaining on his 200-acre game farm. “All my sheep are sold right now and just waiting to leave. I’ve kept them back because I just want the pleasure of birthing out the lambs. It’s something I like to do, and at 83 years old, I like to see new life.”

As for the descendants of the Montana Mountain King, Schubarth said that the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service required him to destroy progeny of the cloned ram back in 2024. The federal court also required Schubarth to dispose of all hybrid bighorn sheep by Dec. 31, 2025, and to stop “from engaging in breeding activity of big game stock while on supervision,” according to sentencing documents.

“We had 190 head of the hybrid Marco/bighorn here at the ranch and none of them got pneumonia in the three years I held them while waiting for a forever home for them. All were slaughtered,” he says. “I did sell some, 15 in all. There are some down in Texas, and every so often I see on Facebook somebody advertising a Marco Polo, but there’s no way they are more than about a quarter Marco” based on genetic dilution, he said.

As for the Montana Mountain King himself, Schubarth says the ram ended up at a zoological park in upstate New York. Indeed, last summer the Rosamond Gifford Zoo in Syracuse, New York, announced the acquisition of “Tilek,” the cloned argali at the center of Schubarth’s case. The ram had been held at an Oregon wildlife rehabilitation center for over three years before being moved to the Syracuse zoo.

Measured Remorse

Schubarth says “Tilek” would be about nine years old, but observed that he has not yet expressed the most distinctive trait of the Marco Polo subspecies of argali: its spiraling horns.

“He’s basically still 54 to 57 inches,” says Schubarth. “He ought to have 75 inches of horn on each side. But this year they put him in a larger grass pen so maybe he’ll grow more. For safety’s sake, they should have castrated him so no more genetics could get out there. Maybe they did, for all I know. I’ll never see him again. I don’t ever leave my ranch.”

Schubarth said that statements from prosecutors that he was motivated to create a hybrid ram with giant horns in order to upsell to commercial shooting operations are wrong. He was more interested in cross-breeding to create a bighorn race that might be resistant to what’s often called “M. ovi,” a respiratory disease that often results in catastrophic pneumonia-caused die-offs in wild sheep.

“My thinking was that here’s this wild sheep that has evolved with domestic sheep for thousands of years,” says Schubarth of the Marco Polo, native to the high alpine plateaus of central Asia. “The feds and Fish and Game made it out that I was selling to the hunt community and only motivated to create bigger horns. In actuality, I was after the immune system, and the reality is that even after several generations, these Marco hybrids still seem to be immune to pneumonia. I think that’s the real story.”

If he regrets anything about the misadventures of the Montana Mountain King, it’s that he trusted the lab that created the clone from genetic tissue smuggled from Kyrgyzstan.

“I’ve been happy all my life except for this last foolishness about the Marco Polo,” Schubarth says. “I wouldn’t have done it had the cloning company not told me it was legal. They lied after they took $32,000 from me. That was the cost of stockpiling the tissue and making the embryos for me.”

The rest of the genetic work was a cinch, he says. The surrogate bighorn ewes, most of which Schubarth bought from zoos in Canada and Colorado, were selected for their ability to carry the hybrid lamb.

“Laparoscopic work is easy,” he says, and artificial insemination is an established livestock practice. The trick is creating a genetically manipulated zygote.

“You take the egg and suck out the product and you put the material from the Marco Polo into the egg and implant it in a ewe. A little electricity, and poof, the thing starts to grow.”

Disease Alert

Schubarth says he will be entirely out of the game-breeding business by 2027.

“Fish and Game has accomplished what they always wanted: to drive me out of business,” he says. 

Meanwhile, he says the captive-shooting industry is thriving. He cites high-fence operations in Kansas and Texas that would pay top dollar for his hybrid bighorns while also insisting that he sold to fellow breeders, not trophy hunters.

“The feds made it out that I was the center of this industry based on shooting animals in pens,” he says, before acknowledging that most of his animals probably met that fate. “Without the interest of the hunting community, these animals would be worthless and only sold for meat. So yes, the industry revolves around the harvesting of these animals by collectors.”

Fish, Wildlife & Parks is less interested in the commercial than in the pathological outcome of the case. The state has struggled to establish bighorn sheep populations in prime habitat in the Missouri River Breaks and in the mountains of southwest Montana. Bands that thrive for years are often wiped out in a single season by pneumonia epizootics that often originate in domestic sheep.

State game wardens are authorized to shoot on sight wild bighorns that contact domestic sheep. The fear is that a traveling ram could become infected by a domestic sheep, and then take that pathogen back to the wild herd.

“We have spent many years and sportsmen’s dollars to restore and transplant wild sheep into available habitat in Montana,” says FWP’s Hagengruber. “We’ve had recent restorations in the Belts and the Breaks, and disease is always a big concern, and so is the ability of this hybrid ewe to breed with either domestic or wild sheep. So we take the possibility that there may be a hybrid domestic/wild sheep running around central Montana very seriously.”

Anyone who sees a lone sheep with a red ear tag in central Montana is urged to call FWP’s Region 4 office at 406-454-5840.

Power and Performance

What stood out to me during testing was how much the Yeti 1500 can handle at once. I can keep multiple devices plugged in without having to constantly unplug one thing to charge another, and the display makes it easy to see how much power I’m using. With 2,000 watts of continuous output, it has enough capacity for more than just phones and smaller electronics. We used it to power an electric cooler on an 8-hour road trip to the beach this summer, and it worked like a dream. I’d honestly feel comfortable keeping it on hand for backup power at home, too.

Battery and Charging

Battery life is one of the most important features of a power station, especially if you’re taking it camping or relying on it during an outage. The Goal Zero is worth every penny. Full stop. At $1,500, it’s definitely an investment, but if you spend a lot of time in the outdoors or want something reliable to have on hand when the power goes out, I think the performance and peace of mind justify the price.

The best way to survive a winter storm is to not get caught in one. True as that may be, however, it’s not a very useful tip when the snow has already started falling. As weather grows more unpredictable, getting stuck on the trail, even when you’ve dutifully checked the forecasts, could happen to any of us. The key to surviving is always being prepared for the worst by packing the 10 essentials and reading articles like this, even if you’re just planning to go on a day hike.

“If you ask me what’s the highest risk, it’s those short hikes because they’re the ones most people aren’t prepared for the longer term,” says Dan Wowak of Coalcracker Bushcraft, a wilderness school and YouTube channel based in Pennsylvania. “A two-mile hike can turn into a devastating emergency situation. You roll an ankle, a storm comes in, it gets dark, you have no equipment, and you don’t know what to do.”

We talked to Wowak and two other experienced hikers about what to do when bad weather rolls in with no warning. The first step: They all suggest packing the right things for winter, including an emergency shelter, communication device, extra layers, navigation, and a fire starter.

1. Stop and Evaluate

You might think that if you’re only a mile from the trailhead, you’re home free. But as snow starts to cover the trail and any tracks you may have left, you might as well be days away. “When you’re out and the weather is turning, you might be inclined to go faster,” says Danny Giovale, the founder of Kahtoola. “I would strongly suggest stopping for a sec.”

Knowing when to stop and wait out the storm will save you from precarious situations. Giovale and Wowak suggest considering how far you have left to go and the time of day. If the storm is moving in quickly and it’s getting dark, it’s best to stop and prioritize survival. Nighttime, Wowak says, is one of the worst times to travel because both visibility and temperatures are at their lowest. So if dusk approaches and you’re not prepared with the equipment and knowledge to travel in the dark, settle in to spend the night out there.

2. Set Up—or Find—Shelter

If you packed an emergency bivy or a tent, it’s crucial to set that up immediately to protect yourself from the elements, like snow, rain, and wind. Blocking the weather is going to prolong your survival timeline. “If you’re not dry, you’re in trouble really fast,” says Jon King, who hikes daily and sends the trail report for 10,000-footer San Jacinto Peak in California.

If you don’t have shelter, Giovale suggests looking for a cave or building your own in the snow. This, of course, requires prior knowledge, but the idea is to create a big pile of snow. Let it set up into a dome and then dig out a space for your body. Don’t forget to poke an air vent.

Then there are trails with man-made shelters, like on San Jacinto Peak, says King. Part of your preparation is knowing where these huts and structures are located in case you ever need them.

3. Keep Warm

As your hands grow cold, they become less functional. As your body loses heat, you become at risk for developing hypothermia and making rash decisions. In addition to a shelter, Giovale always brings a lightweight shell jacket with a hood. It’s “one of the ounce-per-ounce most impactful pieces of gear,” he says. Staying as dry as possible with extra layers will keep you warm. Keep exercising and moving to stimulate blood flow to your fingers and toes.

Wowak also recommends building a fire. “Getting a campfire started is a very relaxing and primal thing that makes people feel safe in just about any situation,” he says. Some caveats apply. It’s harder to do in a torrential downpour than in a snowstorm. It’s also not always advised in fire-prone areas or during a fire ban unless it’s a life-or-death situation.

Should you spend the night outside, make sure to insulate your body from the ground so you don’t lose body heat, he adds. A foam mat will work, but in lieu of that, so will pine boughs. In a pinch, you can sit on your backpack.

4. Stay Hydrated 

Ration your water reserves to stay hydrated, or use the campfire to melt snow and warm up a drink. Drinking cold water can drain your energy, Wowak says, so warm water is better.

Unless you brought snacks, you might start to feel hunger pangs. Hydration can help there, too. “,Even if we don’t have a lot of food, if we were only planning on a day hike and a storm comes in, and we’re stuck there, being hydrated will trick your body and you won’t feel as hungry because you’re full of fluid,” Wowak says.

5. Stay Positive

The worst thing you can do in any kind of survival scenario is dwell on the situation, Wowak says. Keep your mind engaged with survival tasks like making your camp bed, boiling water, organizing your gear, moving your toes and fingers, etcetera.

Giovale says to trust your prior training to get you through the storm.

“My experience with being in serious situations outside is that whatever you have learned or been exposed to, you’ll probably remember,” he says.

Scandinavian design is celebrated for light-filled spaces, pale woods, clean lines, and a sense of calm. Translating that aesthetic to a cruise ship, however, presents a spatial challenge—particularly when compared with the backdrop of the oversized, ornate interiors typical of mass-market cruise liners.

Sunlight pours through the lounge’s expansive windows, illuminating pale wood, textured upholsterery, and warm leather furnishings that lend the space a residential feel. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Viking has made Scandinavian modernism central to its fleet’s design identity, creating onboard spaces that feel less like conventional cruise ships and more like considered residential interiors. Across the vessels, the design language reflects a contemporary interpretation of Scandinavian aesthetics, sometimes called “New Nordic,” through natural textures, tactile materials, subtle color, and sculptural, carefully proportioned furnishings.

A sculptural wood lattice ceiling beneath expansive glazing brings natural light and organic forms into the Wintergarden lounge. Photography courtesy of Viking.

“Although ‘new Nordic’ is not terminology we use formally, traditional Scandinavian principles have evolved and are being interpreted for how people live and travel today,” says Richard Riveire, founding principal of Rottet Studio and the designer of Viking’s river, ocean, and expedition ships. “There’s still a strong sense of restraint, but it’s balanced by a feeling of being lived in. These days, Scandinavian design is less about minimalism and more about creating spaces that feel welcoming, thoughtful, and enduring.”

A deep blue built-in bookcase displays books, maps, shells, coral, and collected objects, connecting the residential atmosphere of Viking’s interiors with the theme of exploration. Photography courtesy of Viking

Drawing On Nordic Principles

Instead of treating Scandinavian design as a static visual style, Riveire and his team anchored the fleet’s interiors around five underlying principles: residential modernism, nature, craft, heritage, and exploration. This framework establishes a restrained architectural foundation, while fabrics, upholstery, rugs, and furniture introduce warmth and texture. “These tactile details make each space feel inviting and personal, while preserving the simplicity that is so central to Scandinavian design,” says Riveire.

An artwork depicting a figure on a beach anchors the atrium stairwell, bringing Nordic art into one of the ship’s main circulation spaces. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Tapping Into Nordic Heritage

Throughout Viking’s fleet, Nordic heritage appears in a collection of regional artwork, including pieces by celebrated Norwegian painter Edvard Munch. Textiles and decorative objects extend those cultural references, featuring fabrics by Dutch designer Hella Jongerius alongside tabletop accessories by Finnish architect Alvar Aalto.

“That heritage connects the design to Norwegian culture and folklore, while exploration reminds us that this is a ship designed for people who are curious about exploring the world,” says Riveire. “So maps, globes, libraries, constellations and other references to exploration play a meaningful role in our designs.”

A traditional Norwegian Marius blanket brings a familiar geometric knitting pattern into the staterooms, referencing a longstanding Norwegian textile tradition. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Mastering Materiality and Craft

Material choices help give Viking’s expansive public spaces a more intimate, residential character. Light woods, natural woven textiles, and stone bring warmth, texture, and visual depth to the staterooms and public areas. “Craftsmanship is fundamental because it gives a space a sense of humanity and authenticity,” says Riveire. “Our design calls for furniture, artwork, textiles, and decor that evoke handmade traditions or honor historic craftwork.” These references to craft appear throughout the ship, in details such as woven leather lounge chairs in the living room areas, the intricate ceiling latticework, and sculptural wood wall accents.

Dramatic glass walls surround the spa’s hydrotherapy pool, pairing natural stone, sculptural driftwood, and wood loungers to create a serene retreat that brings Scandinavian simplicity indoors. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Integrating Biophilic Design

The Nordic landscape—where birch trees, flora, and wildlife symbolize renewal—informs both the materials and sensory details throughout the ships. References to driftwood, lichen, and moss can be found in staterooms and lounges. In the public bathrooms, guests hear birds softly chirping in the background, while birch tree branches in planters and floor-to-ceiling forest wallpaper appear throughout the common areas and spa. “It’s where the landscape and wildlife outside become part of the indoor experience,” says Riveire.

In the spa, amenities such as a snow grotto, cold plunge, and thalassotherapy pool draw on Nordic bathing traditions. “Water reinforces the connection to the outdoors, making the spa an extension of the Nordic design philosophy,” says Riveire. Slate, stone, and glass reinforce the spa’s restrained, minimalist material palette.

Navy and slate blue stripes add a measured dose of color to the upholstered headboard, complementing the stateroom’s otherwise understated palette. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Layering Subtle Color

Rather than relying on stark whites or an entirely neutral palette, the ship’s interiors layer warm creams, sandy tans, and other earthy tones drawn from nature. Navy and slate blue stripes appear on upholstered stateroom headboards, while translucent aqua glass side plates and water glasses introduce light-reflecting accents to restaurant table settings. Orange and rust accents in throw pillows and carpets provide contrast without disrupting the overall palette. “These touches reflect a more expressive side of Scandinavian design,” says Riveire. “We want guests to experience color, but without introducing a lot of visual noise.”

A wall of glass frames ocean views from the pool deck, where pale loungers, blue-and-white textiles, and natural wood maintain the understated palette. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Framing the Natural Landscape

Floor-to-ceiling windows do more than admit daylight; their placement turns the passing landscape into a focal point of the interiors. “A Norwegian fjord, European river, or Antarctic landscape becomes part of the room, an ever-changing piece of art within the interior,” Riveire says. Sightlines and spatial planning help connect the rooms to their changing surroundings.

On land, designers can anticipate the position of the sun and how daylight will move through a home or building. “At sea, the sun is far more variable—not only in direction, but also in duration,” Riveire says. “During Arctic summers, for example, it may never set.” The interiors must therefore accommodate changing light conditions throughout a voyage. Architectural openings, curtains, and shades adjust as conditions shift, balancing dynamic daylight with interior comfort and privacy. “Spaces are connected to the world outside, while also creating more intimate moments indoors,” says Riveire.

A large window frames the passing river landscape, while pale wood, soft neutrals, and streamlined furnishings keep the suite’s interior visually quiet. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Concealing Technology and Softening Acoustics

A residential atmosphere depends on more than visual details. Sound, temperature, and lighting also shape how comfortable a space feels. To the interiors’ uncluttered appearance, technology is integrated discreetly into wood joinery and architectural details, such as underfloor heating in stateroom bathrooms or subtle lighting controls. Acoustic design plays an equally important role in creating a sense of hygge at sea; sound-absorbing textile wall panels, acoustic carpet underlays, and upholstered surfaces help reduce ambient noise in public areas. “In the Scandinavian tradition, design serves human comfort without demanding attention,” says Riveire. “Technology and acoustics are integrated so subtly that you don’t notice how the space functions—only how calm it makes you feel.”

Maritime artifacts, books, a globe, and warm wood shelving create a residential-style display that reflects Viking’s Scandinavian emphasis on craftsmanship and history. Photography courtesy of Viking.

Designing Intimate Spaces at Scale

Capturing a lived-in, residential warmth can be challenging on a vessel with expansive public areas like multi-level atriums, especially when Scandinavian design favors intimate and approachable spaces. “Creating a residential feeling within a ship requires careful attention to proportion,” says Riveire. “At Viking, we subscribe to a ‘cocoon’ principle—a house-within-a-house concept—where the interior provides a quiet and serene onboard environment, rather than constantly competing for attention.” This principle guides the arrangement of open, social hubs and smaller, quiet seating nooks. “Every furniture grouping has been carefully considered for how guests actually move through and use the space,” adds Riveire.

Throwback Thursday: Panasonic’s quarter century of camera innovation

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It’s twenty-five years this week since Panasonic released its first digital cameras, and it’s contributed a lot to the industry in the meantime

DPReview

It was twenty-five years ago this week that Panasonic’s first digital cameras hit the market: the DMC-LC5 and DMC-F7. This means it’s also the twenty-fifth anniversary of the “Lumix” brand.

Panasonic, then itself a sub-brand of Matsushita Electric Industrial Co (which has subsequently adopted the Panasonic name for the whole company), was a little late to the digital camera game, as one of its senior directors acknowledged to Reuters in an article quoted in DPReview’s coverage, just before the first cameras reached the shelves.

The company hasn’t ever quite hit the 10% market share target it set for itself (though it apparently made up 10% of the full-frame market in Europe, earlier this year), but it’s produced some excellent and innovative cameras in the meantime. And its influence on modern cameras and the camera industry transcends such simple metrics.

The original Panasonic Lumix DMC-LC5, one of the two cameras with which the company launched the Lumix brand. Despite the Leica branding, the lens seemed familiar.Panasonic

The original DMC-LC5 was a pretty ambitious first camera, with the larger Type 1/1.76 (40mm2) sensor usually reserved for enthusiast cameras and a design that looks a lot like its contemporary, the Canon G2, with a tunnel-type optical viewfinder, top plate display and mode dial.

The LC5 had a Leica-branded lens: the beginning of a relationship with the German company that continues to this day. Though, interestingly, that lens has exactly the same 7-21mm, F2.0-2.5 specifications as the lens Canon was using in its G2. A fractionally larger sensor meant that the Panasonic delivered a slightly wider 33-100mm equiv range from its zoom, but the likelihood of there being two different lenses with such closely matching specs is incredibly low.  

The remarkably LC5-like Leica Digilux 1 would be launched the subsequent year. And that pattern of Leica-branded variants of many of Panasonic’s most interesting (from our enthusiast photographer perspective) models was to continue.

The Panasonic TZ3 put a 10x zoom in a small camera body. And unlike the compact superzooms that had gone before it, it did so with a lens starting at a wideangle, 28mm equiv.DPReview

Highlights include the FZ series of long zoom ‘bridge’ cameras, starting with the FZ1 in 2004 and encompassing a great range of sensor sizes, as well as zoom lengths, through the years. The TZ series (later rebranded as ZS in the American market) helped define and give a name to the compact superzoom style of travel zooms, and were among the first to use digital distortion correction to allow wideangle zooms in a small camera body. And then there were LX compact enthusiast models, whose ultimate successor is this year’s DC-L10, bringing short but bright lenses, larger sensors and the idea that aspect ratio should be a creative option.

DPReview

Leica wasn’t the only company has Panasonic worked closely with, though. In 2004, it joined Olympus and Kodak’s Four Thirds system, producing two DSLRs before spearheading the reinvention of Four Thirds as Micro Four Thirds: the world’s first mirrorless (interchangeable lens) camera system.

It’s strange to look back and realize that there were only five years between the launch of the first wholly designed-for-digital DSLR and the first mirrorless camera. The original Four Thirds DSLR system wasn’t formally discontinued until 2017 but Panasonic had ushered in a new era the day it announced the G1.

It’s hard to imagine Panasonic (or any company, frankly) having such a profound impact on the industry in a single move again, especially as seems unlikely that the organic film/CMOS hybrid sensor its parent company has developed, will make its way across to consumer cameras.

It looked, and operated, a lot like a contemporary DSLR, but the Panasonic DMC-G1 would fundamentally change the industryDPReview

But the creation of Mirrorless camera isn’t Panasonic’s only contribution to camera technology that it’s easy to now take for granted.

Its Dual IS system was the first to use in-lens and in-body stabilization simultaneously, in a co-ordinated manner. Other brands have subsequently adopted this approach, but Panasonic was several years ahead of most.

Likewise, we’re pretty sure Panasonic was the first brand to adopt context-sensitive noise reduction in its JPEG engine: applying more noise reduction in what it interpreted as low-detail regions and less in areas where details needed to be preserved. It was also one of the first to offer subject recognition autofocus trained by machine learning, though it didn’t think to promote it as “AI”-powered, so it didn’t necessarily get the recognition this warranted.

It’s impossible to cover Panasonic’s contribution to the industry without mentioning its GH series of video-forward hybrid cameras

The other very visible impact Panasonic has had on the camera industry has been in raising the expectations of the video capabilities of hybrid cameras. Particularly through its GH series, Panasonic has been the first to shoot 4K, the first to capture 10-bit video, the first to offer Log gamma, the first to have a waveforms display, the first to let you choose the conversion gain mode and the first to be able to capture 32-bit float audio. All of them features that were once the preserve of dedicated video models, many of which have become commonplace on the most mainstream models, because the R&D to provide them has already been done.

Panasonic’s contribution to cameras isn’t something to solely be discussed in the past tense. The S1II is at the cutting edge of what the latest cameras can offer.Richard Butler

This is far from a complete reflection of the impact Panasonic has had. A quarter of a century of progress has seen it deliver a great many interesting cameras and innovations, and cameras like the S1II and L10 suggest there will be plenty of more interesting things to come. But whether you think of the company as Panasonic or Lumix, and regardless of whether you’ve owned and loved one of its products, it’s impossible to ignore its contributions over the past 25 years.
