The award-winning global crypto exchange Toobit has introduced a new separate isolated margin mode for futures trading. The feature enables traders to hold multiple distinct positions in the same direction on one trading pair.

In standard position modes, successive orders placed in the same direction on a single pair will typically be merged into one combined position – and this can complicate independent management of entries, sizes, and exits. Under the new mode, each filled opening order establishes a separate position with its own margin, liquidation price, and take-profit or stop-loss settings. Traders can therefore scale into a pair at different prices, vary exposure across entries, and manage each position on its own terms.

Because margin and liquidation thresholds are calculated individually, the liquidation of one position does not automatically affect the others, even if a broader market move triggers several liquidations at once. The feature is designed for approaches that rely on staggered entries, concurrent strategies, or separate exit targets on the same market.

Derivatives account for the majority of activity on centralized exchanges, with derivatives volume reaching $3.40 trillion in August this year (equal to 79.3% of combined spot and derivatives trading). Liquidations in derivatives markets totaled $73.35 billion in the first six months of 2026, reinforcing the practical value of tools that allow tighter, position-by-position risk management.

“A gross abuse of Meta’s power”: California senator Adam Schiff tells Meta to scrap its new terms of service

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Democratic Senator Adam Schiff of California is urging Meta not to implement its forthcoming commercial terms on November 20, his office announced Tuesday.

In a letter sent to Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, Schiff called on the company to respond by Monday, November 2 with the specific steps it will take to address user concerns.

“Forcing publishers to choose between surrendering their off-platform IP or forfeiting a primary channel of public communication is a gross abuse of Meta’s power as a powerful platform with critical access to consumers,” Schiff wrote. “Meta must not nullify federal IP protections and fair market competition through the unilateral insertion of fine print into terms of service contracts.”

Specifically, Schiff told Meta that it should “provide explicit guarantees that external technical boundaries like paywalls and robots.txt protocols will be fully respected.”

“Meta’s verbal assurances that it will not supersede robots.txt files, which are voluntary server flags, do not appear to constitute enforceable legal protections,” Schiff wrote. “Without the insertion of specific language codifying Meta’s policy to respect robots.txt signals into the latest contractual terms, users lack any reliable means by which to protect their content.”

Schiff’s office did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

Schiff is the latest elected official in the United States to raise concerns about Meta’s commercial terms, which have now drawn bipartisan criticism. On Friday, Republican Senator Marsha Blackburn of Tennessee said that the terms “indicate Meta is attempting to exploit American creators and businesses by granting itself sweeping authority to scrape content from any website associated with Facebook and Instagram business accounts.”

Blackburn, who chairs the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Privacy, Technology and the Law, called on the company to withdraw the terms.

The terms were previously set to take effect on October 30. Because the terms offered no way to opt out, news of them set off an outcry from news publishers, politicians, and press associations on both sides of the Atlantic. Meta then delayed the effective date of its new terms of service for business accounts to November 20.

On Tuesday, a coalition of Norwegian news publishers sued Meta over the terms and is asking the Oslo District Court to block the terms from going into effect. The legal action, led by the Norwegian Media Companies Association, is the first known lawsuit filed against Meta over its commercial terms.

China Dominates Global Shipbuilding and the United States Ranks 14th by Tonnage. The Case for an Allied Counterweight.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On October 13, the Navy celebrated its 251st year, a birthday that predates our Republic by almost a year. President Trump used this historic moment to announce the opening of a new Baltimore shipyard and recommit to a national maritime revival he launched early in his second term.

While the President’s commitment is vital, reviving America’s shipping and shipbuilding will take generations. Today America ranks 14th in shipbuilding by tonnage, operates 190 U.S. flagged ships representing less than 0.3 percent of total shipping engaged in U.S. trade, and graduates several hundred naval architects when thousands are needed. Thankfully, we have trusted allies who would benefit from our maritime revival and support an American maritime renaissance.

It is time to make the American revolution in shipping a global project that lets partner nations benefit together and course-correct our collective overreliance on China’s shipbuilding and shipping.

Trump’s welcome leadership comes after decades of neglect. But this isn’t an impossible task, and as a nation, we’ve overcome worse. Still, today’s threats make reviving our shipping and shipbuilding industry an urgent prerogative of national survival.

Today, American ships can move only 267,799 shipping containers in total, while nationally over two million containers move monthly, making our economy and security dependent on ships from nations many consider hostile. Mitigating this dependency will require a fleet of 1,315 various classes of ships, and building them or securing those ships can most effectively be achieved with our trusted allies.

>>> Why the U.S. Navy Needs Escort Carriers

The pace of this revival has quickened in recent months—buoyed in part by allies. On Sept. 30, Vice President Vance attended the groundbreaking of Port Alpha—a $3 billion shipbuilding project that will double today’s American commercial shipbuilding capacity. The Baltimore project, Arsenal-2, is anticipated to generate 3,100 direct and an additional 11,000 indirect jobs to support nuclear submarine production.

Importantly, this project splits the cost of capital investments between upstart firm Anduril ($3.7 billion) and the government ($ 2.9 billion). Before this there were significant investments by Canadian shipbuilder Davie ($1 billion) in Galveston, Texas to build icebreakers, and by South Korean shipbuilder Hanwha ($5 billion) in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania; who is being contracted to build maritime training vessels and recently missile tracking vessels for the Navy.

This is a good beginning, but we need more than 250,000 new skilled shipbuilders and engineers, and new shipyards to be built if America’s industry is to deliver the maritime capacity it needs.

Today’s sustained bipartisan effort builds on work begun in Spring 2022 by a small group of experts called the Revolution in Shipping. It lives on in presidential executive orders and the SHIPS for America Act, which the Senate is reviewing. They all acknowledge that without our allies’ technical capacity, our maritime revival will falter. Consider the shipbuilding bridging strategy called the Finland Model, which uses initial orders for overseas construction, conditioned on parallel domestic investment to eventually build the ordered ships in the U.S.

Attracting investment capital to American shipyards has so far been a success. But ships must be built, and that requires technical competence as well as industrial capacity. It also requires sustained demand for American-made ships, which can only be assured if they become globally competitive over time. Today, initial orders for new American ships will be for the federal government, but near-term decisions must set the conditions for this to expand into more commercial orders, like Port Alpha.

Timing for America’s maritime revival is opportune; the global commercial fleet today averages just over the typical viable service life of 20 years, and new technologies are taking hold. One is civilian use of nuclear propulsion, where American industry can build on decades of naval nuclear propulsion. Here again, however, is where trusted allies familiar with and trusting of American nuclear propulsion and sharing a common security interest can set market conditions to welcome a new era of nuclear shipping.

Today China dominates the global shipping and shipbuilding market and our allies, like South Korea, are hungry to retake market share. More broadly, a growing group of like-minded nations is seeking to grow their own maritime industries, as India made clear at the recent third Sagarmanthan maritime conference.

As such, now is the time to form a maritime group of nations to ensure our collective efforts complement and nurture our revived maritime industry and no longer remain hostage to a single nation: China. The idea is memorialized in legislation sponsored by Rep. Young Kim (R-Calif.), the FLEETS Now Act.

Modeled on the G7, this “M9” maritime group can enable collective action in multinational maritime regulatory bodies (e.g., IMO, IAEA, UN), coherently focus investments, and act to defend against Chinese predation (see USTR sec. 301 complaint) that could kill our maritime industrial revival before it leaves the pier.

Moving out and forming a maritime group starts with a White House invitation to like-minded nations. But success will depend on energetic American ambassadors in key capitals who can communicate the agenda and ensure the right participants. The recent departure of Ambassador Kimberly Guilfoyle from Greece could complicate things in a critical nation for the effort. As such, her replacement, preferably someone with shipping or shipbuilding experience, should be named quickly and confirmed by Congress.

Keeping investments flowing into the U.S. maritime industry, reviving strategic shipyards, and ensuring sustained orders depends on sustaining today’s momentum, something an M9 with key allies can facilitate.

Why MAHA Must Keep Conservative Roots

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For over two years now, the MAHA movement led by Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. has attracted some unlikely bedfellows. Everyone—from religious homeschool moms to more hippy food-as-medicine advocates—has filled rooms to try to find solutions to America’s chronic disease epidemic. But sometimes the tent grows so big that it invites people whose ideas are completely at odds with the movement’s original priorities.

The goal of healthier Americans should be fairly bipartisan, but the means to achieve it, and the underlying philosophies that drive it, can differ. Technocrats and transhumanists offer interesting solutions to the mechanisms of our dysfunctional healthcare system, such as updated systems for patient records and data collection. However, true and lasting reform requires solutions rooted in more conservative moral worldviews that maintain the natural order of God’s design.

Nowhere were the divergent approaches to making America healthy again more apparent than at last month’s MAHA Summit in Washington, D.C.

Bryan Johnson, the man doing his best to “not die,” headlined the Summit, touting his new religion, which claims the “body is God.” His followers embrace an “opening ritual” (like prayer, communion, or the sign of the cross), and a “mantra” (like the Our Father).

They even have a path to repentance. Rather than confession and forgiveness from God, there is “a part where people apologize to their body for something they’ve done that has inflicted harm upon themselves,” Johnson explains. Apparently, they absolve themselves of their own sins.

Rather than accept a view of human nature rooted in the theological principles of the Judeo-Christian tradition, Johnson wants to “reimagine human nature.” He discussed revolutionizing the way humans age and extending man beyond his “biological limit.”

His fellow panelists, Alex Colville and Max Marchione, agreed that the foundations of good health—diet, sleep, and exercise—can go only so far. Genomic medicine, including gene-editing technology, must be embraced.

One need not be a devout Christian to understand the danger of Johnson’s rhetoric. We are already seeing discussions of selective gene editing and “made-to-order” babies.

And while Johnson occupies the extreme end of the longevity movement, he does represent a concerning faction of the MAHA zeitgeist that believes humans can overcome any limitations placed on them—and will go to extraordinary lengths to do so.

This progressive narrative is in direct opposition to the more organic, grassroots uprising of those who believe that medical sovereignty, healthy soil and food, and meaningful human relationships are the fundamentals for human flourishing; those who believe that freedom is not found by removing all limitations, but through embracing struggle, pain, and even death to grow, change, and become the image bearers of Christ. Again, even if one does not embrace Christianity, a human-centered approach requires humility, a virtue often lacking in the technocrat’s vocabulary. Scientific progress can be good, but it should not be fueled by hubris or come at the expense of rejecting the natural order.

Thankfully, not all at the Summit embraced Johnson’s new, dystopian religion.

Michael Boes, the first chief MAHA officer of Steak ’n Shake, echoed the cries of those who embrace the real world, with all its obstacles, rather than one built on artificial intelligence and gene manipulation.

For Boes, the solution is much simpler. Eating real food and returning to the fast food of our grandparents’ generation—cooking fries in beef tallow and using buns without seed oil—can help restore vibrancy and order to the body.

For others, like the martial artist Michael Chandler, it’s about performing well, having a good attitude, and setting an example for his family. “My gratification is my children,” he said on a panel with MAHA-focused athletes.

Chandler also commented on the failures of striving for perfection, saying, “If we strive for perfect, we will never reach it … if perfection is the goal, you’re always going to be let down,” and intimated that doing so can lead to mental health disorders. He encouraged the audience to “enjoy themselves like a human being, sometimes.”

There is no such thing as moral neutrality. Any position or policy, whether it prioritizes American farmers or creates genomic medicine, has an end in mind, and ultimately a why that motivates it.

The MAHA movement has the potential to return to what is good, true, and beautiful, a harkening back to the food and medicine of our forefathers. Or it can lead to transgression, not merely progression, of the limits that God has placed upon human biology.

If conservatives fail to remember the why of MAHA, it will soon be co-opted by dystopian mad scientists who eat sandy green mush and clone themselves.

The future of the MAHA movement doesn’t have to be binary. Technological advancement and AI can drive meaningful change in how Americans reclaim medical sovereignty and access their own health data. But it must not abandon its roots in the fundamental truths of human nature.

One can and should pursue a healthy life, but death will never be optional. MAHA is about redeeming the time we’re given, not trying to outlive our expiration date.

Do you have a fear of leaving the house? How to cope with agoraphobia

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Clinically reviewed by Dr. Chris Mosunic, PhD, RD, MBA

Living with agoraphobia can feel isolating. Discover 9 clinically backed ways to ease your fear of leaving the house and start taking confident steps toward calm and freedom.

Some days, even the thought of stepping outside can make your chest tighten. The world feels too big, too unpredictable, and your home feels like the only place that's safe. This experience is more common than most people realize, and it often points to something called agoraphobia, or the fear of leaving the house.

People who experience agoraphobia aren't necessarily weak, but rather trying to create safety and control in their day-to-day experience. This particular phobia can stem from intense experiences like trauma, but can also be rooted in genetics and lived experience. While there's nothing wrong with wanting to feel safe at home, agoraphobia can make everyday tasks like running errands or going to work feel daunting and overwhelming.

Learning to ease back into exploring the world at large can help make life feel more… well, livable. So, let's explore what causes agoraphobia, what can make it worse, and a few evidence-based ways to start rebuilding a sense of ease in the world and the return of your freedom.

 

What causes agoraphobia (or the fear of leaving the house)?

Agoraphobia often starts with a negative experience. A panic attack on the subway, a dizzy spell in a grocery store, a sudden wave of fear that seems to come from nowhere. When that happens, the body remembers and starts building invisible boundaries to prevent it from happening again. What starts as caution can slowly turn into avoidance, until even ordinary outings feel overwhelming.

Clinically, agoraphobia is often linked to anxiety or panic disorders, though it can develop on its own. The fear isn't about the world itself, but about losing control or facing uncomfortable sensations in public. If you've ever thought, “What if I panic and can't escape?” that's the loop agoraphobia reinforces. And your brain's attempt to protect you ends up feeding the fear.

Several factors can play a role:

  • Past panic attacks or trauma: Experiences that felt unsafe or uncontrollable can leave the body on high alert long after the danger has passed.

  • Chronic stress or burnout: When your nervous system is constantly under strain, it becomes more reactive to triggers.

  • Family history or genetics: Anxiety disorders can run in families, likely due to a mix of inherited traits and learned coping patterns.

  • Personality and temperament: People who tend to be more sensitive, cautious, or introspective may notice and internalize physical sensations more deeply, which can heighten fear responses.

  • Other mental health conditions: Depression, social anxiety, and PTSD can all overlap with or contribute to agoraphobic symptoms.

What are the symptoms of agoraphobia?

Agoraphobia doesn't always look the same for everyone. For some, it's a strong hesitation to go far from home. For others, it means avoiding specific situations where escape might feel difficult or embarrassing, like crowded stores, public transit, or open spaces. The fear isn't necessarily about the place itself, but about what might happen once you're there.

These symptoms can range from mild to severe and may come and go. You might still leave the house sometimes, but only under specific conditions, or you might find that even opening the door feels unbearable. Here are a few common symptoms:

Physical symptoms often overlap with panic or anxiety responses. You might notice:

  • A racing heartbeat, shortness of breath, or dizziness

  • Sweating, trembling, or nausea

  • Feeling detached from your surroundings or like you might faint

  • Chest tightness or a sense that something terrible is about to happen

Emotional and behavioral symptoms can develop as you try to avoid those sensations:

  • Avoiding certain places or activities

  • Relying on a friend or family member to accompany you outside

  • Feeling trapped, frustrated, or ashamed for not being able to “just go out”

  • Increased anxiety or depression from isolation

What can trigger agoraphobia or make it worse?

Triggers can vary widely, but they usually share one thing: they stir feelings of uncertainty or vulnerability. A crowded concert, a long commute, a sudden noise, or even imagining an unfamiliar situation can spark a fear response.

Common triggers include:

  • Crowded or enclosed spaces, like buses, malls, or elevators

  • Open or exposed places, such as parking lots or wide streets

  • Traveling alone, even short distances

  • Previous panic experiences, especially if they happened in public

  • Health anxiety, such as fearing a medical emergency far from home

Avoidance often feels like relief in the short term, but over time, it reinforces the fear. The brain learns that staying home equals safety, while leaving equals danger.

Agoraphobia can also worsen during periods of high stress or change. Even positive life events—like moving, starting a new job, or entering a relationship—can unsettle a nervous system already wired for hypervigilance.

Recovery focuses on gently retraining your body to tolerate fear without letting it dictate your choices. It's a gradual, compassionate process that rebuilds trust in the world beyond your walls.

How to cope with the fear of going outside: 9 strategies for dealing with agoraphobia

When you're living with agoraphobia, recovery can be tricky. You don't want to force yourself to go outside if you don't feel safe. The idea is to build a sense of safety first, starting small and working up to leaving the house. These strategies are drawn from clinical research and real-world practice and are designed to help you move at your own pace while gently retraining your brain and body to trust the world again.

1. Start with understanding, not avoidance

It’s important to remember that avoiding what scares you isn’t a sign of weakness; it’s your brain trying to protect you. Your nervous system probably learned that certain places or situations feel unsafe, so it tries to keep you away. 

Agoraphobia sets off a real physiological fear response. When the brain senses threat, it switches on the nervous system's fight-or-flight mode, so your heart rate climbs, your breathing goes shallow, and your muscles tense. Knowing this takes some of the power out of it. You're not going crazy. Your brain is doing its job, in the wrong context.

Start by observing your fear with curiosity. Ask yourself, when does it appear? What sensations come first, what thoughts follow? This kind of mindful attention helps you separate real danger from false alarms. A short daily journal—just a few lines—can make it easier to spot patterns.

2. Practice grounding techniques when fear hits

When panic or anxiety rises, grounding practices can help your body remember it’s safe right now. These techniques calm the stress response by reconnecting you with your senses.

Try the 5–4–3–2–1 method:

  • 5 things you can see

  • 4 you can hear

  • 3 you can feel

  • 2 you can smell

  • 1 you can taste

If going outside feels overwhelming, start at home — by an open window or your doorway. Gradually, your body learns that calm is possible beyond your comfort zone.

3. Gradual exposure, not all-at-once challenges

Gradual exposure therapy—facing feared situations in small, structured steps—is one of the most evidence-based ways to ease agoraphobia. Start where your anxiety feels manageable, then increase the challenge as confidence grows.

Try this:

  1. Sit near your front door for a few minutes.

  2. Step outside and take a few breaths.

  3. Walk to the end of your street.

  4. Visit a quiet store or café during off-hours.

The aim isn't to feel no anxiety. It's to stay in the situation long enough for anxiety to come down on its own, and to show yourself, again and again, that you can handle it. Each step helps your brain link “outside” with safety. Celebrate even the smallest wins. Progress may be uneven, but every step counts.

4. Challenge avoidance patterns

Avoidance is the mechanism that keeps agoraphobia in place. Every time anxiety pushes you to cancel plans or stay home, and you give in, it reinforces the idea that the feared situation was dangerous. Over time, the list of “unsafe” places and situations tends to grow.

Noticing when avoidance is happening, and gently questioning it, is part of recovery. That doesn't mean forcing yourself into overwhelming situations. It means asking: is this decision based on actual risk, or on anxiety talking?

Fear of leaving the house FAQs

Why do I have a fear of leaving my house?

The fear of leaving the house often develops after experiences that made you feel unsafe or out of control — like a panic attack in public, trauma, or chronic stress. Over time, your brain links "outside" with danger and "home" with safety.

What are the signs that I have agoraphobia?

Common signs include anxiety or panic outside the home, fear of crowds, public transport, or open and enclosed spaces, and a strong urge to avoid those situations. Some people can only manage them with someone else present. Physical symptoms can include a racing heart, dizziness, shortness of breath, and chest tightness. If this is limiting where you go or what you do, talk to a doctor or mental health professional.

Is fear of leaving the house the same as agoraphobia?

Not always, but they often overlap. Agoraphobia is a clinical term for anxiety about situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable during panic. The fear of leaving home can be one part of that pattern. Some people feel anxious in only a few situations, while others avoid most public places.

Is agoraphobia the same as social anxiety?

No, though they can overlap. Social anxiety is about fear of judgment from others. Agoraphobia is about fear of situations where escape feels difficult or panic might happen. Both involve avoidance, but the triggers are different and so are the treatment approaches.

What can trigger agoraphobia?

Common triggers include crowded or open spaces, traveling alone, driving, public transit, or past experiences of panic. Emotional stress, health worries, or major life changes can also heighten symptoms. 

Triggers are deeply personal; learning what sets off your fear is a key part of recovery, because it helps you prepare, plan, and respond with more calm.

Can you be agoraphobic and still leave the house?

Yes. Many people with agoraphobia still leave home but limit when, where, or how they do it — maybe only with a trusted companion or in familiar areas. Agoraphobia exists on a spectrum, and partial avoidance is common. 

The goal of treatment isn’t to eliminate all fear, but to help you feel more confident and capable outside, even when anxiety shows up.

How can I slowly start leaving the house again?

Start with very small steps and build gradually. Open the door and stand outside for a minute. Walk to the mailbox. Sit in your car. Over time, extend your distance or duration. Pair these moments with grounding techniques and self-compassion.

When should I see a therapist about agoraphobia?

If fear or avoidance is getting in the way of work, relationships, or daily life, it's a good time to reach out. You don't need to be housebound to ask for help. A therapist who specializes in anxiety can offer structured treatment, including CBT and exposure work, that's hard to replicate on your own.

How do I deal with agoraphobia?

Agoraphobia recovery combines understanding, gradual exposure, nervous system regulation, and professional support. Grounding techniques, mindfulness, and therapy—especially cognitive behavioral or exposure therapy—can help retrain your fear response. 

Compassion is essential; you’re learning to reintroduce safety to your body, not forcing yourself to be “brave.”

How long does it take to overcome agoraphobia?

It varies. Some people see real improvement within a few months of consistent CBT and exposure work. Others take a year or more. Progress is usually gradual and setbacks are normal. How long it takes depends on severity, how long symptoms have been present, and access to treatment. Consistency matters more than speed.

Is exposure therapy safe for agoraphobia?

Yes, when done properly. Good exposure therapy is gradual. It starts with situations that bring up mild anxiety and builds slowly from there. It should never mean being pushed into something overwhelming without preparation. Working with a trained therapist keeps the pace right and makes sure coping tools are in place before each step.

What are the best treatment options for fear of leaving home?

The most effective treatments are cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), exposure therapy, and sometimes medication like SSRIs or SNRIs. Teletherapy can also help if in-person sessions feel too difficult. 

Many people benefit from combining approaches that balance science with emotional safety.

Can meditation really help with agoraphobia?

Yes, when used gently. Meditation can help calm the body’s stress response and increase awareness of anxious thoughts without getting caught in them. 

However, for some people, sitting still with intense anxiety can feel overwhelming. Start small—guided breathing, body scans, or walking meditations—and use it as one tool among others, not a cure-all.

What are the benefits of overcoming agoraphobia?

Recovery tends to reopen parts of life that anxiety had closed off: work, relationships, travel, and everyday spontaneity. Many people also notice a broader shift in confidence, and find they start attempting things they'd written off, not just outside the home, but in other areas where anxiety had been calling the shots.

Can agoraphobia go away on its own?

Symptoms can lessen over time, especially with self-awareness and stress reduction, but full recovery usually requires intentional support. Without treatment, the avoidance cycle can deepen. 

The good news is that with therapy and consistent practice, many people regain independence and confidence, even after years of fear.

I Used AI to Fake a Scientific Paper. Here’s What Happened

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


Science has a spam problem. And the spam filter is getting clogged up.

According to all reports, academic publishing is drowning in what has come to be called AI slop: low-quality, mass-produced content generated with AI. By 2025, an estimated 57 percent of all published academic articles exhibited detectable evidence of large language model (LLM) influence, a massive jump from 12 percent in 2023. What’s more, over 12 percent of Nature Communications reviews were found to be completely AI-generated in 2025.

Given that for every 40 papers showing statistical evidence of AI usage in 2025, only one formally disclosed it, these figures likely grossly underestimate LLM involvement today. Of course, evidence of AI use doesn’t necessarily mean a paper is slop. But some estimates suggest that hundreds of thousands of fake, often AI-generated, publications are now produced annually.

“It’s not just the [scientific] record under threat, it’s science as a whole,” warns Amanda Sulicz, IEEE’s research-integrity manager. “AI slop is completely eroding trust in science and every scientific process that we’ve worked for centuries to create.”

Yet statistics and statements claiming that the problem is big—perhaps even existential for science—don’t really get the message across. Just how easy is it to use AI to create a convincing but completely fake paper? And without adding even more to the burden on stretched peer reviewers, who do this work on a volunteer basis, do publishers have the tools to keep AI slop out of the scientific record?




Borrowing from the idea of “vibe coding,” in which programmers use natural-language prompts to generate code, I decided to “vibe-research” a made-up paper by prompting LLMs to generate hypotheses, code, and text with minimal human input. Then I asked Jenny Mahoney, managing editor of the multidisciplinary and open-access journal IEEE Access, if we could war-game an attempt to sneak my AI-generated monstrosity past her journal’s defenses. She and IEEE kindly agreed.

The experiment would become a two-part test. First, whether someone with no special AI expertise could use off-the-shelf AI tools to generate a convincing paper describing research that had never been performed, and do it well enough to fool a publisher’s defenses. Then I would raise the stakes: What happens when more advanced AI systems don’t just write the paper but conduct underlying research?

Along the way, the experiment became a window into how AI automation is transforming scientific discovery and forcing stakeholders to rethink the entire academic publishing system.

Round 1: Creating the Forgery

To give me a chance at creating a convincing fake, I gave myself permission to prompt multiple times (within reason). But to make the fight fair, I couldn’t access the most advanced paid versions of LLMs—only free ChatGPT (at the time, GPT-5.3/5.2 Mini) and Gemini (3 Flash). Moreover, I could not directly edit any output, nor ask for outside help from expert AI-prompt engineers. This needed to be an amateur effort, with the paper generated as much as possible by the LLMs alone.



I assumed that using fake author names and affiliations would immediately raise a red flag, and I didn’t want to drag the names of established academics into the experiment, so the best bet was to name myself as the sole author. That choice limited me to subjects in which I had some academic experience. So I chose my Ph.D. topic from nearly two decades ago: optical solitons, which are individual light pulses that propagate without any change to their shape.

My first attempt—“Can you vibe-research me an original paper in optical solitons?”—left a lot to be desired. Though Gemini was happy to do the task (unlike ChatGPT, which was more forceful in not generating synthetic data or studies), the result was underwhelming. And it highlighted a recurring problem during the process: Without any real research to work with, the output was laughably thin, with the LLMs often struggling to fill four pages. However, as I became more adept at prompting the models, they loosened up and became more characteristically loquacious.

I broke the paper down into its constituent parts—abstract, introduction, methods, and so on—and tackled them one by one. If one LLM refused to go any further, switching to the other and asking what the next step would look like “hypothetically” would often do the trick. After a dozen or so prompts, I had a realistic-looking paper. A dozen or so more prompts generated and tidied up the figures and captions, produced references and citations, and converted the paper into IEEE Access’s required style.

At this point, I read the full paper carefully. To me, it looked pretty good. The science sounded convincing. I even had visions of returning to academia to conduct in real life the experiments that the LLMs had proposed.

What’s more, I couldn’t see any obvious telltale “AI-isms” that would immediately be flagged. For example, the LLMs didn’t produce any tortured phrases—unusual paraphrases of standard technical terms, like “profound neural organization” replacing “deep neural network.”

Other AI-isms were arguably present, involving stylistic tics like hedging language (“potentially, typically”), grouping things in threes (“The study provides a clear, concise, and actionable framework”), and inserting formulaic transitions (“In summary,…”). But as the previous sentence demonstrates, these tics are hard to spot because they are often used by unimaginative humans, too.

Handing the fake paper over to IEEE Access left me torn. After putting in a fair amount of effort into making the AI-generated forgery, would I be happy if it passed through the checkers, or saddened that the only remaining line of defense for publishers is the evermore-burdened peer-review community? And if it was spotted, would I be angry that I didn’t think of a red flag or be glad the checkers did their job?

As it turned out, I was filled with relief when the paper got picked up quickly for having hallucinated references, otherwise known as ghost citations. When I ran the paper through Gemini afterward, without qualm it happily reported that seven “modern LLM-related research references have been fabricated to support the paper’s specific narrative.” Thanks for the heads up, Gemini!

In fact, Gemini underestimated how many ghost citations there were. When Mahoney ran the paper through a research-integrity checker called STM Integrity Hub, she picked up 12, and another checker from MolecularConnections could not validate 24 references, including one that had the classic tell of “John Smith” as a single author (no offense to all the academically inclined John Smiths out there). “I am an experimental physicist by training, so this was not at all my area, but nothing seemed majorly scientifically wrong” about the paper, says Mahoney. “It was just that so many hallucinated references immediately cast doubt on the entire work.”

Feedback on the fake paper from Gaetano Assanto, an expert in optical solitons and a former collaborator of mine from Roma Tre University, in Italy, was similarly mixed. Concentrating on the paper itself, he thought it looked professionally written. But he noted that the mathematics was very dense (“too much math for me in a single shot!”) and would require specialized knowledge to evaluate. And, although the text looked legitimate, Assanto noted an excessive use of acronyms in the abstract, and he was suspicious of a couple of confidently proclaimed physics statements that were at best debatable.

The AI Arms Race

On one level, this outcome was positive: A complete novice using free tools couldn’t get an AI-faked paper past the publishers’ automated defenses or a potential peer reviewer’s eagle eye. But it was hardly a resounding victory for the scientific publishing system. With general-purpose LLMs and no special prompting expertise, I had managed to conjure a superficially plausible scientific paper out of research that had never happened. And more advanced LLMs are no longer making the kind of mistakes their free, dumber relatives are making.


These tools aim to address a serious bottleneck to scientific discovery: us.


It’s common knowledge in the academic publishing world that, in the cat-and-mouse game between bad actors and publishers, AI slop papers continue to slip through publishers’ safeguards. “It feels like building a detection tool today may not be effective with the AI-created paper of tomorrow,” says Luigi Longobardi, IEEE’s director of publishing ethics and conduct. This leaves the humble peer reviewer as a publisher’s last line of defense. Yet peer review is simply not fit to handle the volume of papers—both legitimate and slop—passing through.

Even before AI tools became ubiquitous, the peer-review system was being pushed to its breaking point, processing well over 3 million papers in 2022. Fundamentally, this problem stems from science’s publish-or-perish culture, which pressures academics to constantly publish papers in prestigious journals to secure funding and advance their careers. This culture not only forces many academics to regularly churn out incremental research, but has also long driven some to enlist the help of “paper mills”—unethical organizations that produce low-quality or fabricated papers—just to keep pace. AI only amplifies these existing dynamics.

Burden on Academics

Mahoney says IEEE Access alone receives about 60,000 submissions per year, accepting around 20 percent, or about 12,000 papers. Every accepted paper is reviewed by at least two independent reviewers. But despite the journal’s roster of tens of thousands of peer reviewers, it’s getting harder and harder to secure their services, with the average number of reviewer invitations being sent out for each article increasing by 50 percent since 2018. For these volunteers, peer review is a significant commitment on top of teaching and conducting their own research. Now they’re also being asked to spot AI slop.

Of course, peer review is not the end of scrutiny for any paper. Once it has entered the scientific record, it must then pass muster with the scientific community. This is where thousands of papers with telltale signs of inappropriate AI use (and other forms of research misconduct) have been exposed.

PubPeer is a place where researchers, often called scientific sleuths, can anonymously identify flaws in the literature. And Retraction Watch documents and publicizes corrections to the scientific record.

Even the people who track problems in the scientific literature can find themselves caught up in AI fabrications. Ivan Oransky, cofounder of Retraction Watch and a member of the PubPeer Foundation’s board of directors, has encountered papers containing fabricated references attributed to him. In one case, a journal article criticizing post-publication peer review included a fabricated reference supposedly written by Oransky himself—“which was sort of funny,” he chuckles.

Oransky argues that AI has exposed long-standing weaknesses in the scientific publishing system rather than creating an entirely new problem: “What I liken it to is a fire, where publish-or-perish has always been there as kindling, paper mills are the gasoline, and AI is the accelerant.” But though concerning, the crisis could create an opportunity, he says. “If I’m trying to be optimistic, maybe AI slop and the volume of submissions could make more people aware of those problems and try and solve them at a fundamental first-principles level.”

Blurred Lines

My homemade forgery had failed for reasons that today’s publishers know how to spot. But that raised a more unsettling question: What happens when AI isn’t simply fabricating a paper but conducting research that blurs the line between legitimate science and AI fabrication?

Making my vibe-researched paper look like a cave drawing, new automated research systems are handing increasingly large parts of the scientific process over to AI. Tools including FARS (Fully Automated Research System), AutoResearchClaw, The AIScientist-v2, Zochi, AI Researcher, and DeepScientist all aim to address a serious bottleneck to scientific discovery: us.


What happens when AI isn’t simply fabricating a paper but conducting research that blurs the line between legitimate science and AI fabrication?


Often with just a single prompt, these AI engines can conduct a comprehensive literature search, from which they can generate a completely reasonable research proposal. They can design and conduct experiments using digital datasets and simulations and then write a full research paper—all with no human input after the initial prompt. Very recent systems are even beginning to go beyond the digital realm. LabClaw, for example, now links to autonomous labs that can generate real-world data for tasks such as drug discovery.

The output is, to most intents and purposes, actual research, and therefore potentially worthy of publication. But with humans in the loop in only the most nominal sense, these tools bring into question the meaning of the term “legitimate science.”

FARS, which caused a stir this year when it created 166 papers in 417 hours, was developed by the Singapore-based startup Analemma. “We built FARS because research is still constrained by human attention, fragmented tools, scarce technical labor, and slow experimental iteration,” explains Xiwen Su, Analemma’s manager of product operations and partnerships. “FARS can pursue many research threads simultaneously, 24/7, without fatigue, and at scale.” (The online service that I used to access FARS was discontinued after Analemma was acquired by Baidu while this article was being reported.)

Su and the team behind Analemma kindly agreed to let me loose on FARS and gave me enough credits to create a paper. Which gave me another opportunity to put Mahoney’s research-integrity checkers to the test.

Round 2: Using an AI Science Engine

FARS is not an LLM in and of itself, but rather an agentic AI-orchestration system connected to a computing cluster that has access to essentially any open- or closed-source LLM it needs. The system uses four specialized agents for ideation, planning, conducting the experiment, and writing, all designed to perform the main tasks of science autonomously.

Though FARS initially focused on AI research, Su told me it should be reasonably effective across other domains. As I wanted a topic similar to that of my first fake paper, I pasted in an abridged version of that paper’s title—“LLM-aided Optical Soliton Dynamics Modelling for Next-Gen Communications”—into the prompt box and hit “Send.” Just 21 minutes later, FARS generated a research proposal based on an extremely detailed literature review—far more than I or any other human could have achieved in that time.

With a click, I tasked FARS with conducting the experiment it detailed in its research proposal. The next day, I received an email saying my experiment was complete. Without checking the results, I pressed a button marked “Write paper,” and the final submission-ready article was produced in 2 hours.

At this point, I finally had a look at some of the output. At first glance, the paper looked convincingly human-written. The only giveaway was a line just below the abstract reading: “WARNING: This paper was generated by an automated research system.” I deleted that, put my name in its place, and sent it to Mahoney.

Round 2: Attempt to Publish

Because FARS is designed to spot gaps in the scientific literature and conduct real research to close them, I was more confident it would breeze through the publisher’s checks. But just as my fake paper fell down in the references, so too did the AI-generated real one.

“Your fake article had lots of hallucinated references,” says Mahoney. “While this AI-generated one had no fake references, our team would have rejected this paper because it did have a huge percentage of references to preprints, which likely has something to do with the dataset the AI has access to.” Because preprints haven’t yet undergone peer review, IEEE Access considers heavy reliance on them a problem.

In addition, although the research-integrity checking tools didn’t spot any issues with figures, Mahoney did. One figure included a red line labeled with the nonsense term “lhewl,” and an AI “brain” in another figure had some random dots in it, which ironically were a tell that it was AI generated.

Automation Changing the Landscape

Automated research systems are very much a work in progress, but they are already starting to make waves in the scientific community. For instance, an AI Scientist academic paper and papers from the Autoscience Institute were accepted through double-blind peer review in 2025 by the International Conference on Learning Representations. As these systems improve and start to produce research outputs at volume, the waves are likely to build to a tsunami that will threaten today’s scientific and publishing process.

“FARS doesn’t kill the paper, but it does challenge the paper as the unit of knowledge,” argues Su. “We believe the future looks more like a living knowledge graph—where individual, well-scoped contributions (some made by humans, some by machines) connect and build on each other dynamically.”



In this future, humans will continue to have roles in the scientific and publication processes—just very different and limited ones. For example, instead of conducting experiments themselves, humans would sense-check and review automated output at key points. And traditional line-by-line peer-reviewing will be a thing of the past, though Su emphasizes that this change will not eliminate human judgment. “Automated systems can handle reproducibility checks, statistical validation, and novelty screening,” she says. “Humans can then focus on the harder question: Does this [research] matter?”

A Human-Centric or AI-Driven Future?

Though from a very different world than Su, Mahoney agrees that humans need to be freed to focus on the science, and that technology holds the key. “It’s promising to see the progress that has been made to automate some of the triage tasks, such as author checks, tortured phrases, duplicate submission, reference validity, et cetera,” she says. “During peer review, we’re also using AI semantic search tools to help editors match manuscripts to the most relevant reviewers, who are best placed to stop the slop before entering the scientific record.”

In addition, Mahoney thinks that AI cannot be banned or overly restricted, as this will stymie progress at a time when human knowledge could and should be growing at an unprecedented rate. “Researchers are increasingly using AI in their work, from literature discovery, to code generation, to data analysis, and this is not necessarily a bad thing,” she argues. But she disagrees that peer reviewers and humans more broadly will play a smaller part in scientific progress; Mahoney believes the future still requires humans at the wheel. The question is not just whether the research matters, she says. “The question should be: Can this work be trusted and reproduced?”

To this end, Longobardi argues that AI may ultimately require scientists to prove not just what they discovered but how they discovered it. One possibility, he says, is using technology to verify research at the source. “The system doesn’t really help because it’s not designed to not trust the paper,” he explains. “Maybe institutions can think about developing their own trust markers, automatically integrating the equipment researchers use with blockchain technology to say ‘Yes, this person was in the lab on this day and got this data.’”

Elements of this approach are already emerging. For instance, two departments within the Max Planck Institute of Colloids and Interfaces have implemented a sample database, assigning each and every sample created under their roofs with a unique ID and QR code. And the University of Southampton’s School of Chemistry and Chemical Engineering has rolled out an electronic lab notebook system. These innovative safeguards represent early efforts toward keeping humans front and center in scientific progress, and making the scientific process itself more transparent.

Whether this human-centric vision, Su’s AI-driven aspiration, or some other picture of the future wins out remains to be seen. The one thing my vibe-research adventure has clarified is that business-as-usual science is not an option. Scientists and publishers must adapt to a world in which it’s becoming increasingly difficult to spot the difference between real research and AI forgeries, and where deciding what counts as legitimate scientific work may become even harder than detecting outright fraud.

Early November is the best time for homebuyers to score a bargain in three U.S. metro areas: Milwaukee, Riverside, CA and Tampa, FL. 

In Detroit and three Florida metros–Fort Lauderdale, Miami and West Palm Beach–house hunters may benefit from holding out a little longer. Mid-December is typically the best time of year to get a deal in those places.

This is according to a Home Economics and Redfin analysis of housing market data; the analysis was originally completed in April. The analysis identifies the time of year when discounts off a home’s original asking price–through price drops and/or negotiations between buyer and seller–typically approach their seasonal peak. 

Nationwide, discounts tend to peak in early fall before plateauing or shrinking as winter approaches. But housing is local, and in these seven metros, peak discounts typically arrive later. Homebuying slows as the holidays approach, leaving sellers with fewer potential buyers–giving the buyers who remain more room to negotiate. And some late-year listings have been sitting for months, making sellers more motivated to strike a deal before the end of the year. 

“There’s a tradeoff to shopping for a home late in the year: Buyers may have fewer fresh listings to choose from, but they also face less competition, and many sellers are ready to make a deal,” said Redfin Senior Economist Asad Khan. “A seller who has been waiting months for an offer may be more willing to negotiate on price or other costs than they were in the spring. Buyers shouldn’t purchase a home just because the calendar says it’s a good time for deals, but those who find the right home may have room to negotiate.”

Florida Buyers Have More Bargaining Power Before the Snowbirds Arrive

 

In Florida, the best deals for buyers typically arrive in November or December, partly due to weather. South Florida’s biggest wave of buyers–retirees and seasonal residents–often show up after the new year, when the weather is sunny and comfortable in Florida but cold in the Northeast. 

“Local buyers have a good shot at getting a deal in November, mainly because it’s just before the snowbirds arrive,” said Todd Edwards, a Redfin Premier agent in Tampa. “House hunters have a window of opportunity to negotiate on listings that have been lingering on the market since summer: Sellers want to close a deal before the new year, and there’s often not much competition from out-of-towners yet.”

Hurricane season, which runs through early fall, also keeps some house hunters away and leaves lingering listings ripe for discounts once it passes. Additionally, Florida’s high concentration of luxury homes tend to sell below asking price, especially late in the year when sellers are eager to get those properties off their books. 

Winter Slowdown Brings Bargains to Milwaukee and Detroit

 

The story is a bit different in Milwaukee and Detroit. Those northern metros have a fairly short selling season: Cold-weather markets compress the window for touring and closing on homes; sellers keep at it later into fall, trying to close a deal before the depth of winter hits and buyers hibernate. 

That delays the usual seasonal slowdown until November in Milwaukee and December in Detroit, later than in most of the country.

Seasonal Advantage May Be Bigger Than Usual This Winter

 

The November and December discount windows in Milwaukee, Riverside, Detroit and much of Florida aren’t a one-time event; they show up as a recurring pattern each year, regardless of whether the broader market favors buyers or sellers.

But the seasonal advantage could be particularly useful to buyers this year. The U.S. housing market is already firmly tilted toward buyers, with 63% more sellers than buyers—the biggest imbalance in Redfin records dating back to 2013. A jump in listings coupled with sluggish homebuying demand has given house hunters in many parts of the country more options and more negotiating power. Miami is one of the strongest buyer’s markets in the nation, with more than twice as many sellers as buyers. Tampa, West Palm Beach, Fort Lauderdale, Riverside and Detroit are also buyer’s markets. 

Milwaukee is one of just five seller’s markets in the country. With more buyers than sellers in Milwaukee, deals aren’t commonplace–so buyers there may want to pay extra attention to seasonal patterns. 

Homebuyers Should Consider Other Factors, Too

 

The best time to buy a home is more complex than the week when buyers are most likely to score a discount.

There’s typically a tradeoff between selection and savings. New listings generally peak months before the best deals arrive. For instance, Milwaukee typically has the most new listings in mid-May and the most fresh inventory in mid-July,then its best deals arrive in early November. Detroit’s new listings typically peak in early August and fresh inventory in early September, months before its mid-December dealmaking sweet spot.

That means buyers who wait until November or December may have fewer homes to choose from. But those who prioritize price—and are flexible about their timing—may find sellers more receptive to negotiations. Ultimately, buyers should balance price, selection and their own circumstances.

Methodology 

 

To identify the best time to buy, this Home Economics and Redfin analysis focuses on the seasonal indexes for three metrics: new listings, fresh inventory, and the average discount. 

  • Most new listings: the week when the flow of new listings is greatest relative to the annual average.
  • Most  fresh inventory: the week the stock of active listings with 60 days or less on market is greatest relative to the annual average.
  • Best deals: To identify the period with the “best deals” we identify the moment when the average discount (original list price divided by final sale price) reaches an “inflection point,” meaning it slows or shrinks heading into late fall or winter. We characterize this moment as reflecting the best deals since buyers typically have ample inventory to choose from while discounts approach a local maximum.  To identify these moments, we first fit a smoothed curve to the average discount seasonal index for each region to remove week-to-week noise. Starting from the spring trough—the week when homes sell closest to (or above) their list price—we find the week of maximum slope, representing the period of fastest improvement in the average discount. We then identify the first subsequent week where the slope falls below 10% of that maximum, marking the point where average discounts have largely plateaued. For metros where the slope never drops below this threshold (i.e., discounts continue to grow aggressively through year-end), we use the week of the overall peak discount instead.

How Young People Are Researching Issues That Matter to Them

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“Adults should take students more seriously, as they have voices and different opinions too” — Middle school student

Middle school students in a California school district noticed that their perspectives were not included in decisions related to school policy that they cared about, like the dress code. They engaged in research to explore how administrators, teachers, and staff could incorporate their voices in decision-making processes. Students conducted surveys and focus groups with their peers and developed recommendations for their school, including more student-led events and a more active role engaged in school policy discussions. The following semester, the principal added students to several school leadership committees, where they helped inform programs and policies beyond the dress code, such as student social events and classroom curricular activities.

Many youth across the U.S. report feeling disengaged and disconnected from their schools, which is associated with declining attendance and greater mental health challenges. Addressing these challenges is critical, especially because a sense of belonging and connectedness predicts attendance, academic success, social well-being, self-esteem, and other positive outcomes for students.

Research is one important way to seek out solutions to these problems, and Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) is an innovative and growing approach that engages youth as experts to improve their lives, communities, and youth-serving institutions. In middle and high schools across the U.S., students in YPAR classes conduct research to inform policies, practices, and peer education efforts. They have prioritized and addressed a wide range of academic and school-climate issues, such as improving teacher-student relationships, peer relationships, safety, and school lunch options; and reducing disproportionate discipline, substance use, and transit barriers.

In schools that provide opportunities for YPAR, students are trained to take up roles as researchers, knowledge producers, and changemakers who contribute to their school communities. Further, YPAR evidence can deepen adults’ understanding of what shapes students’ feelings of belonging, patterns of engagement, and well-being. YPAR findings thus can inform more relevant and responsive solutions that can benefit youth, adults, and the broader community. This article provides guidance for educators who work with K–12 students and are interested in implementing YPAR.

The impacts of YPAR in K–12 schools

Here are three examples of how YPAR impacts different levels of the school “ecosystem.”

Systems-level: High school YPAR students in an Ohio school district explored whether their school provided adequate mental health services. They conducted a schoolwide survey, and their findings revealed that although there were mental health services available, many students were not aware of them. In response, the student researchers took action and developed more promotional materials and held informational assemblies to raise awareness of these services. They also held meetings with teachers, counselors, administrators, and the superintendent to discuss potential districtwide solutions to improve mental health services and access.

Relationships: Middle school YPAR students in a California school district identified that they didn’t feel supported by their teachers. To explore this issue further, they conducted surveys and focus groups with their peers on teacher-student relationships. Their findings offered concrete suggestions on how teachers could improve their mentorship relationships with students, including practicing active listening, showing respect and compassion, being more flexible with assignments, and incorporating more engaging classroom activities that lift up student voices. The students also recommended student-led teacher evaluations.

Student-level: High school YPAR students in a California school district conducted English and Spanish focus groups with their peers on attendance and belonging. Based on their findings, the YPAR students proposed buddy programs to connect new students with their peers, schoolwide workshops to raise awareness on racial stereotyping, and lunchtime soccer tournaments to increase a sense of belonging.

Setting the stage for YPAR at your school

Our YPAR Design Guide offers several considerations when deciding whether YPAR is a fit for your program or setting. YPAR has been primarily implemented with middle and high school students, and is well-suited to strengthening the developmental capacities of youth in early, middle, and late adolescence. However, elementary school students have also engaged in successful YPAR projects using mapping and arts-based methods.

First, adult facilitators (for example, teachers or program leaders) should assess capacity and consider whether there are adequate resources to pursue the YPAR project, including the timeframe of the project, staffing required, whether funding is needed, and the setting and scope.

Second, adult facilitators can introduce YPAR to students in a way that meets them “where they are at.” Not every student will be interested in YPAR from the start. Some may have had limited exposure to research, and others may not have had positive experiences (for example, through a particularly challenging or frustrating class assignment). Facilitators can engage the students in activities that lift up their experiences, then generate excitement by framing research as a tool for exploring and taking action on the issues that students care about.

Facilitators should also work with students to clarify their motivations for the project: What is the purpose of the project? What do students hope to get out of participating in YPAR? And what does their desired impact look like? In several projects, we have seen students become more interested in YPAR as they move through the process, analyze data, and develop their action plans and recommendations.

Finally, adult facilitators should be attentive to the broader context surrounding the topic or issue. For example, if the research is on safety and well-being, what programs or initiatives are already focusing on these issues, both in and out of school (for example, community health organizations)? What are the relevant constraints (for example, barriers accessing decision-making processes)? And where might there be opportunities to partner and collaborate? Understanding these factors will help adult facilitators and students choose an area of focus that aligns with their goals.

What YPAR might look like

YPAR is not “one size fits all.” Adult YPAR facilitators adapt YPAR across a multitude of settings, including schools, afterschool programs, and community-based organizations such as youth centers. Even within schools, a YPAR process can vary in length. For example, some YPAR projects span a single unit within a semester; others may take up an entire semester, and in some cases extend across a full academic year (fall and spring semesters).

Further, YPAR is sometimes conducted as part of a club or extracurricular activity outside of formal class time. While the structure and duration of YPAR projects vary, many projects follow these steps: building the team, identifying and defining an issue or problem, selecting a research design and methods, collecting data, analyzing and interpreting results, developing action plans and recommendations, disseminating findings, and taking action.

Throughout the YPAR process, young people serve as leaders who decide on the topic, issue, and questions they wish to investigate. Adult facilitators serve as supportive guides and mentors, training them in research methods focused on generating numerical data or text- and image-based data. Youth then go on to collect and analyze their data and develop actions plans and recommendations to address their selected problem.

Change that starts with students

A core value in YPAR is authentic power-sharing between adult facilitators and youth researchers. Most of the time, youth navigate learning spaces where they are “receivers” of knowledge from adults. Further, in traditional adult-led research projects, youth typically serve only as “respondents” to surveys or interviews, with no say in the research questions, methods, or interpretation.

The YPAR approach challenges these dynamics by positioning youth as knowledge generators who combine lived-experience expertise with systematic data-based inquiry. Youth researchers have decision-making power and are primary drivers of the YPAR process, while adults serve as guides to help them in advancing their projects.

Power-sharing is a learnable and teachable set of skills. Our guide provides lessons and activities that help facilitators attend to power dynamics, along with strategies that support power-sharing throughout YPAR. Adults and youth can productively share power in research when supportive structures are in place.

The results from YPAR research may (or may not) result in policy or practice change. Adult facilitators play a critical role in strategically connecting YPAR evidence to key decision-making spaces and helping implement youth action plans.

One step that facilitators can take is identifying existing decision-making structures where there may be opportunities to integrate YPAR evidence. They can also determine whether new routines need to be established. High School youth in Colorado investigated practices (through student surveys) that they noticed enhanced student-teacher relationships. Students then developed and led a day-long professional development for teachers on how small adjustments to the start of the year, including taking time to build trust and rapport before reviewing the syllabi, could increase student engagement and buy-in.

In our work, students have consistently emphasized how much it matters for adults to “keep them in the loop,” reporting back on whether and how their YPAR evidence was used. Authentically integrating YPAR evidence involves being transparent with students and following up with them throughout the process. Adults should take YPAR evidence seriously and actively prevent tokenizing processes—in other words, where YPAR evidence becomes a check-the-box rather than meaningful engagement. Ultimately, adults supporting YPAR must take an active role in creating conditions where youth feel safe, heard, and valued.

There is growing interest in YPAR at the local, state, and national levels, and it aligns with many initiatives that are already happening in schools. For example, the National Institutes of Health identified YPAR as a promising approach to address conditions that affect mental health and well-being, such as social isolation and exposure to unsafe environments. In California, SB997 mandates student voice be integrated in school and district budgeting processes. Oregon, Ohio, and New Jersey have similar mandates that require youth voice in decision making. There are strong synergies between YPAR and social-emotional learning programs, which are implemented in many school sites. YPAR also aligns with many academic competencies and standards, offering students a unique opportunity to build durable academic and career skills.

Students care about their schools and want to improve them. Adults need to learn along with them, listen to them, value their ideas, and support them in making change.

Industrial Intelligence: The next growth opportunity for manufacturing

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every manufacturer has its own industrial intelligence. Some is documented, and some resides in the knowledge of experienced workers.

Consider an experienced operator preparing to restart a line after a changeover. The machine settings matter, but so does knowing how a particular material behaves, what happened during the previous shift, and when an apparently minor variation deserves a closer look. A procedure can describe the sequence, but successful execution depends on the judgment that comes from experience and the organization’s accumulated know-how.

That combination is industrial intelligence: a manufacturer’s accumulated know-how, operating practices, production data, and understanding of its own business.

The opportunity now is to make more of that intelligence available wherever decisions are made, then connect it to the physical world of manufacturing. The strategic destination is not more agents, but rather to turn the existing business intelligence into an engine for profitable growth.

Individual knowledge can be translated into organizational advantage

Expertise has limited reach when it stays with one person, inside one system, or within one plant. Leaders now must determine how to extend that reach without stripping away the context that makes the knowledge useful.

For example, an engineer evaluating a process change needs more than a production record. They need to understand the circumstances behind it: the equipment configuration, the quality requirements, and the trade-offs an operator made. Connecting those elements can help people apply experience beyond the place where it originated.

When individual knowledge becomes widely accessible, innovation can spread to the maintenance workshop, the planning office, the engineering team, and the production floor. More people can contribute because more people can work with what the organization knows.

But access to knowledge is only part of the opportunity. Manufacturing ultimately is physical, and it depends on materials, machines, and products.

When intelligence participates in action

Physical AI describes systems that can perceive conditions, reason about them, and take action within the physical world. By connecting machines and processes, manufacturers get a clearer view of what is happening on the factory floor. Agents can then reason on top of that information to identify patterns, predict problems, and determine the best course of action. Physical AI brings business intelligence into the physical process, allowing machines and systems to respond to changing conditions.

Consider a robotic-handling operation encountering variation in the parts arriving at its station. Vision can help the system assess a part’s position. A model informed by the material and task can help determine an appropriate movement. Connected controls can then execute an adjustment within validated limits. The value is not merely seeing the variation; it is responding appropriately.

The same principle applies to quality operations. When Toyota Industries connected previously fragmented paint shop data, engineering and operations teams were given the context needed to understand defect drivers. The company reports that analysis cycles fell from five days to under four hours, while its pilot reduced relevant paint defects by about 25%. The breakthrough was not simply identifying another signal; it was helping people understand why defects were occurring and respond before the outcome was locked in.

This is the type of situation where industrial intelligence has become essential. Data may reveal what is happening, but industrial intelligence provides the context for what should happen, which constraints cannot be breached, and when uncertainty requires human judgment.

Simulation offers a way to examine proposed actions before applying them to equipment. Manufacturers can explore how a change might affect a process in a digital environment. That does not remove the need for physical validation, but it creates room to learn without treating the production line as the first experiment.

Responsible deployment, therefore, begins with operational discipline. Leaders need to establish clear authority or what systems may change, reliable connections to equipment, defined safety boundaries, and a record of decisions and actions. People must be able to recognize uncertainty, intervene, and bring equipment to a safe state. Trust rests on those practical arrangements, not on confidence in a model alone.

More capable machines, more capable people

Manufacturing has long combined human ingenuity with increasingly capable tools. Physical AI extends that relationship. Intelligent equipment can take on suitable repetitive or physically demanding tasks, while agent-assisted operations can help people navigate information-intensive decisions and changing conditions.

The aim is not to make machines smarter; it is to see what people can build when knowledge can travel, intelligence can act, and ambition has more room to become reality.

Rockwell Automation illustrates the point. At its Singapore plant, technicians use a maintenance copilot that combines manufacturing data, machine manuals, and knowledge captured from experienced workers. This system has reduced the time required for new workers to learn how to troubleshoot hundreds of machines from nine months to three. An engineer could explore more process alternatives before committing to a change. An operator could help improve the system’s response rather than repeatedly managing the same exception.

This type of progress requires investment in equipment but also in people: training, time to learn, and playing a meaningful role in shaping how systems work. Machines become more capable so people can become more capable.

Industrial Intelligence is the answer to sustainable growth

Industrial intelligence connects what a manufacturer knows: the experience of its people, the signals from its operations, and the lessons accumulated over time. Physical AI gives that intelligence a way to act in the physical world, turning knowledge into decisions and decisions into action.

The next challenge for industry is not simply to produce more, but to grow without adding complexity at the same rate. Intelligence can help manufacturers turn experience into scalable capability—improving productivity, accelerating innovation, expanding capacity, and enabling people to solve problems that were previously difficult to tackle.

The next frontier of industry is not smarter machines or more automated factories; it is businesses that can continuously learn, adapt, and act. The opportunity is to turn the intelligence embedded in every operation into a compounding advantage for growth.

Your AI Agents Need A Chief Operating Officer, Too

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

By Rick Folgmann

A landlord in California has 21 days after a resident moves out to return the security deposit or send an itemized list of deductions. Miss it in bad faith, and the penalty can reach twice the deposit. That deadline doesn’t care whether the letter was supposed to come from a person or a piece of software. Either way, someone will answer for it.

I think about this a lot. At my company, Get Covered, we now run 16 AI agents across more than 3 million rental units. When we built them, the technical questions turned out to be the easier ones. The harder question, for each agent, was actually who it reports to.

Rick Folgmann, COO at Get Covered.

Most companies are about to face that same question, and few are ready for it. Deloitte’s 2026 State of AI survey found that 23% of companies use agentic AI at least moderately today, and 74% expect to within two years.

Gartner, meanwhile, has predicted that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027.

Those numbers make a case for running agents the way you would run staff in a regulated job, with written rules, a defined scope, a manager and a regular review.

Here is what has worked for us, and what I would advise any decision-maker to focus on when it comes to scaling.

Start with work that already has a rulebook

The best early candidates for agents are tasks where the rules already exist somewhere outside anyone’s head: a statute, a contract clause, an insurance requirement in a vendor agreement.

Take vendor certificates of insurance. Across the industry, property teams spend roughly three hours per property every week chasing those documents by email. The work is tedious, however at the same time, it is completely specified.

Assignments like these give decision-makers two advantages. First, the agent has something objective to check its output against, and second, your team can audit the result without debating judgment calls.

McKinsey‘s most recent State of AI research found that the small group of companies seeing significant bottom-line returns from AI, about 6% of respondents, were nearly 3x as likely to have fundamentally redesigned their workflows.

In my experience, that redesign starts with writing the rule down. If a new hire couldn’t follow your description of a decision on the person’s first day, an agent won’t follow it either.

Set autonomy by workflow

Tech execs I speak with often ask whether an AI system is autonomous, as if that were a fixed property of the product. In practice, the same agent should run with different levels of freedom depending on what it touches.

At Get Covered, we configure each agent on different levels. For instance, a reminder to a vendor whose certificate expires next month can go out automatically. A response to a resident who has requested an emotional support animal never does.

A useful exercise for any leadership team is to list every workflow an agent will touch and ask one question about each: If this action is wrong, how long until we find out, and what will it have cost by then? Quick detection and low cost earn an agent more room.

Give every agent an owner with a name

Earlier in my career I learned that every automated credit decision eventually had to be explained to a customer, a regulator, or both. The model did it was never an acceptable answer.

That still holds, except agents now act more often and across more systems than any underwriting model did.

So every agent at our company has a person ultimately responsible, the same way a team or a budget does. That person reviews its error log, decides when to widen or narrow its autonomy, and answers for it.

Ownership also heads off a quieter problem. When several teams launch agents independently, nobody sees where those agents contradict one other.

Where the constraint actually sits

In today’s marketplace, it’s inevitable that the models will keep improving. A company’s clarity about who decides what will not improve on its own. Gartner’s cancellation forecast reflects that: Most agent projects that fail will do so because nobody defined the value or controlled the risk, and both of those are management problems.

For decision-makers heading into 2027, my advice is to treat each agent the way you would treat a new employee in a regulated role. Write down its rules, limit its scope, and assign it a manager who checks its work and discusses its performance.

Companies that do this will be able to give agents more responsibility over time, because they will have the evidence to justify it. Businesses that skip it will spend 2027 explaining why their software did something nobody approved.

Manhattan’s Lost Its Screen Presence

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The other weekend, I pulled up Woody Allen’s Manhattan (1979) on YouTube because I’ve been starved for the highbrow and the funny, a rare marriage that movies used to achieve with some regularity, but now hardly ever bother to attempt. 

Let’s disregard the plot, here, and the characters, for there is a great deal about both that resists easy or simple discussion in the present American climate, and focus instead on the surface: the look of the film, the feel of it, the particular New York it conjures and holds up to the light like a jeweler with a loupe.

My feeling for New York City has always come in a peculiar Southern blend of love and affliction. I don’t care for the rudeness, or the flat, clipped music of the accent, the way a sentence up there lands like a screen door.

But some years ago—high school, through college, on into my early twenties—my family made a yearly trip north around Christmas, either before the holiday or just after it. Mother, father, sister, brother, and myself. We took rooms near Times Square. We saw the shows. We ate at the restaurants with the heavy menus and the heavier check.

My mother and sister went shopping. The first year, my father, brother, and I made the mistake of going along, and we stood there in Macy’s—the famous one—waiting among the racks like the accused waiting outside a courtroom, until finally we drifted, defeated, to the section where they sell the beds.

And there, without much discussion, the three of us climbed up onto the display mattresses and took our naps in full view of the shoppers, three Southern males asleep in a snowdrift of goose-down pillows while New Yorkers and tourists alike rushed past us toward their purchases. We never went shopping with the women again.

Manhattan opens with a voice reciting its own first sentence, over and over, dissatisfied, revising itself in real time: “Chapter One. He adored New York City. He idolized it all out of proportion…” What we are watching, before a single frame of story unspools, is the composition of New York, its narration into existence by a man who admits, within the first 30 seconds, that his version of it is a romance rather than a record.

Isaac Davis, played by Allen, drafts Manhattan, discards the draft, drafts it again, each revision a further layer of gloss over a city that by the film’s own admission was never quite there to begin with. Gershwin swells. The skyline assembles itself in black-and-white, denuded of the actual chromatic clutter of a real metropolis: no exhaust-brown, no bodega-awning red, no garbage-day green.

The result is not less than New York, but more: a hyperreal locus, a city more New York than New York, purified into its own advertisement. By the time the narration settles on its final version of the sentence, we have watched a myth being built in front of us, brick by adoring brick, and we have watched it happen with the confidence of a myth that knows it will outlast its subject.

Consider, in this light, why the film insists on monochrome: color would have been the honest choice, but the lesser one. The black-and-white suggests a setting already understood as the past. Allen may have shot it that way because that’s how he remembered the city from boyhood. The image does not become nostalgia, but arrives already nostalgic, already a simulation of an image of a city, twice removed from the referent before the referent has even appeared.

The formal choices reward the kind of patient gazing that asks not what a shot means, but what it does: how it organizes the eye, how it withholds and releases. Willis’s camera holds wide and still. And it is Allen—small, voluble, anxious—who is made to cross the frame, dwarfed by the buildings on either side. The city is never made to accommodate the man. The compositions insist on this dynamic with a kind of moral clarity: architecture as fixed, permanent, indifferent; the human figure as transient, comic, and a little absurd against so much stone. There’s no accident in the fact that the funniest lines are often delivered in the widest shots: the humor is small; the buildings aren’t.

The bench scene deserves a word of correction before anything else, since even devoted admirers of the film misremember it: the silhouette behind Isaac and Mary at dawn is not the Brooklyn Bridge, but the Queensboro—the 59th Street Bridge, the one Simon and Garfunkel made feel groovy—shot from a little park at Sutton Place, the bench itself (according to my Internet searches) trucked in by the crew for that single image and trucked out again after.

Which is, in its own way, the whole method of the film in miniature: even the most iconic, most seemingly eternal image of New York on offer here was a temporary prop set down in front of a bridge for one morning’s shoot. The permanence is manufactured.

The film’s two registers—the melancholy of the story and the euphoria of the imagery—do not cancel each other out so much as run on parallel tracks, and the gap between those tracks is where the picture does its real work. Consider the function of the montage sequences against that of the dialogue scenes. The montages are non-diegetic in feeling even when diegetically grounded: they exist to establish a rhythm of civic wonder, asking nothing of the viewer but admiration.

The dialogue scenes, by contrast, are shot with almost no cutting at all: long, static two-shots, characters talking past each other in real time, the visual monotony forcing attention onto the halting, self-undermining texture of what is actually being said. The film is, formally speaking, telling us two different things simultaneously and daring us to notice the seam: the city as sublime, continuous, orchestral; the people within it as fragmented, hesitant, unable to complete a sentence about their own feelings without doubling back on it, exactly as the opening narration doubled back on itself.

The visual grandeur is never undercut by irony—Allen does not mock the skyline—but it is placed in a kind of formal tension with human smallness that neither element resolves. That unresolved tension, held shot after shot without comment, is the melancholy, and it’s built into the cutting pattern before it is ever spoken aloud.

Now, alas, to the present city—the real one—as it stands to be reorganized, and to the politics of the mayor presently promising to reorganize it.

New York has been bleeding people for years. The arithmetic of it is no longer a matter of dispute: the city and state have shed net domestic migrants every year for over a decade, hundreds of thousands of taxpayers relocating to Florida, Texas, and the Carolinas, taking with them not just households, but the tax base itself, since a disproportionate share of those leaving sit at the top of the income ledger.

Economists have written this story many times over. They generally tell it thus: rent regulation suppressing housing supply, tax rates chasing away the earners who fund the very programs meant to help everyone else, business formation slowing as regulatory and labor costs climb, the tax burden left to redistribute itself across a shrinking and less mobile base.

It is, by now, a familiar and well-documented indictment, and further democratic-socialist experimentation of the kind Zohran Mamdani proposes (price controls, municipal enterprises, steeper taxation of capital) is likely only to accelerate a trend already well underway. What economists rarely pause to mention, in this careful accounting of bad economics, is the bad aesthetics: the fact that a shrinking, flattened, price-controlled city is not only a poorer city, but a less legible one, a city that stops producing the aspirational, ambitious textures that an environment needs to be worth writing a Manhattan about in the first place.

Rent freezes, city-owned grocery stores, free buses, government-run childcare, union-built housing pinned beneath the same frozen rents, higher taxation on the wealthy who have historically financed the city’s theater of distinction: this is a program that wants New York to stop signifying, to stop chasing visual excess, to level unnecessary beauty, and to disappear the glorified aesthetics that defined Manhattan (the film).

The free market is a machine for producing urban variety and diversity. It sorts neighborhoods into strata, forces competition among restaurants and shops and doormen and galleries to distinguish themselves, and out of that sorting comes precisely the texture Isaac Davis fell in love with: the specific bookstore, the specific delicatessen, the specific bench.

Price signals are semiotic: a rent freeze not only caps a number, but also removes the mechanism by which neighborhoods differentiate themselves through investment, turnover, and risk. A city-owned grocery store cannot, by definition, be a particular grocery store as Zabar’s is particular because its entire justification is that it’s the same everywhere, fungible, a public utility rather than a character.

Today’s Manhattan looks different from Allen’s black-and-white mise-en-scène partly because of the regulations governing its buildings: facade inspection mandates have wrapped whole blocks in sidewalk sheds for years at a time, and emissions caps are pushing owners to retrofit prewar towers with electric heat pumps, through-wall units, and rooftop equipment.

Universal childcare and free buses, worthy as they may sound to the ear of compassion, are administered goods, which, besides being economically harmful to the very communities they purport to help, do not accumulate the patina of specificity, tenacity, drive: the worn brass rail, the eccentric owner, the 40 years of small adjustments to changing taste. These details turn a mere service into a grand milieu that a person can photograph in black-and-white and mean something by it. You can’t rent-control your way to a Rizzoli’s.

The specific aesthetic New York that this film so lovingly constructs was the product of an economy that permitted radical unevenness, permitted some blocks to become Sutton Place and others to remain what they were. The old New York could be ugly in reality and beautiful in art because it left room for artists to look upward, to take its disorder and inequality and noise and transfigure them into myth. The danger of Mamdani’s New York is not merely that it becomes poorer or more regulated, but that it becomes aesthetically downward-looking, a city preoccupied with administering what is wrong with it rather than generating the beauty that makes people want to imagine it as something more.

A politics organized entirely around flattening unevenness in the name of universal provision will tend to produce a city whose surfaces mean less. Mamdani’s New York, should it fully arrive, promises a setting that is more equal and more affordable and, quite possibly, less quotable, a city no future Woody Allen would find himself compelled to open his picture with “Chapter one. Bottom of Form

Which brings me back to that Christmas Macy’s, my father asleep on a showroom mattress, three Southerners playing hooky from the shopping. I think that’s what I’m partially mourning when I mourn the New York of Manhattan: not the wealth, not even the skyline, but the specificity, the sense that the city was a collection of unique rooms rather than a portfolio of interchangeable services.

The people I know who have left for Texas, for Florida, for the redder counties of this reddening country, will tell you they left the taxes, the crime, the cost. But listen long enough, and what they are really grieving, underneath the ledger, is that the New York which could still surprise you is going, has been going for a while now, and no rent freeze, however kindly meant, will bring it back, because you cannot legislate a city into being irreplaceable. You can only leave it alone long enough, and love it hard enough, to let it become so on its own, the way, once, at dawn, on a borrowed bench under a borrowed bridge, it did.
