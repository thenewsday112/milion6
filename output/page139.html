From Japan to Finland, this photographer finds a fresh perspective on architecture

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

DPReview community member Andrew Keithly photographs architecture with a Sony kit and a patient eye. What helps you see a familiar scene differently when you focus on it?

Andrew Keithly

Meet Andrew Keithly

Home base: Nashville, TN, USA

Nashville-based architectural photographer Andrew Keithly, known as AndKPhoto in the forums, got his start with a Sony A77 his grandfather gave him for his 15th birthday. Years later, an interest in drones led to real estate work, before he found architectural clients who valued his slower, more deliberate approach.

In this spotlight, Andrew shares the Sony cameras and Canon tilt-shift lenses he uses today, the great-grandfather’s Nikkormat he still takes out for street photography, and why time spent observing a building’s design can matter as much as worrying about what gear you took with.

“I’ve always loved landscape photography. Nature is a peaceful break from a chaotic world. And although I photograph it for work, really incredible architecture is genuinely fun to shoot.”

Our ‘What’s in your bag?’ community spotlight series features readers sharing their favorite gear and photography, including from past DPReview collaborators. See below for what today’s guest has to share.

For my bag, the primary consideration is weight. My big backpack can carry over 35 lb, and the Think Tank rolling bag, full of lighting gear and accessories, is a lot to handle at times. But my best work is frequently produced when I have nothing but two or three lenses, one camera, and all the time in the world. That’s it.”

How did your drone collection collide with your architecture interest?

Walt Disney Concert Hall.

Sony a7R VI with a Canon TS 17mm lens.Andrew Keithly

“My grandfather gave me my first camera for my 15th birthday. It was a Sony A77, back in the old A-mount days. He loved photography and wanted to encourage me in it. I photographed my dog, flowers, my family, landscapes and anything else that caught my eye.

In 2017, I picked up a DJI Phantom 3 Advanced, the first of many drones, and a year later I was occasionally shooting aerials for real estate agents. I’d planned a career in drone surveying and LiDAR because I didn’t think I could make a living taking photos, even though I was interested in that.

In the summer of 2020, a friend of a friend of a friend who knew I flew drones asked me to photograph a resort in the Smoky Mountains, and I realized right then this might actually be a career. I launched a real estate photography company that summer, but agents were hard to work with and weren’t willing to pay for my slower, more careful style. So I reached out to a few architects, and they preferred exactly that approach and gave me the freedom to experiment.

By January 2021, I had completely made the switch to architectural photography.”

What kind of architecture strikes you the most?

Louvre Abu Dhabi.

“Most of my real work is relatively normal architecture in the southeastern United States. But I’ve had the chance to photograph the Suiden Terrasse hotel in Japan, the Louver Abu Dhabi, and a remote house in the Finnish archipelago. I’ve also been a cinematographer in Ukraine for a humanitarian organization, and I filmed cacao fields in a Peruvian village that had never been visited by a foreigner.

I’ve traveled across the globe for work and personal projects, and Japan and Finland are my favorites. Japan’s culture is incredibly unique, with countless opportunities for street and architecture photography. Finland was happy and laid-back, and the Nordic architecture was simply stunning.

My business tagline is ‘buildings don’t travel, but photos do’.”

How has your photography changed since you started?

Suiden Terrasse.

Sony a7R VI with a Sony 24-105mm F4 lens.Andrew Keithly

“Tilt-shift lenses are extremely useful for architectural photography, and these days I own three. They let you control perspective from a fixed position. Some shots simply would be impossible without them. But I no longer see them as what defines me as an architectural photographer, because that becomes a crutch.

Sometimes my humble Sony 24-105mm lens frees me from the constraints of “making” a shot and lets me find the true vision and meaning behind the project.

When you slow down and take in the light and structure from every angle, you start to see what only you can notice.

Slowing down matters even more, which I’ve noticed several of your other spotlight guests have said. Three of the 20 images in the printed portfolio I give prospective clients came from sitting in a single spot for nearly an hour. When you slow down and take in the light and structure from every angle, you start to see what only you can notice, instead of the “good” images that every other photographer visiting the same spot will also shoot. Your own perspective can shine through, and your work starts to take on a flavor of its own.”

What suggestions do you have for others on improving their photography?

A Bedroom in Brisbane.

Sony a7R VI with a Sony 24-105mm F4 lens.Andrew Keithly

“Spend more money on travel, or on that dream class or mentorship, and less on gear. Gear is genuinely helpful, but it doesn’t make you a good photographer or bring a sense of fulfillment. And set a few flight trackers on Google Flights. You never know when you might snag a $400 flight overseas.

Even so, on the gear side, moving from an old APS-C camera to the Sony A7 III drastically improved image quality and gave me far more flexibility. The sensor in the A7 III still holds up today and reigns supreme in low light (The R3 has noise reduction on raw files).”

Court to hear argument in religious discrimination challenge brought by Catholic preschools in Colorado

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court will hear oral argument on Nov. 3 in a challenge brought by Catholic preschools in Colorado, which argue that their exclusion from Colorado’s universal preschool program is a form of religious discrimination in violation of the First Amendment, because Colorado will not provide them with an exemption from rules that would require them to admit everyone – including LGBTQ children and children with LGBTQ parents.

The Catholic preschools say that they “have no objection to Colorado’s decision to allow over 1,000 … preschools to selectively serve the communities they are designed to serve.” Instead, the preschools emphasize, they “simply want the same accommodation extended to them.”

Colorado counters that the Catholic preschools “remain welcome to participate in” the universal preschool program. However, the state writes, if they do, they must be “subject to the same equal-opportunity requirements that apply to all other publicly funded preschools—public and private, secular and religious.”

Under the universal preschool program, families in Colorado can receive up to 15 hours of free preschool per week at participating schools. To be part of the program, preschools must meet state standards in areas such as employee training; they are also required to sign a nondiscrimination agreement, in which they consent to “provide eligible children an equal opportunity to enroll and receive preschool services regardless of race, ethnicity, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity, lack of housing, income level, or disability.” Colorado does not allow them to refuse to serve LGBTQ families, because it believes such refusals would violate the nondiscrimination agreement.

The plaintiffs in the case are the Archdiocese of Denver, two Catholic parishes with preschools (one of which has since closed), and two parents of preschool-aged children. They went to federal court to challenge the state’s policy, arguing that making Catholic preschools’ participation in the universal preschool program contingent on accepting the agreement amounts to unlawful religious discrimination and that the First Amendment requires Colorado to create a religious exemption to allow them to participate in the program.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the 10th Circuit ruled for the state. It acknowledged that in a line of decisions, the Supreme Court has held that “when a state offers subsidies for private education, it cannot categorically withhold those funds from religious institutions.” In the 10th Circuit’s view, however, those cases do not apply to the preschools’ challenge because Colorado “did not exclude” religious preschools from the universal preschool program. To the contrary, the court of appeals wrote, it “welcomed and actively solicited their participation” and it is not seeking “to prohibit funds from being used for religious purposes.” “The restrictions imposed by the nondiscrimination requirement universally cover enrollment policies and conduct, but they are not a targeted burden on religious use.”

The court of appeals also rejected the challengers’ contention that the nondiscrimination requirement is unconstitutional because it burdens their right to freely exercise their religious beliefs. In the court’s view, Colorado’s conditions were neutral and generally applied to every preschool, regardless of religion – the kind of government actions, the Supreme Court held in 1990 in Employment Division v. Smith, that normally do not violate the Constitution.

Although a regulation issued by the Colorado Department of Early Childhood contains a series of preferences to facilitate the matching of families to preschools, including a “catchall” preference that allows preschools to request (and the state to grant) a preference not listed in the regulation, the court of appeals said that preference is not the kind of individualized exemption that would render the law not generally applicable, because the preschools are still required to comply with the nondiscrimination requirement.

The court of appeals also rebuffed the challengers’ argument that the nondiscrimination requirement is not generally applicable because the program permits schools to give preferences to low-income families and children with disabilities. As an initial matter, the court of appeals suggested, it was “not persuaded” that such preferences actually are exceptions to the nondiscrimination requirement because they were intended to protect such children and families and do not discriminate against wealthier families or children without disabilities. But in any event, it continued, the nondiscrimination agreement is still generally applicable because the Supreme Court’s cases only prohibit the state from treating religious activity less favorably than comparable secular activity. And the preferences for low-income families and children with disabilities are not comparable, the court of appeals concluded, because – unlike the exemption that the challengers in this case seek – they do not “undermine[] the government’s asserted interest in ensuring equal access to preschools.”

The challengers came to the Supreme Court in November 2025, asking the justices to weigh in – which they agreed in April 2026 to do.

At the Supreme Court, the justices will review two main questions. The first is the scope of the court’s 2022 decision in Carson v. Makin, in which the court struck down a Maine law that allowed public funding to be used for private schools, but not for schools that provide religious instruction.

The challengers contend that under Carson, this case should be “an uncomplicated one.” Colorado, the challengers say, “knowingly excluded Catholic preschools solely on account of their religious exercise” – specifically, “their religious practices of ensuring that all enrolling families are supportive of the Catholic Church’s teachings on sex and marriage and of treating enrolled four-year-olds in accordance with their biological sex.” That exclusion, the challengers argue, places a burden on their ability to exercise their religion, in violation of the Constitution.

Colorado counters that Carson “applied the settled principle that government may not target religion for disfavored treatment.” What the preschools are seeking instead, it says, is a “new rule that would trigger strict scrutiny whenever public-benefits conditions incidentally burden religiously motivated conduct.”

The second question before the justices centers on what a challenger has to show to prevail on a claim that a law is not generally applicable for purposes of Smith.

In Tandon v. Newsom, the challengers contend, the Supreme Court made clear that, for purposes of Smith, “[a] law is not generally applicable if it ‘treat[s] any comparable secular activity more favorably than religious exercise’” – a test that the universal preschool program fails. Colorado created the universal-preschool program to ensure equal access for all preschool-aged children, they emphasize. Because the exemptions for low-income families and children with disabilities allow preschools “to provide families with unequal access based on protected characteristics,” the challengers argue, “they undermine Colorado’s asserted interest in equal access ‘in a similar way’ to” the religious accommodation that the challengers seek. Moreover, the challengers add, the program also fails the “general applicability” test under Smith because it gives Colorado discretion, through the preference system, to allow participating preschools “to discriminate in favor of LGBTQ families and other groups,” such as “‘gender-nonconforming children,’ ‘children of color,’ or members of ‘the LGBTQ community.’”

Colorado pushes back, telling the justices that the law creating the universal preschool program “expressly prioritizes specialized services for” children from low-income families and children with disabilities, based on the state’s “longstanding awareness that ensuring equal opportunity for these children often requires providing them with additional support.” But “allowing publicly funded preschools to provide” such services does not “allow any secular conduct that undermines Colorado’s equal-opportunity interests”; therefore, it says, the law is generally applicable for purposes of Smith.

Similarly, Colorado continues, the system of preferences, used to match preschools with families, that are “unrelated to the characteristics protected by the equal-opportunity requirements, by definition, are not exceptions from those requirements.” It is clear, the state stressed, that such preferences can be implemented “only when” they “do not violate the equal-opportunity requirements.”

As No. 10 on the 2026 Builder 100 list, Century Communities reported net income of $12.6 million, or $0.44 per diluted share, in the third quarter as higher mortgage rates and economic uncertainty continued to weigh on home buyer demand, particularly among the company’s first-time buyer base.

Adjusted net income was $28.8 million, or $1.01 per diluted share, while total revenue reached $864.5 million. Home sales revenue totaled $842.5 million on 2,442 deliveries, with an average sales price of $345,000.

“The third quarter was marked by a significant rise in interest rates, continued inflation, and macroeconomic uncertainty, which weighed heavily on consumer confidence in general and first-time home buyers in particular,” executive chairman Dale Francescon said.

Despite the challenging demand environment, Francescon said the company continued to make progress operationally. Century ended the quarter with 343 communities, up 7% year over year and 4% sequentially, marking a company record.

“Our direct construction costs, finished lot costs, and fixed G&A excluding advertising all decreased versus the prior quarter as we continued to effectively manage our costs and grew our book value per share to a company record $90.61,” he said.

Century generated 2,284 net new-home contracts during the quarter and ended Sept. 30 with a backlog of 1,106 homes valued at $414 million.

Adjusted home building gross margin, excluding interest, purchase price accounting, and inventory impairment, was 19.3%. GAAP home building gross margin was 15%, including inventory impairment charges, while home building gross margin excluding inventory impairment was 17.3%.

HistoryMaker Expands Dallas-Fort Worth Footprint

Century also highlighted its early October acquisition of substantially all of the assets of HistoryMaker Homes, a Texas home builder operating exclusively in the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex.

According Francescon, the transaction will increase Century’s Dallas division from 12 to 41 active communities, based on Sept. 30 figures. On a trailing 12-month basis through the third quarter, deliveries associated with the expanded operation will increase from 211 to 669 homes.

“In early October, we acquired substantially all of the assets of HistoryMaker Homes in support of our strategy of deepening our share in existing markets to drive improved margins and returns,” Francescon said. “This acquisition will accelerate our growth in the largest housing market in the country and one we believe will benefit from solid long-term fundamentals.”

Century Updates 2026 Outlook

“Given the impact of increased mortgage rates on our orders and deliveries, we now expect our full year 2026 home deliveries to be in the range of 9,350 to 9,650 homes, including 100 deliveries from HistoryMaker in the fourth quarter, and our home sales revenues to be in the range of $3.3 billion to $3.4 billion,” chief financial officer Scott Dixon said.

Century finished the third quarter with $2.6 billion of stockholders’ equity and $863.8 million of total liquidity, including $119.8 million of cash, cash equivalents, and cash held in escrow. The company also increased its revolving credit facility to $1.2 billion and extended its maturity to November 2030. Book value per share reached a company record $90.61 as of Sept. 30.

Work boot and apparel brand BRUNT Workwear has announced a commitment to donate $1 million of work boots to trade school students nationwide in 2027, as demand for skilled workers continues to grow. The commitment expands BRUNT’s longstanding pledge to donate 100,000 pairs of boots to trade school students by 2030.

“America needs the next generation of builders, electricians, plumbers, and skilled tradespeople more than ever,” says Eric Girouard, founder and CEO of BRUNT Workwear. “These are the people who will build our homes, maintain our infrastructure, and keep this country running. We want to do our part to make sure they’re equipped to succeed. We’ve already donated approximately 25,000 pairs of boots to students across the country, and this $1 million commitment is another step toward our goal of donating 100,000 pairs by 2030. As BRUNT grows, our commitment to the trades is only going to grow with it.” 

Taking a boots-on-the-ground approach to putting the people who wear its products at the center, BRUNT often names products after real tradespeople and incorporates direct customer feedback into product development. The gear brand shares workers’ stories through its Bucket Talk podcast and brings the trade community together through weekly BRUNT Garage events.

Through the existing trade school boot donation program, BRUNT strives to help ease an upfront expense, while welcoming them into a community that understands, supports, and celebrates this workforce. 

“BRUNT Workwear’s boot donation program is truly changing the face of vocational education in Massachusetts one pair of boots at a time,” says James Hachey, director of the Center for Career & Technical Education at Somerville High School.

“Particularly for students in high-need communities, having the proper and safe work boots can mean the difference between opportunity and exclusion. Students not having the same access to required safety gear can create an immediate barrier to hands-on career and technical learning. Donations like BRUNT Workwear’s help remove that barrier, thereby ensuring all students can safely participate, build the necessary skills, and prepare themselves for lasting careers.”

Starting January 1, 2027, trade schools nationwide can find more information and apply for work boot donations at bruntworkwear.com. Selected schools will receive boots throughout 2027.

Mortgage rates are a key factor in the home-buying process, and online shopping activity suggests consumers are paying close attention to borrowing costs. With rates around 7.5%, Zonda’s Homebuyer Outlook Clicks Index, which tracks NewHomeSource online new-home shopping activity, has fallen to its lowest level since 2022.

Of course, the relationship between rates and shopping activity is not one-to-one. The job market, broader economy, stock market, geopolitical uncertainty, and consumer confidence can all influence whether prospective buyers start searching for a home. The stability of mortgage rates can matter as well.

Still, the Homebuyer Outlook data suggests 7% may represent an important psychological threshold for consumers.

When mortgage rates first moved above 7% in 2022, online shopper activity weakened noticeably. Another significant pullback occurred as rates climbed well above 7% in late 2023. The latest data shows a similar pattern, with the Clicks Index dropping in September as mortgage rates approached 7.5%.

That relationship matters because online shopping often represents an early step in the purchase funnel. Fewer consumers browsing new-home options can translate into a smaller pool of prospects moving toward community visits and eventual purchases.

Importantly, Zonda’s Homebuyer Outlook shows that the right communities can still outperform even in a challenging market. For builders, that puts the focus beyond simply generating traffic and toward understanding which buyers remain engaged, what they can afford, and how closely a community’s location, product, and price point align with those consumers.

The insights in this article were taken from more in-depth research reports published in Zonda’s Homebuyer Outlook subscription.

Since 1975, Nikon's Small World competition has featured images produced by optical microscopes, an art that usually stays in scientific papers and textbooks. But through the carefully engineered lenses of the microscope, photomicrographers sometimes capture glimpses of strikingly surreal, alien beauty. The 2026 Photomicrography Competition winners include subjects both living and mechanical, small and very small.

The fruiting bodies of a Hemitrichia slime mold remind one distinctly of Dr. Seuss. Photo: Eric Epstein/Nikon Small World

Inside the printer

The winning image shows bubbling globules of black ink being injected onto a piece of paper by an inkjet printer. Behind the lens was 25-year-old German design student Ole Bielfeldt, whose work explores the surprising complexity of everyday objects. His process is largely DIY: Bielfeldt builds his own setups, cobbles together parts, and learns techniques online.

“I just pick somewhere to start and go for it," Bielfeldt explained. "You're likely going to fail at first, and that’s what makes it even more interesting."

The experimentation and imperfection, he added, are the most interesting parts of the process and, ultimately, more important to him than achieving the highest image quality.

Photo: Ole Bielfeldt/Nikon Small World

 

Mouse parts surprisingly beautiful

Ah, mice; the humble martyrs to scientific progress. A significant amount of science seems to involve cutting up mice and learning from the result. But in this sort of photography, even the strongest mouse advocate must admit that the results are visually spectacular.

Satu Paavonsalo and Dr. Sinem Karaman of the University of Helsinki took this image of a mouse's eye while researching drug therapy. Photo: Satu Paavonsalo and Dr. Sinem Karaman/Nikon Small World

 

Third place went to another rodent part: a 5X-magnified image of neurons in a mouse's brain.

Photo: Kaelin Wulf and Rachel Flannery/Nikon Small World

 

In defense of humanity, we were also willing to subject our own body parts to photographic investigation. The 7th-place winner came from Dr. Bruno Cisterna and Dr. Eric Vitriol at the Medical College of Georgia at Augusta University. Using confocal imaging, which involves scanning a specimen to generate optical sections, they captured human neurons at 20X magnification.

These human neurons were grown from reprogrammed skin cells. Photo: Dr. Bruno Cisterna and Dr. Eric Vitriol/Nikon Small World

 

I'll end with a personal favorite of mine, John-Oliver Dum's image of a little jumping spider, dusted with pollen. Dum, who won second place, credits a childhood bite for his fascination with spiders. The pollen was cultivated by his wife, specifically to create images like this one.

You can disagree, but I think this little guy is adorable. Photo: John-Oliver Dum

As marketers chase citations and visibility in AI search, some are repeating an old SEO mistake: optimizing for search systems rather than the people who use them.

From keyword stuffing to content farms and scaled link-building schemes, SEO has a history of tactics that delivered short-term visibility but little lasting value. Today’s fixation on AI visibility risks following the same pattern.

AI search visibility matters, but it shouldn’t come at the expense of useful, original content. Google continues to emphasize creating valuable content for users and warns against tactics such as scaled content abuse.

Rather than chasing every new AI optimization hack, focus on what has always made SEO valuable: serving users better than anyone else. That’s the foundation of human-first SEO.

The role of human expertise in AI-assisted SEO

Much of the online conversation treats AI as a threat to SEO. But AI is a tool, and its value depends on how it’s used.

Research from Graphite found that 86% of high-ranking pages in Google Search were written by humans, compared with 14% generated by AI. AI-generated pages also tended to rank lower than human-authored content.

Research from iPullRank reinforces the importance of human judgment. When researchers removed evidence from real SEO scenarios, many AI models still confidently provided a diagnosis rather than acknowledging they lacked enough information. AI can accelerate workflows, but human experts remain essential for validation and decision-making.

The pages winning in search tend to include elements AI can’t independently produce:

  • Original experiences.
  • Unique industry insights.
  • Opinions and viewpoints.
  • Proprietary data.
  • Expert analysis.
  • Real use cases.

Use AI to accelerate research, prepare briefs, organize ideas, and transcribe interviews while letting human expertise provide the substance.

4 reasons why originality matters more now

The biggest risk of AI-generated content is that it becomes indistinguishable from other content. Sameness will limit your organic growth. 

1. Algorithms are built to filter sameness out

Search engines have spent years fighting low-value, repetitive content. The pace of Google’s algorithm updates this year continues to reinforce that battle. 

Google repeatedly encourages content creators and brands to create non-commodity content that adds value through firsthand experience, expert insight, and unique perspectives. If the content you’re creating can be summarized within the current top five results, you’re already losing.

2. AI systems don’t need to cite common knowledge

Some marketers assume that publishing more content increases the chances of being cited. In reality, AI systems will blend common information without citing every source. 

If your blog posts are repeating information that already exists, with no added insight or perspective, what incentive is there for an AI system to reference it? Or include it within its training data?

Originality will increase your content’s citation potential. Repetition will do the opposite. 

3. Humans reward differentiation 

Even in an AI-first world, humans are still making buying decisions. Searchers click when they want more depth or proof, whether it’s on ChatGPT or a Google AI Overview. 

Some may disagree with me, but I don’t believe the click economy is disappearing. I think it’s changing. AI can answer straightforward questions exceptionally well, so yes, you may lose the click on those queries. But when searchers are closer to a decision, they want context, examples, and real-world application, and that’s when they click through to seek human expertise. 

Differentiation creates curiosity. Curiosity creates the click.

4. Consumers are growing skeptical of generic AI

According to Gartner, 65% of consumers believe brands produce too much AI-generated content in their marketing materials. Even more alarming, 57% say this content has made them less trusting of brand messaging overall.

When every article sounds the same and every social post follows a similar pattern, consumer trust erodes. Today’s consumers don’t want a perfectly crafted message. They want evidence, proof, and the confidence that a real person is standing behind the information. Research from the Harris Poll, presented by Quad, shows that 80% of Americans say brands that feel human and imperfect stick with them more than AI-generated perfection.

Consumers increasingly assume content is AI-generated until proven otherwise. That means that authenticity has become a real differentiator. 

So, how can we prevent repeating the same patterns? 

Introducing human-first SEO

The future of SEO is human-first. It’s what SEO should be and always has been. 

Early SEO saw the listicle craze, with authors listing their own brands as No. 1 or No. 2. Then search engines evolved, and it no longer worked. This story is repeating itself with AI search. Anthropic’s usage policy already includes policies against deceptive campaigns and artificial activity.  

Stop chasing every “new” GEO/AEO/AIO hack. History has shown brands and marketers that these may provide a short-term boost, but in the long run, they can hurt both your visibility and your brand’s reputation. 

Instead, go back to tried-and-true, human-first SEO built upon these four pillars. 

  • Experience: Provide firsthand knowledge that AI models cannot manufacture. Share your brand’s lessons learned, implementation challenges, successes, and real-world observations.
  • Opinion: Take a position. Not every piece of content needs to be controversial, but valuable content usually requires interpretation. Readers want insights that explain what happened and why it matters. 
  • Proof: Support your claims with original data, whether that’s customer stories, internal research, experiments, or other evidence. 
  • Identity: Real people build trust. Showcase your experts. Strong bios, professional histories, research, speaking engagements, and community involvement all create authority signals that neither machines nor users can easily ignore. 

Interestingly, these four pillars mirror what Google has consistently encouraged for years and now includes in its AI optimization guide. 

Human-first SEO use cases

Replace scaled AI content with expert content

Although it’s tempting, especially when you see it working for others, remember that using AI to produce hundreds of pages modeled after a competitor or other source will likely deliver short-lived results and hurt you in the long term. Why? Because competitors and other sites can create the same content just as easily. 

Meanwhile, marketing teams that invest in expert reviews, SME insights, and unique data earn visibility and keep it.

Unlock your $1M+ organic growth engine.

Everything enterprise teams need to grow visibility across search and AI.

One of my B2B supplier clients and I watched a competitor publish more than 300 AI-generated how-to guides over a few months. As many of us have seen, this resulted in increased visibility for some time.

Citations began to decline, rankings dropped, and organic visibility started to disappear. Within six months, this competitor had seen its strategy rise and fall. To stop the bleeding, this competitor removed all AI-generated content. 

Instead of following their lead, our SEO strategies held their course, with blog posts that spoke directly to customers’ common issues and questions and by incorporating their team into the discovery process.

Rewrite generic manufacturer product descriptions

Many ecommerce sites still rely too heavily on descriptions provided by the manufacturers. Even when those descriptions are well written, how do you stand out if other sellers are using the same copy? 

Fortunately, human-first content can solve this problem. Add any of the following to help your product pages stand out from the rest.

  • Actual customer questions and answers.
  • Use cases.
  • Results from your own product testing.
  • Comparisons.
  • Real customer stories and reviews.

These elements and others like them provide value that generic product descriptions cannot.

Reframe your ‘how-to’ and ‘what is’ content

SaaS companies are frequent offenders of publishing large volumes of “what is” and “how-to” content that is similar to many others. The real content opportunity lies within their product teams, implementation specialists, customer success managers, and other internal teams.

Instead of a how-to article, develop content based on insights from your development and implementation teams, including common challenges they’ve faced, issues they’ve worked through with customers, and integrations that work best. Those types of insights are difficult to replicate and highly valuable to your buyers.

Each of these examples comes back to the same question: What can you say that no one else can? The checklist below can help you answer that. 

The human-first SEO checklist

Use these strategies to shift your SEO program toward a human-first approach. 

  • Run a content quality review. Ask yourself if AI could create the same content from current top-ranking pages. If yes, then you’re at risk of not appearing.
  • Create an expert content workflow. Use AI for transcription, structure, and outlines, but source the substance from your subject matter experts. 
  • Build a presence beyond your website. You and your team must be visible outside of your website to earn trust. 
  • Adjust your KPIs. Focus on share of voice, branded search, and citations. The attribution funnel has changed, and traffic source and rankings can’t be your priority.
  • Make your SMEs visible through bylines, bios, LinkedIn activity, podcasts, guest writing, conferences, and more. All of these provide authority and trust signals for your brand.
  • Invest in original data, whether that be surveys you conduct or internal datasets you compile. 

The new competitive advantage: Being human

The more AI-generated content that exists, the more valuable human expertise becomes. When everyone can create content faster, speed ceases to be a competitive advantage. 

SEO has never been about who can publish the most content. Success belongs to the brands whose content contributes something others can’t. 

  • Real experience.
  • Real opinions.
  • Real proof.
  • Real people.

Search engines are rewarding these. AI systems rely on them. Users are actively looking for them. The irony of SEO is that the more advanced AI platforms become, the more important human-first thinking is. 

That’s why SEO success shouldn’t be defined by who uses AI the most. It should be defined by who stays unmistakably human.

What happens when your company is acquired by Salesforce? Johnny Rodgers, the former principal engineer at Slack, says Slack and Salesforce had such different ideas about how work should be done that it felt like being lost in an enterprise process manual.

  1. Slack: “Put it in a channel.” Salesforce: “Open a ticket.”
  2. Slack: “Someone will jump in and fix it.” Salesforce: “Someone will route it to someone who may know someone.”
  3. Slack treated friction like a bug. Salesforce treated it like a workflow.
  4. Slack wanted work to feel less bureaucratic. Salesforce had enough bureaucracy to require its own onboarding.
  5. Slack sold a new way of working. Salesforce sold into functions that already had budgets, org charts, and opinions.
  6. Slack needed broad adoption across a company. Salesforce reps preferred products they already knew how to sell.
  7. Slack assumed the seller used the product. Plenty of Salesforce reps apparently treated Slack as a place to send DMs.
  8. Slack culture said, “Change how the company works.” Salesforce culture said, “Which business unit owns this?”
  9. Slack expected the acquisition to change both companies. Salesforce had 80,000 employees and other ideas.
  10. Slack CEO Stewart Butterfield: If Salesforce changes Slack and Slack doesn’t change Salesforce, congratulations, you didn’t integrate a company. You digested it.
