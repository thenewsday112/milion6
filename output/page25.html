Exploring the forest of ecologicstudio

 

In 2025, a forest emerged in the Triennale Design Museum in Milan. It was DeepForest³, a project by the London-based design studio ecoLogicStudio, co-founded by Claudia Pasquero and Marco Poletto. It was presented as part of the exhibition We the Bacteria: Notes Toward Biotic Architecture, curated by Beatriz Colomina and Mark Wigley, who authored the book of the same title. While the installation looked as though a few saplings had risen from the shiny museum floors, what was on display was, in fact, a hypothesis about how domestic space could be transformed by processes inherent to nature, with Poletto describing it as a system constructed with a ‘fully functional and tangible biotechnological living system, grounded in the metabolic cycles of algae and fungi.’

 

Seeing the deep green cyanobacteria and the lichen growing from a cork bench felt like following a fungi-covered path back through the London-based studio’s projects. Since its inception in 2005 , the duo has proposed a built world that leverages the powers of nature – algae air-purifying mechanisms, waste-digesting mushrooms, and insulation that grows itself – to develop a style of architecture that grows with the environment instead of against it.

DeepForest³, a project by the London-based design studio ecoLogicStudio, presented as part of the exhibition We the Bacteria: Notes Toward Biotic Architecture, curated by Beatriz Colomina and Mark Wigley | image © Synthetic Landscape Lab

 

 

The Urban Algae Folly

 

Nearly ten years earlier, at EXPO Milano 2015 Future Food District, a pavilion of triangular panels glowing with a flow of green algae was erected in the event’s central square. Called The Urban Algae Folly, the canopy provided shade through the growth of Spirulina, a type of microalgae that is highly nutritious for people while also being an effective CO₂-capturing organism. Here, ecoLogicStudio created a symbiosis between the sun, the algae, and humans. On sunny days, more algae grows in the membrane of the canopy. When a person stands under it to take advantage of its shade, the CO₂ they release continues to feed the system. The project is born from the idea that architecture should work with microbial life rather than attempt to exclude it. Here, algae become active collaborators in shaping the environment, turning a pavilion into a living, carbon-consuming system.

The AirBubble in Nyon

 

In Nyon, Switzerland, the studio developed what they call ‘the world’s first biotechnological healthcare garden that combines air-purifying algae cultures with medicinal plants.’ Here, an outdoor space, a kind of circular, tent-like structure made of semi-transparent fabric, provides a respite for its inhabitants. Inside, the circumference is lined with ‘36 large bioreactors in borosilicate glass which contain 350 litres of living green Chlorella algae cultures that can filter a flow of polluted air of 150 litres/minute.’ The project challenges the assumption that healthy spaces must be sterile. Instead, it positions living organisms as essential infrastructure, using algae and plants to create an environment where ecological and human health are interconnected.

CryoflorE at MUDAC’s Solar Biennale

 

In collaboration with the Synthetic Landscape Lab at the University of Innsbruck, the studio also produced CryoflorE: a model for cyber gardening the city, which expanded this thinking beyond a singular structure and into the urban realm. As part of the MUDAC’s 2025 Solar Biennial, Soleil·s, the project takes the form of a near-hexagonal model resembling a miniature cityscape of high-rises filled with cyanobacteria.

 

Here, they ‘capture and channel electrons generated through photosynthesis into electrical currents flowing along the copper wires embedded in the streetscape.’ Using the energy-producing and CO₂-capturing potential of cyanobacteria, the studio takes initial steps towards developing a simulation of a city powered by bio-solar energy networks.

Constructing DeepForest³

 

In creating DeepForest³, Pasquero remarked, ‘We are now more and more aware that our own nature is cyborgian and collective, and that our own identities extend far beyond the limits of our bodies. We are microbial ecosystems, we are algorithmic networks. It is a necessary consequence that our home becomes an extension of these ecosystems and networks. Our home is our microbiome.’

 

The installation itself, on the sun-filled upper floor of the Milan design museum, looks like an abstraction of a forest. There are no leaves, dirt, or moss. Instead, there is a careful arrangement of what look like birch trunks, stripped of their twigs and standing upright on a textured floor. These are what ecoLogicStudio calls ‘biodegraders, built from 3D-printed bark-like shells made of algae biopolymers, [which] host living mycelium networks.’ They get their nutrition from coffee grounds and develop a layer of sinewy material that acts as a kind of insulation.

 

Between these structures are a series of glass containers holding ‘photosynthesizers, 50 liters of living cyanobacteria, actively capture CO₂ from the gallery environment,’ which serve as living, breathing filters. Lastly, there are ‘carbon storers,’ which digest waste material and give life to living lichen. Among these elements are the tubes, seams, and cords that make this environment possible. In this installation, ecoLogicStudio proposes not a traditional space for living, but a space that lives, grows, digests, and filters. It draws on the logic of the forest to propose an alternative way of living, in which human technologies take a back seat to organic processes.

Colomina, Wigley, and We the Bacteria

 

DeepForest³ serves as a case study for the ideas presented in We the Bacteria. Written by Colomina and Wigley and published by Lars Müller Publishers, the book acts as a sequel to their previous book, Are We Human? In We the Bacteria, the authors examine the sterility of contemporary architecture – its impulse to kill bacteria and what we interpret as germs and grime – and reposition this hostility as a threat to both human and planetary health. Within the text, they write, ‘this book is covered in microbes,’ offering preliminary evidence of how pervasive single-celled organisms are. The authors use evidence of disease in industrialized, developed spaces to encourage a return to the microbial, advocating forms of design that embrace environments in which bacteria can thrive.

DeepForest³ serves as a case study for the ideas presented in We the Bacteria | image © Synthetic Landscape Lab cover of We the Bacteria by Beatriz Colomina and Mark Wigley | image courtesy of Lars Müller Publishers

 

 

We the Algae, Fungi, and Carbon as Well

 

Although We the Bacteria centers on the microbial world, it offers an equally useful framework for understanding ecoLogicStudio’s wider practice. Across these projects, algae, fungi, and carbon become active agents in architecture, challenging the separation between the built environment and the living world.

 

Together, these projects suggest that architecture can do more than accommodate human needs; it can actively support the organisms and ecological processes with which we share the planet. When done intentionally and carefully, these decisions can result in net-positive outcomes for human life as well. In these spaces, bacteria, fungi, and other organisms are not contaminants to be eliminated or resources to be exploited, but participants in the environments we inhabit. The home and the building become parts of a wider living system, proposing a form of mutualism in which human well-being is inseparable from the health of the more-than-human world. Rather than building against the environment, ecoLogicStudio proposes an architecture that works with it, using the capacities of living organisms to rethink how we construct our homes, buildings, and cities.

From Bug Report to Production: How I Fix Bugs with AI

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Hi! I’m Tim, QA Engineer at inDrive. Some time ago, when I was updating tests for new endpoints, one of them behaved strangely. The service rejected a request, but on the next attempt it said the action had already been completed. In fact, nothing had happened.

The problem was with an idempotency key: the service reserved it before validating the request. If validation failed, the key remained reserved anyway. I prepared a fix, went through code review, and shipped the change. After the release, duplicate-key errors on the affected endpoints dropped by about 30%.

Previously, my work on a bug usually ended with a detailed report and retesting someone else's fix. Now, with Claude, I can go through the whole process by myself: understand unfamiliar code, check a hypothesis against logs, prepare a patch and tests, submit the review change, and take it through to release. Today, I independently ship an average of three to five small fixes and improvements a week.

I distinguish three levels of this approach based on my experience at inDrive, which I'll describe below. It's important to note that this is not a mandatory career path for every QA engineer, nor does it mean handing responsibility over to an agent. In this approach, AI helps investigate systems faster, but the engineer still checks the facts, assesses the risks, and takes responsibility for the result.

Level 1. A Bug Report That Lets a Developer Start Fixing Right Away

A typical bug report answers the question, “What is broken?” But the developer still has to find the cause, understand the related parts of the code, and choose a solution. To save developers time, I started adding the results of that investigation to my bug reports.

First, I ask an AI agent to trace the flow of data and find a possible cause. Then I check every conclusion in the repository: whether that piece of code actually runs in the scenario, how similar cases are handled elsewhere, and what other parts of the system the patch might affect. If a hypothesis isn't confirmed, I don't present it as a fact.

As a result, each ticket includes six sections:

  1. The problem and the conditions that trigger it;
  2. A specific proposed fix;
  3. Acceptance criteria and a regression test;
  4. Risks and solutions that look plausible at first but are actually wrong;
  5. Impact on users;
  6. References to files, lines of code, and related tickets.

This kind of report doesn't guarantee that the proposed solution is correct. But it gives the developer a verified starting point instead of a task that still needs to be investigated from scratch.

Example: Passenger Requests That Never Reached the Driver

The inDrive order service has separate ride options for an assistive device, a service animal, and a wheelchair-accessible car. When the service built its response, all three values were mistakenly read from the same field: assistiveDevice.

As a result, the driver might not see a request for an accessible car if the passenger hadn't selected an assistive device. And when the device option was selected, its description appeared under the other options as well. No tests covered this case.

Before the fix:

response.assistiveDevice = describe(order.assistiveDevice) response.serviceAnimals = describe(order.assistiveDevice) response.wheelchairCar = describe(order.assistiveDevice)

After the fix:

response.assistiveDevice = describe(order.assistiveDevice) response.serviceAnimals = describe(order.serviceAnimals) response.wheelchairCar = describe(order.wheelchairCar)

The cause was two copy-and-paste mistakes. In the ticket, I pointed to the exact location and specified the necessary changes and checks. The first available developer quickly made the fix, and the ticket was closed a couple of days later.

What matters here is not only the size of the patch but also the consequences of the bug: because of two incorrect field references, passengers who needed special ride arrangements might not get the service they required. That’s frustrating, and it had to be fixed fast.

What to Try in Your Team

Take a bug you can already reproduce:

  1. Ask the agent to trace the flow of data from the incoming request to the system's response.
  2. Ask it to separate conclusions confirmed by the code from hypotheses, and to provide references to specific files and lines.
  3. Check those findings yourself.
  4. Then add the smallest possible fix, potential side effects, and a test that should stop failing after the patch to the ticket.

In another investigation, the agent helped find an endless recursive call in an error handler: for an unknown error, the function called itself with the same arguments. This could cause a stack overflow and crash the process. I explicitly noted in the ticket that the defect had been found by reading the code, not reproduced on a running instance. It's essential to preserve that distinction.

Level 2. Prioritize Bugs Using Production Data

Even when you've found the cause, a question remains: how serious is the problem? How many requests or inDrive users does it affect? Can you confirm it not just from the code but also from how the system behaves?

For this, I analyze logs and metrics. The agent helps identify which events the relevant code writes, build queries, collect results across clusters, and group errors by cause. Where access to logging and metrics tools is available, MCP connectors are a convenient option.

But the results still need to be checked: the wrong filter or denominator can easily turn a convincing number into a false conclusion.

Example: Nearly 1.8 Million False Server Errors in One Week

While analyzing logs from the inDrive order service, I found a large number of 500 errors occurring during calls to the bidding service. To understand the causes, I used AI to build log queries and collect one week of statistics across 13 clusters.

In total, I found about 1.86 million such errors. Preliminary analysis showed that roughly 96.6% of them—about 1.79 million—were caused by clients cancelling requests.

The cause was in how errors were handled between services.

When a client closed the connection during a call to the bidding service, that service returned gRPC Canceled. The order service converted it into a generic 500 server error and wrote it to the logs.

Yet other parts of the service already handled these situations correctly: if the client closed the connection, the server did not try to send a response or log an error.

I checked whether these cancellations were caused by slow responses from the bidding service. They weren't: calls completed in milliseconds. For example, the median duration of an unsuccessful bid retrieval call was 7.5 ms, compared with 5.6 ms for a successful one.

In other words, slow responses were not the problem. The client had already stopped waiting, while the server continued to count the cancelled request as its own failure.

As a result, monitoring showed errors where the server had not actually failed. Including related scenarios, client cancellations generated several million ERROR-level log entries in a week. This noise made it harder to distinguish real failures from expected system behavior.

Based on the investigation, I proposed changing the error handling:

  1. If the client cancels a request during a call to the bidding service, don't return a 500 error or write an ERROR log entry.
  2. If the cancellation originates in the bidding service itself while the client is still waiting for a response, keep treating it as a server error.
  3. Check how the change affects event consumers, and notify the owners of dashboards and alerts about the expected drop in error counts.

According to a preliminary estimate, this change could eliminate about 96% of the 500 errors in question. That's the expected effect calculated from logs, not the result of a deployed fix. The findings also need to be reviewed with the bidding team.

This example shows why QA engineers should analyze production data. A high number of errors in monitoring doesn't necessarily mean that users experienced the same number of failures. Before assigning priority and proposing a fix, you need to understand what exactly the system measures and how it classifies errors.

Another Example: Errors When Changing a Ride's Status

While analyzing another scenario, I found that about 3% of requests to the ride-status transition endpoint failed over the course of a day. The logs showed that 90% of those errors occurred when the driver requested a status the ride was already in. And 55% of the errors involved a transition to “completed” for a ride that was already completed.

Timing data and traces pointed to repeated taps, stale screen state, and bursts of requests from an older ride screen. The next day, I repeated the analysis and got the same 90%.

We split the work into two tasks:

  1. Return success if the ride is already in the requested status.
  2. Add logging where we still lacked data—on the passenger side.

How to Verify an Agent's Findings

Before putting metrics in a ticket, I check the time period the data covers, which clusters are included, what counts as an error, and what denominator is used to calculate each percentage. Then I check whether a spike can be explained by one instance or a rare scenario and, where possible, repeat the analysis over a different period.

It's also important to distinguish three things:

  1. An observation from the logs;
  2. A hypothesis about the cause;
  3. The expected effect of a fix.

They shouldn't be presented together as one proven fact.

Level 3. Prepare a Fix, Pass Code Review, and Ship It

When the cause is clear, the risks are limited, and the change is small enough, a QA engineer can prepare the patch by himself instead of handing the task to a developer. Although AI helps write code and tests, this in no way means that a patch can be shipped without the usual engineering checks.

At inDrive, I started with simple changes reviewed by developers. Over time, I began taking on more substantial tasks. Now my process includes code analysis, testing, code review, the standard release process, and verifying the impact after release.

Example: An Idempotency Key Was Reserved Too Early

Let's return to the bug from the beginning of the article. Some endpoints reserved the request key before validating its contents. If validation failed or subsequent processing ended unsuccessfully, the key stayed reserved. The user's next attempt received a response saying an operation had been repeated, even though it hadn't happened in the first place.

In simplified form, the original sequence looked like this:

reserve(key)          // the key is reserved before validation validate(request) process(request)

After the fix:

validate(request) reserve(key)          // only a valid request reserves the key try:     process(request) onFailure:     release(key)      // the request can be retried after failure

For an operation that really has already completed, the conflict response remains. But the response now also includes an explicit reason that can be tracked in monitoring.

After the release, I checked the metrics. Duplicate-key errors on these endpoints were down by about 30%; the decrease held every day after the release and across every cluster with traffic. Median response time, the 99th percentile, and other errors showed no noticeable change. The additional load on the shared cache—about 0.01%—had been assessed before the merge and considered acceptable.

That result matters more than the fact that AI helped write a few lines of code: the fix reached production, and we could verify its expected effect.

A More Complex Example: A Bug in a Shared Authentication Library

One service was crashing at startup with an unclear error. At first, it looked like a broken response parser. By tracing execution with AI, I followed the issue beyond our service into the shared authentication library. When the auth service returned a plain-text error page, the client tried to parse it as JSON and lost the actual response status code.

While investigating the source of that error further, I found a more serious problem: a background job that was supposed to refresh keys regularly ran only once and then stopped. After the next key rotation, services using an older version of the library could keep using stale keys, causing token validation to fail.

We reproduced the scenario. The fix was reviewed by the owners of the shared library, and then a new version was released. This case shows why it's important to investigate a root cause all the way through, including beyond the service where the error first appeared.

What I Don't Delegate to AI

The agent is good at reading unfamiliar code, tracing connections between services, and drafting log queries, patches, and tests. But it can also confidently explain a cause that doesn't actually exist.

That's why I keep four decisions for myself:

  1. What has been confirmed by evidence;
  2. How broad the change should be;
  3. Which tests are needed;
  4. Whether the patch is ready to ship and what to check after release.

I also follow specific rules:

  • In each ticket, I state what was reproduced and what was found only by reading code.
  • If a conclusion turns out to be wrong, I add an explicit correction to the ticket history rather than silently replacing the text.
  • Before merging, I measure the load on shared systems.
  • I run automated tests in a test environment and separately note flaky tests and tests that were already failing.
  • I don't turn a small patch into a large refactor.
  • I send changes to shared code to the owners of dependent components.

These aren't special rules for AI. They're ordinary engineering discipline, and they become essential when a QA engineer at inDrive starts changing production code.

Where to Start

  1. You don't need a big task for your first experiment. Choose a bug you filed this week and try only Level 1: use an agent to trace the implementation flow, verify the likely cause, and add code references, a proposed fix, risks, and acceptance criteria to the bug report.
  2. Once you can verify the findings, add Level 2: take one metric from logs or monitoring and use it to confirm the scope of the problem. Don't put a percentage in the ticket until you've checked exactly what it measures.
  3. Move to Level 3 with a small, straightforward change—for example, removing a feature toggle that has been fully rolled out and is no longer needed. Prepare the patch and tests, pass code review by the code owners, and follow the usual release process. After release, make sure the system behaves as expected.

For me, the biggest change in QA work isn't that an agent can write code. It’s time to make Shift happen.

Game Off, GitHub’s annual month-long game jam, returns for its 14th year this November. Thousands of developers have joined us over the years to make everything from polished platformers to wonderfully weird experiments.

Whether you’re making your first game or your fiftieth, we’d love for you to join us.

The theme for Game Off 2026 is SHIFT!

Shift gears. Shift perspectives. Shift dimensions. Shift bits. Work the night shift. Or make a game where the only buttons are the Shift keys.

How you interpret the theme is entirely up to you.

You have until December 1, 2026, at 13:37 PST to build and submit a game inspired by SHIFT. Strong or loose inspirations apply!

Join Game Off 2026 on itch.io

shift /ʃɪft/

Noun

  • A change in position, direction, perspective, or reality.
  • A period of scheduled work, usually when something breaks.
  • A keyboard key used to capitalize letters, sprint in games, and accidentally enable sticky keys.
  • In computing, an operation that moves bits left or right, because apparently bits need exercise too.

Verb

  • To change gears, direction, or the laws of physics.
  • To move between worlds, dimensions, timelines, or questionable life choices.
  • To transform into something (or someone) entirely different.
  • To pass responsibility to somebody else, especially when the production build breaks.

Acronyms (that we definitely just made up)

Okay, we made these up:

  • Space Hamsters Initiating Faster Travel
  • Shapeshifters Hiding In Forgotten Temples
  • Suspicious Humans Investigating Feral Toasters
  • Skeletons Hastily Improvising Functional Transportation

Or invent your own. We love seeing where you take the theme.

Need inspiration? Here are a few concept ideas

  • A one-button (or two-button) game where the Shift keys are is your only control.
  • A racing game where shifting gears changes more than your speed. Second gear reverses gravity, third slows time, and fourth removes the brakes.
  • A perspective puzzler where rotating the camera turns walls into floors and shifts shadows into platforms.
  • A puzzle-platformer where you shift between two overlapping realities. That broken bridge might exist in the other world. Unfortunately, so might the monster standing on it.
  • A shapeshifting adventure where you transform into creatures and objects, inheriting their abilities and weaknesses.
  • A night-shift horror game set in a convenience store where the customers get progressively stranger, and whatever’s in aisle seven definitely isn’t covered by your training.
  • A comedy detective game where you committed the crime and must shift the blame onto increasingly unlikely suspects without contradicting the evidence.
  • A programming puzzler: Use bitwise shifts to manipulate the world. Shift left to double an object’s size, shift right to halve it, and discover what happens when you run out of bits.
  • An adventure game about convincing somebody to cover your shift so you can attend the final boss battle.

Of course, you could also build a game about shifting tectonic plates, shape-shifting aliens, shifting alliances, shifting sands, or a particularly ambitious stick shift.

Don’t overthink it. Pick an interpretation that interests you and see where it takes you.

Pro tip: Start with one mechanic and one playable level. Get those working before adding the multiverse. Future you (and your bug tracker) will thank you.

Need another perspective? Try brainstorming with GitHub Copilot. Give it the theme, a few constraints, and see what it comes up with:

Act as an indie game designer. Suggest 10 original game concepts inspired by SHIFT, each with a unique core mechanic, genre, and technical twist. Prioritize ideas a solo developer could prototype in a weekend.

How to participate

Work alone or form a team. Use whichever programming languages, game engines, frameworks, libraries, and tools make sense for your game.

  1. Create a free GitHub account if you don’t already have one.
  2. Join the jam. Head to the Game Off 2026 page on itch.io. If you don’t have an itch.io account, you can sign in with your GitHub account.
  3. Create a public repository. Store your game’s source code on GitHub. Push your game before December 1, 2026, at 13:37 PST.
  4. Submit your game on itch.io before December 1, 2026, at 13:37 PST. Pushing your code to GitHub does not submit your entry to the jam. After submissions close, you’ll be able to play other entries and cast your votes.

Voting

Here’s the fun part: you decide who wins.

Once submissions close, it’s your turn to play and rate the games your fellow developers have created. Every vote helps determine which games take home the bragging rights.

Entries will be evaluated in the following categories:

  • Overall
  • Gameplay
  • Graphics
  • Audio
  • Innovation
  • Theme interpretation

Voting will end on January 1, 2027, at 13:37 PST. Winners will be announced on the GitHub Blog and GitHub social channels shortly thereafter.

Light rules

Game Off is intentionally relaxed, but there are a few guidelines to help keep it fair and fun:

  • Your game must live in a GitHub repository. You should begin the project during the jam, although templates and starter projects are welcome. The vast majority of the work should take place during the Game Off submission period.
  • License it however you like. Open source is encouraged but not required.
  • Fly solo or join a team. Work in whichever way makes you most comfortable.
  • Use any tools and assets you prefer. Open source, commercial, commissioned, and original assets are all welcome.
  • AI-assisted development is allowed. We recognize that AI use in game development is a sensitive subject, and participants have different perspectives. When submitting your game, you’ll be asked about your use of AI so others can better understand how it was made.

That’s it. Keep it creative, respectful, and fun… and make sure your final code is pushed before the deadline.

Help

New to game development?

You don’t need to be an expert to participate. Many Game Off participants create their first game during the jam.

Some use established engines. Some build their own. Others make games for old consoles, unusual hardware, terminals, calculators, or platforms we assumed had been lost to time.

However you build your game, there’s no single correct way to play.

Here are a few engines and frameworks you might want to explore:

  • Godot (GDScript, C#, C++): Open source, lightweight, beginner-friendly, and excellent for both 2D and 3D games.
  • Unity (C#): A popular option for 2D, 3D, mobile, and XR games, with a large ecosystem of tutorials and assets.
  • Unreal Engine (C++, Blueprints): A strong choice for cinematic visuals, complex 3D environments, and high-end experiences.
  • Phaser (JavaScript, TypeScript): Great for browser-based 2D games, arcade games, and platformers.
  • Pygame (Python): A friendly way to learn game development fundamentals or prototype an idea quickly.
  • Bevy (Rust): A modern, data-driven engine built around an entity component system.
  • LÖVE (Lua): Lightweight, fast, and well suited to 2D games and creative experiments.
  • Flame (Dart, Flutter): A useful option for developers interested in mobile-first 2D games.
  • Ebitengine (Go): A straightforward, cross-platform 2D game engine for Go developers.
  • Defold (Lua): A compact, cross-platform engine with built-in development tools.
  • libGDX (Java): A familiar option for developers coming from Java or Android development.
  • HaxeFlixel (Haxe): A good fit for retro-inspired 2D games, platformers, and fast-paced jam projects.
  • raylib (C, plus bindings for other languages): A simple, code-first library for developers who want to work closer to the metal.
  • PICO-8 (Lua): A fantasy console with deliberate constraints, ideal for tiny games with distinctive pixel-art aesthetics.
  • TIC-80 (multiple languages): Another fantasy computer for making, playing, and sharing small games.

Looking for teammates or a place to share progress? The Game Off 2026 Community is a great place to ask questions, share progress, and look for teammates. There’s also a friendly community-run Discord server.

New to Git or GitHub?

Game Off is the perfect opportunity to check it out. Version control pun fully intended.

  • Git documentation: Learn the fundamentals of version control and get started with Git.
  • GitHub Docs: Explore tutorials, guides, and documentation for building and collaborating on GitHub.
  • GitHub Community: Ask questions, share your work, and connect with other developers.

Whether you spend November shifting pixels, shifting bits, shifting timelines, or shifting unfinished tasks into the final 24 hours, you’ll be part of a global community making games together.

So take a different perspective, change the rules, and see what happens when you press ⇧.

Good luck and have fun! Can’t wait to play, err, test all your games! <3
