The 10 best places to visit in November 2026

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

November is peak shoulder season: The weather can be unpredictable (but you can get really lucky), crowds are small nearly everywhere and prices are often the lowest they'll be all year for many destinations. It is an ideal time to travel in many ways, if you're willing to risk less-than-ideal weather in some places.

Thanksgiving also takes place in the U.S., and if you are not obligated to visit family, you can enjoy a wonderful four-day vacation without missing any school or work. And for those who love Christmas markets and lights, several spots start their displays at the end of the month.

Here are 10 great destinations to consider for your November getaway.

Marrakech and the Atlas Mountains, Morocco

MICHAEL HANSON/GETTY IMAGES

This stunning desert and mountain region enjoys cooler weather in November that's ideal for hiking in the Atlas Mountains. Meanwhile, the nearby city of Marrakech is as vibrant as ever, with more people coming out in the less-than-scorching temps that linger all summer. Wander the souk, stroll the gorgeous gardens, and visit architectural highlights without breaking a sweat.

Nov. 18 is Morocco's Independence Day, and this year's Marrakech Coffee & Tea Festival will run Nov. 5-7, with more than 400 exhibitors and various workshops and classes. This year's Marrakech International Film Festival runs from Nov. 20–28.

Use points to stay at the Park Hyatt Marrakech, which opened in 2025 (rates from $594 or 25,000 World of Hyatt points per night), or check out the new Riad Beij, in the heart of the Medina (rates from $168 per night). To stay in the Atlas Mountains, you can use Virgin Red points at the luxe Virgin Limited Edition Kasbah Tamadot (rates from $577 or 140,000 Virgin Red points per night).

TPG tip: Marrakech has increased seasonal direct flight access, including a direct flight from Newark on United Airlines, which launched in 2024, and from Atlanta on Delta Air Lines, which debuted at the end of 2025.

Oaxaca, Mexico

JAN SOCHOR/GETTY IMAGES

Oaxaca's celebration of Dia de los Muertos, or Day of the Dead, is legendary, especially in Oaxaca City, where festivities and commemorations take place every year from Oct. 31 to Nov. 2. Expect elaborate costumes, parades, street festivals, cemetery and altar visits, and plenty of music, food and drinks.

After the city festivities, plan to stay at one of the region's design-forward hotels. Boutique hotel 7 Casa Laurel opened in April this year, and Casa Yati opened in Ventanilla on the beach last summer. La Valise Mazunte is a member of Small Luxury Hotels of the World with just six suites and a villa (rates from $400 or 130,000 Hilton Honors points per night).

TPG tip: You can fly directly to Puerto Escondido on United Airlines' Houston-to-Puerto Escondido route, which debuted in 2025. American Airlines launched its direct flights to Puerto Escondido from Dallas-Fort Worth in December 2025.

Bangkok

PRASIT PHOTO/GETTY IMAGES

November is the very beginning of the dry season in Thailand, so it's a good time to beat the winter crowds and score good airfare and hotel pricing. Nov. 24 is Loy Krathong, the beautiful floating lantern festival that lights up the Bangkok waterways on the night of the full moon in the 12th month of the Thai lunar calendar. Various events happen along the Chao Phraya River, in public parks and at cultural landmarks.

The city, which has several new hotels this year, with something for almost every loyalist. Voco Bangkok Surawong opened in January 2026 (rates from 19,000 IHG One Rewards and $137 per night). Canopy by Hilton Bangkok Sukhumvit opened in August 2026 (rates from 45,000 Hilton Honors points and $170 per night). The long-delayed Fairmont Bangkok Sukhumvit finally opened, also in August, with LEED Gold certification, a jasmine-inspired spa, seven food and beverage outlets and 474 rooms (rates from 12,345 ALL Accor points and $278 per night).

TPG tip: Last year, United Airlines launched a new daily flight to Bangkok via Hong Kong from Los Angeles and San Francisco, making it the only domestic airline to offer a daily one-stop flight to Bangkok from the U.S.

The Amazon, Brazil

FG TRADE/GETTY IMAGES

Looking for an adventure? November is the last month of the dry season to visit the Amazon, so there's still time to squeeze in an adventure before the end of the year. If colorful birds, jaguars, river dolphins and caiman sightings aren't enough for you, the first-ever Global Citizen music festival in the Amazon, in Belem, is Nov. 1. Global Citizen Festival Amazonia brings Brazilian music stars Anitta, Seu Jorge and Gaby Amarantos and Coldplay's Chris Martin together with Indigenous leaders to amplify climate action campaigns.

TPG tip: Avianca flies to Belem via Bogota, Colombia, from New York's John F. Kennedy International Airport (JFK), and LATAM flies there through Sao Paulo/Guarulhos International Airport (GRU). Book a room at Ibis Styles Belem Batista Campos ($67 per night) or Grand Mercure Belem do Para ($474 per night). Both are members of Accor Live Limitless.

St. Barts

PER BREIEHAGEN/GETTY IMAGES

If you've dreamed of a vacation in St. Barts but thought you couldn't afford it, November may be the best time to visit the gorgeous Caribbean island. You'll enjoy warm weather, beat the winter crowds and enjoy better prices on hotels and flights. And while the French island is known for always having excellent food, November brings even more culinary star power to the isle.

The Saint-Barth Gourmet Festival is Nov. 9-15 and features five chefs from Michelin-starred restaurants, including chef Amaury Bouhours of Restaurant Le Meurice Alain Ducasse and Thibault Sombardier of Les Parisiens.

TPG tip: Getting to St. Barts can be a bit of a journey if you don't have a private jet (ahem), but Cape Air flies twice daily from well-connected St. Thomas.

Western Australia

CHAMELEONSEYE/GETTY IMAGES

Looking to the other hemisphere is always a good idea, and in Australia, they're experiencing the other shoulder season: spring. In Western Australia, this means gorgeous wildflowers and some excellent festivals worth traveling for.

Foodies should plan to attend the world-renowned Pair'd Margaret River Region food and wine festival (Nov. 19-22), which brings world-class wine, food and music to the Margaret River region. This year's lineup includes chefs Rodney Scott, Matt Abe, and Mindy Woods, as well as wine writer Erin Larkin.

TPG tip: Hyde Perth opened in March of this year (rates from $239 per night) as the first Hyde hotel in Australia, a brand from Ennismore (also behind the Hoxton, Mama Shelter, Delano, and 25Hours brands) that also has locations in London, Ibiza, Dubai, and Miami.

Palm Springs, California

MATTHEW MICAH WRIGHT/GETTY IMAGES

Some November trips require compromising on the weather, but not in the desert. Palm Springs in November is ideal: With a daytime high of 79 degrees Fahrenheit and a low of 52 degrees Fahrenheit at night, you'll still avoid its peak tourist months, which begin in January. It's also ideal weather to hike in nearby Joshua Tree National Park.

Though most Pride events occur in June, the Palm Springs Pride Festival is a fall event. This year, it runs from Nov. 6–8, with the big parade on the final day. Expect live music, comedy, local vendors and artists, and plenty of food and drink stands in several areas across the downtown area. The Sunset Music and Comedy Festival runs concurrently each night at the Agua Caliente Casino.

Auen Learning Center, and DSRT Surf, a manmade surf lagoon that creates the perfect wave, every time, opened this summer in Palm Desert.

TPG tip: The iconic La Quinta Resort & Club celebrates its centennial in 2026, with special events and guest experiences all year long. It's now a part of Hilton's Curio Collection, so you can use and earn rewards points there (rates from 85,000 Hilton Honors points and $329 per night)

Florence, Italy

GUIDO COZZI ATLANTIDE/PHOTOTRAVEL/GETTY IMAGES

Las Vegas

CLIVE MASON/GETTY IMAGES

Miches, Dominican Republic

Though invisible itself, the air we breathe is a microcosm of identifiable pollen, fungal spores, viruses, dust particles, and fine particles of human pollution from everything from cars to asphalt sidewalks. It’s not something we should obsess over day to day, but as more people measure air quality on their own, many are getting readings they’d prefer to address. However, not everyone wants to buy an air purifier, which can cost several hundred dollars.

One proposed solution for at-home air filtration is to use readily available materials like cardboard, box fans, square HVAC filters, and everyone’s favorite building material: duct tape. I did a version of this myself for PopSci, and, though I have mixed feelings about the process, I’m overall satisfied with the result. Here, we’ll go through what I built, how I built it, and my overall conclusions after using it, along with resources for anyone interested in reading further. 

Introducing the DIY air filter cube

There are several configurations for making your own air purifier at home. One of the most documented is a Corsi-Rosenthal box, or CR box for short. It’s essentially an air filter cube, which is what I’ll be calling mine, with four sides made of furnace filters and one side made of a standard box fan (seen above). The fan pulls air through the filters and pushes out cleaner air than came in.

Resources and info

The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) provides a lot of information about DIY air cleaners, including visual construction guides, tips on improving DIY air cleaners, and general air cleaner usage advice. EPA research on DIY air cleaners for wildfire smoke will also inevitably interest those in wildfire-prone areas who don’t want to purchase an air purifier for smoke.

These resources were used extensively throughout this project. Instead of using this guide as a total replacement for these resources, use this page as a waypoint for how an individual or student group might use one of these devices, with practical concerns highlighted along the way.

Materials and considerations

The two primary purchases for your air cube project. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

For this project you’ll need:

  • 4 x 20-inch furnace air filters (MERV13 preferred)
  • 1 x 20-inch box fan with a modern ETL or UL listing
  • Cardboard box the fan came in
  • A thick roll of duct or masking tape (1.88-inch works nicely)
  • A cutting instrument.

I went to my local Walmart and bought a Lasko box fan, two pairs of Filtrete 20-inch MERV 11 furnace filters, and a roll of Duck Brand General Purpose Masking Tape. This represents what a typical consumer might buy without specialty ordering options or bulk purchasing. The total came to $73.58 before taxes and $78.36 after Florida state sales tax.

Notes on choosing the box fan

The box fan, or any fan in a roughly square housing, should be a modern ETL- or UL-listed fan (clearly marked on the box) because older fans and those not ETL- or UL-listed may have issues when run facing upward. This is a safety issue and should not be skipped.

Since the cardboard box your fan comes in will likely become part of your air filter cube, it’s worth getting one that isn’t damaged, though you can tape over small tears.

Note on choosing filters

EPA guidance recommends MERV13 or equivalent filters for the best experience. The MERV scale indicates how much and what kinds of particles the device can filter. High-tier MERV filters can be costly, so I opted for MERV11 since I was able to get a fairly good deal (around $23) per two-pack of these panels, and the notable difference between MERV11 and MERV13 was that the latter can handle forest fire smoke particles, which is not an issue in my area.

Assembling the air filter cube

You can assemble your air filter cube in less than an hour, even with one person. Rather than a strict job that requires exhaustive measuring and cutting, it’s more of an approximate job that relies on the magic of tape to do the heavy lifting. This is a feasible parent-and-child project for those 6+ and a solo project for those 10+.

Step 1: Establishing the interior

Make sure you point your fan and filters in the correct direction. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

For this project, we want air to flow in through the filters and out through the fan. That means that the part of the fan that blows air out needs to be on the exterior of your cube, and the back side of the fan that pulls in air needs to be on the inside of your cube. Like a surgeon marking which limb to operate on before making the first cut, I marked the interior of my fan with ‘I’s for safety. Meanwhile, your filters should have arrows. Those arrows should point towards the inside of the cube.

Step 2: Fit the filters together

Form a square with your filters. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

Place two filters together and use a small piece of tape to connect them as shown. Remember to have the arrows pointing inwards. Continue taping the filters until you form a square. You can either use two people here or secure one filter vertically with a trash bin to do it solo.

Step 3: Secure the edges

Secure the seams of your cube. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

The resulting square will be flimsy and contain cracks that air can get through. Tape the seams with duct tape. Don’t worry about being imperfect on the first try. In fact, going over the seams multiple times will only make your cube more secure and airtight.

Step 4: Flip and finish securing

Assemble the filters as your base. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

At this point, your square of filters should be feeling more and more structurally sound. You can now flip it over and repeat the simple taping method from Step 2 on the side that was previously on the ground.

Step 5: Cut off a side of the fan box and tape it to the bottom of your filter square

Take care not to damage the box, as part of it will prove useful. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

Remember, the box is an important part of the final product. If you place your square over the box, you’ll see they’re roughly the same size. Carefully cut out the most structurally sound side of the box and trim it to be roughly the size of your filter square. It won’t need to be perfect. I used scissors for this, as if imitating a school project, but a standard box cutter or multitool with a cutting edge would be better.

When done, tape the cardboard to one end of your filter square. Your air filter cube now has a bottom.

Step 6: Tape the outward-blowing fan to the top

Finally, put the fan on top. Image: John Alexander / Popular Science

As a last step, take the fan and put it on the top side of the box, taping it down carefully. If your fan has rounded corners, tape those too. If you notice small gaps in the fan housing, you can seal them with tape, provided they aren’t ventilation openings or near electrical components.

Final steps and optional additions

At this point, you have a completed air filter cube. With the fan side up, plug it into an outlet and turn the fan on. Look for structural integrity, a smooth spinning blade with no obstructions from tape or cardboard, and no unusual sounds or vibrations. I recommend being around your new filter cube for a bit before leaving it unattended. Follow any fan manufacturer guidance about long-term or unattended usage.

While I opted not to, you may optionally wish to cover the top of your air filter cube with the cardboard from the other side of the fan box, cutting out a circular hole over the spot where the blades spin. This reduces unintended air recirculation and overall increases the efficiency of the box.

Verdict, results, and final thoughts

Verdict: Making an air filter cube or CR box is a worthwhile experiment that results in a usable final product, but it may not be the best bang for your buck out there.

Results

After having run my air filter cube for several days now, despite not currently being in possession of an air quality monitor, I can still tell it has worked. For example, there is visible dust on the outside of the filters, which accumulated while I was cleaning dusty gear nearby. Furthermore, neither the grill nor the fan blades themselves are showing the dust that typically accumulates on long-running fans. I would call this project, from an engineering perspective, an obvious success.

Practicality concerns: Cost, time, and space efficiency

Functional mechanics aren’t the only issue, though. Money is time and time is money, and both of these are major issues with these boxes.

One of my biggest complaints about how CR boxes are described to the public is that they are “marketed” as cheap when filters are so expensive. Claims of hyper-cheap CR boxes with EPA-like performance are abundant, though EPA sources use MERV13. My use of MERV11, while reasonable, is another reason to call my creation an air filter cube rather than a true CR box.

Low-cost air purifiers are available commercially that even include HEPA filtration. For example, the Dreo Air Purifier for Home is just $44 right now and, according to Google Chrome’s Shopping Insights tool, despite its $100 MSRP, has never been above $56.

The Dreo air purifier highlights a lot of what feels absent from a DIY air filter cube: a small body you can comfortably place in your home, quiet operation, and filter changes that don’t require completely rebuilding the air purifier every time the filters are used up. For average consumers, I suspect something like the Dreo, or even a larger air purifier like the Levoit Core 300, is going to be a much better option in nearly every way.

Is a homemade air purifier the perfect science fair project?

Where this really shines is student science. Building your own air filtration cube is fun, sort of like Minecraft block crafting in real life. Students can explore airflow, cost-effectiveness of different materials, and bring home a usable appliance at the end of a one-hour lesson.

I particularly like the idea of students experimenting with different MERV levels, or comparing the effectiveness of low-cost commercial purifiers to their air filtration cubes. Students can, and should, ask themselves at what point the additional health benefits stop justifying the costs in time and money. These sorts of questions are at the core of environmental and healthcare policies that dictate our real world.
