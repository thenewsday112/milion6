Halloween Storm With Inches of Snow and 50 MPH Gusts Heads to California

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

This year, Halloween may be more than spooky.

In California’s Sierra Nevada mountains, the coming weekend looks wet, with significant snowfall possible at the region’s ski resorts and winds gusting up to 50 miles per hour. 

The National Weather Service Reno suggested delaying or re-evaluating travel plans throughout the Sierra, noting that snowfall is expected to be the most intense late Saturday night. Slick roads, closures, and travel delays are possible. Winter weather advisories apply until 7 a.m. Sunday morning.

A report from NWS Reno issued on Thursday, October 29, 2026.

National Weather Service Reno

The NWS also announced Thursday morning that forecasted snow totals for the storm had increased. Still, freezing levels seem a bit fuzzy.

In the Tahoe Basin, a winter weather advisory applies to elevations above 7,000 feet. In Mammoth Mountain’s home, Mono County, the advisory applies to elevations above 8,000 feet. 

The advisory that applies to one ski hotspot around Lake Tahoe, Palisades Tahoe, cautioned that two to five inches are possible, with nine inches on the highest peaks. 

Palisades Tahoe’s base area sits at 6,200 feet, but the resort’s upper reaches crest 9,000 feet. The slopes of Mt. Rose Ski Tahoe—home to Lake Tahoe’s highest base elevation—start at 8,260 feet.

The Mono County advisory calls for up to 12 inches at the highest elevations, with lower elevations slated for between three and six inches.

In other words, this could be a classic rain at the base, snow at the summit situation, depending on the resort. A previous Wednesday report from the NWS said that snowfall and rainfall totals remained uncertain because “snow levels keep fluctuating with each model run.”

Despite the prospective stoke, ski resort opening dates largely remain weeks away across California. 

Still, some precipitation could help those with more aggressive timelines. Mt. Rose is shooting for November 6 and, per its webcams, appears to already have a strong start on snowmaking. The natural stuff would certainly wouldn’t hurt, though.

Further south, Mammoth Mountain plans to open on November 13. 

In the meantime, that leaves one thing to do: think cold. Hopefully, all the Sierra folks have a white Halloween ahead, followed by some skiing next week.

EA Reportedly Closed BioWare To Invest In Developing Star Wars Battlefront III Instead Of The Next Mass Effect

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Electronic Arts shut down the legendary RPG studio BioWare last week, ending development on an untitled fifth Mass Effect game. Since then, we've seen what appears to be a leaked trailer for the game, which suggests it might have been called Mass Effect Universe, and heard a sneak peek at what Grammy-winning composer Austin Wintory was cooking up for the beloved sci-fi RPG series. Over the past week, fans across the internet have tried to piece together how a publisher could decide to shut down a studio as prolific as BioWare, but according to a new Bloomberg report, the answer is Star Wars. 

More specifically, Bloomberg writes that, presented with the opportunity to develop Star Wars Battlefront III with Disney, the publisher, which went private last year after a $55 billion acquisition,  chose to invest its money into that instead of BioWare's in-development Mass Effect sequel. The publication writes that EA looked at its portfolio for the coming years and had two massive sci-fi investments it could continue: Star Wars Battlefront III or BioWare's next Mass Effect. Even though Mass Effect was well into development, and even though the Battlefront threequel hasn't even entered development, EA chose to continue with the latter. And so BioWare was shut down, and Mass Effect development ended, though Bloomberg's sources say there are other, less significant reasons the studio was shuttered, too. 

Those other factors include the poor reception to 2017's Mass Effect: Andromeda, the failure of Anthem in 2019, and 2024's Dragon Age: The Veilguard, which, despite garnering positive critical reception, apparently missed EA's sales expectations by 50%. 

There's also the uncomfortable reality that BioWare made progressive LGBTQIA+ positive games but was a studio within a publisher house now partially owned by the Public Investment Fund of Saudi Arabia, a government fund from a country where same-sex activity, including marriage, is criminalized, sometimes punishable by death; these harsh laws extend to other facets of the LGBTQIA+ community. Another investment partner in this acquisition of EA is Affinity Partners, a Miami-based investment firm founded and run by former White House senior advisor Jared Kushner, who is the son-in-law of President Donald Trump, a political figure who imposes laws that discriminate against the LGBTQIA+ community, especially the 1% of Americans who are transgender; Trump has also been known to mock this community in various ways. No official statements say BioWare shut down because of its progressive leanings, but one has to wonder if that was a factor, especially after reports that BioWare scrubbed diversity from demos to avoid negative feedback from EA (thanks, Kotaku).

When BioWare closed last week, only 50 employees remained, all working on the next Mass Effect, after years of layoffs at the studio. 

A Mass Effect TV series is still in the works at Amazon, even though EA seems to have hung up Commander Shepard's video game suit for good.

How Édouard Manet’s Black Cat Became a Symbol of Modernity

Every Halloween, black cats return as emblems of witchcraft, bad luck, and the supernatural. Their sinister reputations stretch back centuries: starting in the Middle Ages, people associated cats with heresy and sexual transgression, and their perceived affinity with witches intensified during the trials of Elizabeth Francis and Agnes Waterhouse and their cat Sathan between 1565-1579.

School safety depends on strong community partnerships

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Place yourself at the start of a school emergency. A teacher is trying to communicate what’s happening. A 911 dispatcher is gathering critical information. School leaders are working to understand the situation while first responders start decision-making with whatever information is available.

In those first moments, the outcome of the emergency is already being shaped.

Many people think emergency response begins when police, fire, or EMS arrive on campus. But after more than 30 years in public safety as a firefighter, 911 dispatcher, supervisor, and emergency response technology professional, I have learned that the opposite is true. 

The most effective responses begin long before the emergency occurs, through the relationships, communication plans, and coordination schools build with their surrounding community. Ensuring success starts with mastering three fundamentals: speed, clarity, and coordination.

Speed: Reducing the time between an incident and response

One of the biggest misconceptions about emergency response is assuming when someone calls 911, responders can immediately take action. In reality, dispatchers must determine what is happening, where it is happening, and what resources are needed. 

That process takes time, especially when callers are frightened, injured, or unsure of exactly what they are seeing. Even small delays, like searching for a phone, relaying information through multiple people, or struggling to explain the situation clearly, can slow response times and impact outcomes.

Schools can help shorten that timeline by establishing emergency communication systems and  response plans that quickly connect dispatch centers with accurate and actionable information. 

The sooner first responders understand the situation, the sooner they can mobilize resources. 

This is especially important in rural communities, where staffing shortages and geographic distance often prolong response times. In some counties, a single officer may cover over 100 square miles, and EMS crews may be responding from 20 to 30 minutes away. Schools in these communities can benefit significantly from proactive coordination and clearly defined protocols.

Clarity: Getting responders the information they need

One of the most important shifts in modern emergency response is the ability to provide responders with detailed mapping information instead of general location data. School campuses are complex. Knowing an emergency is in progress “at the high school” is very different from knowing the exact floor, classroom, and closest entrance to quickly render aid.

When I started in the fire service, we relied on paper map books because GPS and Google Maps were not at our fingertips. I experienced an emergency firsthand where our crew responded to a completely wrong location because an important update had not been made to our map pack. We lost valuable time simply trying to reach the right location.

Today, schools can work more collaboratively with first responders by ensuring campuses are mapped clearly and operational plans are shared proactively. This allows dispatchers and responding agencies to make informed decisions before they arrive on campus.

This also strengthens clarity when different responding agencies are moving simultaneously. Law enforcement, fire personnel, EMS, school resource officers, and school administrators have different responsibilities during an emergency. When everyone can operate from the same information, they work together more effectively and reduce confusion during high-stress scenarios.  

Coordination: Building partnerships before an event

The strongest school safety programs are built on relationships, not binders stuffed with emergency plans. Effective coordination depends on schools regularly interacting with the people who support an emergency.

This collaboration should include:

  • Joint training exercises
  • Shared campus maps and operational plans
  • Direct communication pathways with dispatch centers
  • Regular reviews of response protocols and access points

These efforts create familiarity and trust. When first responders know the campus, school leadership understands emergency processes, and dispatchers can quickly and efficiently gather the critical information they need, teams operate more confidently and effectively.

A community responsibility

In public safety, we often talk about situational awareness during an emergency, but effective preparation begins long before an incident occurs. 

We cannot predict every emergency, but we can prepare for them. Investing in strong community partnerships empowers everyone involved to respond faster, communicate more clearly, and coordinate more effectively when it matters most.

November’s Night Sky Notes: Light-Day Away NASA’s Voyager mission took advantage of a once-every-175-year alignment of the outer planets for a grand tour of the solar system. The twin spacecraft revealed details about Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune – using each planet’s gravity to send them on to the next destination. Voyager set the stage for such ambitious orbiter missions as Galileo to Jupiter and Cassini to Saturn. Today both Voyager spacecraft continue to return valuable science from the far reaches of our solar system. NASA/JPL-Caltech

Twin Travelers

In 1977, NASA launched twin spacecraft Voyager 1 and 2, taking advantage of a once-every-175-year planetary alignment of the four largest planets in our solar system: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Using six gravity assists between them, this Grand Tour gave us up-close views of the gas and ice giants, including several moons. By the 1980s, the two probes went their separate ways at Saturn. As Voyager 2 continued toward Uranus and Neptune, Voyager 1 grabbed one more portrait of the solar system (excluding Mercury and Mars), including a final image of the pale blue dot we call home.

The Golden Record Cover NASA/JPL-Caltech

Playlist

“Hello from the children of planet Earth.”

Every good road trip needs some music to match. In this case, each craft carries the Golden Record, a 12-inch phonograph record with spoken greetings in 55 languages, 27 musical tracks, 116 images, and other sounds from Earth. Each record is protected by an outer cover featuring etchings showing our Sun’s location relative to 14 pulsars, along with instructions on how to play it. The design used various metals to protect the information encoded from space radiation: the record used copper and gold plating, and the cover used aluminum. The cover was electroplated with an ultra-pure sample of the isotope uranium-238. Uranium-238 has a half-life of 4.468 billion years.

Are We There Yet?

Did you know that a light-minute is a measure of distance, not time? In August 2012 and November 2018, Voyager 1 and 2 crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the Sun’s solar wind meets the interstellar medium. Using NASA’s Deep Space Network, Earth communicates with both spacecraft and tracks each on its journey. At the time of this article, the Canberra antenna tracked Voyager 1 at 25.80 billion km, or just over 16 billion miles.

This graphic shows the position of NASA’s Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 probes, outside of the heliosphere, a protective bubble created by the Sun that extends well past the orbit of Pluto. NASA/JPL-Caltech

On November 18, 2026, 49 years after its launch, Voyager 1 will cross the one-light-day mark, 16.1 billion miles from the Sun. You may have heard the term light-year in your favorite sci-fi movie or show, or when stargazing and referring to an object outside of our solar system.
