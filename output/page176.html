#754: Q&A: We Have $80K In Cash and Want a 1-2 Year Sabbatical. Is It Enough?

📅 Sep 29, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

“You have this golden opportunity during these low-income years.” That’s what I told a listener who’s heading into a one-to-two-year career break with $80,000 in cash and a paid-off home.

Is that enough? It depends on the one number she didn’t give us: how much she plans to spend.

So Joe and I walk through how to set aside what the sabbatical needs from a $394,000 taxable brokerage account, why a career break is the perfect window for Roth conversions, and whether an all-stock portfolio is too risky in your 30s.

Before we get there, we help a listener in the UK who wants to fund a career break, travel, and home projects while her emergency fund is only half built, and who’s wondering whether she has to finish one before starting the others. We close with something we rarely touch on this show: how to judge whether a politician is actually good for the economy.

Listener Questions

The questions we answered this episode

+

Anonymous (let’s call them “Amelia”) asks: How would you define the difference between an emergency fund, sinking funds (such as for a new laptop), and medium-term savings (like a career break or a big trip within the next two years)? How should we think about and treat these pots differently, and is there a defined line or overlap between them? Additionally, some people replace the term emergency fund with opportunity fund, which adds another dimension.

Secondly, how do you create a structure to decide how to apportion discretionary money across competing goals? How should someone choose which pot or goal to prioritize or allocate the largest contribution to, versus funding each pot equally?

For context, I am 42 and based in the UK. My defined contribution pension—similar to a 401(k)—can be accessed around age 57, and with my current contributions, I expect to retire at 64. My goal is to have an eight-month emergency fund, and I currently have four months saved. I would like to start funding other goals, such as a career break, travel, and home improvements, alongside building my emergency fund. Can I start funding those other pots now, or should the emergency fund be fully funded first? Once the emergency fund is taken care of, at what point should I pivot back to bringing my retirement age forward versus funding adventures over the next two decades? How should I balance multiple goals across differing timelines and magnitudes, weighing the flexibility of cash savings against the growth of investing?

Anonymous (let’s call them “Esther”) asks: I am trying to figure out the best way to organize my finances now that I finally have the courage to take a sabbatical after listening to one of you and Joe’s previous episodes.

My partner and I are in our mid-to-late 30s. We have been DIYing our finances, so it is not the most sophisticated. Jointly, we have $392,000 in our 401(k) and $189,000 in our Roth IRAs, and we max out the Roths every year. We are considering converting our 401(k) to Roth during these low-income years. We have $394,000 in a taxable brokerage account and about $80,000 in cash. We own a paid-off home worth $315,000 and moved to a rental for the sabbatical period. I am thinking of investing half of that into the brokerage account and saving the other half for a down payment on our future home, likely after my one- to two-year sabbatical.

Most of our investments are spread between VFIAX, VIGAX, and VGIX, and I recently added some VITAX. We have no bonds, and I am worried that we lack diversification, but I am not interested in managing rentals because we love to travel. We would also love to have the option to retire early in our mid-40s or 50s.

If that is our goal, should we wait to convert our 401(k) and do it when both of us retire? Will our current portfolio be too volatile, and should I rebalance everything? What are the implications of converting during early retirement years if we also want to take advantage of the $98,000 tax-free withdrawal from our brokerage account?

We often judge the success of politicians based on emotion or political spin. For example, when a recent jobs report showed great numbers, the opposing party claimed the data was rigged. Key Takeaways

The biggest ideas from the conversation

+
  • Don’t turn your emergency fund into an emergency: With four months already saved, I’d keep making small, steady contributions while also funding a career break, travel, and home projects. Joe’s test: check twice a year that it’s still growing.
  • Put a price and deadline on every goal: Divide each goal’s cost by the months until you want it, and the monthly total is almost always unrealistically huge. That’s the point: now you can cut goals, shrink them, or push the timeline out.
  • Earmark sabbatical money before making any other plans: Esther’s $80,000 in cash probably won’t cover a two-year career break, so I’d first set aside what the sabbatical will pull from her brokerage account. Of what’s left, half can stay invested and half becomes flexible cash for a future down payment.
  • Convert to Roth now, not in early retirement: A sabbatical’s low-income years are a golden window for Roth conversions, as long as you plan for the tax bill. As Joe puts it, the sooner you stop splitting your returns with Uncle Sam, the better.
  • Your all-stock portfolio’s real risk might be you: In your 30s, you have the risk capacity for zero bonds; the question is risk tolerance. Look up each fund’s standard deviation on Morningstar, translate it into real dollars, and add small-cap and international exposure for true diversification.
  • Judge politicians on process, not outcomes: No single leader controls much of an economy shaped by earnings, the Fed, wars, and disasters. Like judging someone’s health by their habits rather than one checkup, ask whether the underlying policies are sound, not what this quarter’s numbers say.

I jotted this apple crisp recipe into the notes app in my phone several years ago, and I return to it every fall.

This apple crisp is it for me.

I remember first making this in our old kitchen for a late-October dinner party – I made wild rice soup and apple cider old fashioneds (unreal), and someone pulled out the guitar just as the smell of apple crisp was floating from the oven. Absolutely charmed. I obviously bonded with this specific apple crisp that night and it has been with me ever since.

Why it’s good, if you ask me: the caramely apple layer underneath gets properly jammy, the topping gets properly crisp – just enough to provide contrast, AND, and, and, where the two layers melt together in the middle is just pure fall magic. Soft, buttery, and delicious.

Apple crisp in general is very simple, and this one feels rustic, almost, especially if you make it in a cast iron pan, which I usually do. Is she making apple crisp or is she living out her dreams from Little House on the Prairie on a chilly fall night? We’ll never know.

Description

My one-and-only apple crisp recipe! Tender jammy honeycrisp apples under a blanket of buttery brown sugar oat topping. The perfect fall treat!

Ingredients

Apples:

  • 6 cups diced apples (I use 4-5 large Honeycrisp, unpeeled)
  • 1/2 cup granulated sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1 tablespoon all-purpose flour

Topping:

  • 1/2 cup salted butter, melted (1 stick)
  • 1 cup brown sugar, lightly packed
  • 1 cup all-purpose flour
  • 3/4 cup rolled oats
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Preheat Oven: Preheat the oven to 350 degrees.
  2. Mix Apple Filling: Toss diced apples with the sugar, cinnamon, and flour in a large bowl until well-coated. Transfer to a 9×9 baking dish or a cast-iron skillet.
  3. Mix Topping: Mix melted butter, brown sugar, flour, oats, vanilla, and salt in a large bowl. Stir together with a spoon or fork. If needed, use your hands to bring the mixture together into a crumble; distribute evenly over the apples in the pan.
  4. Bake: Bake for 40-45 minutes, until the apples are jammy-looking around the edges, and the topping is golden brown.
  5. Rest and Serve: Let stand for 10-15 minutes, so the apple layer can cool and thicken slightly. Serve with vanilla ice cream.

Notes

This can work with or without peeling the apples. Personally, I don’t peel the apples – I am lazy, and I just can’t be bothered to mess with what is already a perfectly good fruit. The peels cook down the same way the apples do, so they become soft and unobtrusive in the apple crisp.

Here’s the client story this comes from.

“I get bored easily.”

This was the first thing a new client said to me.

When we dug into it, here’s the pattern that emerged:

-> Start a program

-> Get bored a few weeks in

-> Fall off the map

-> See something new, give that a try

-> Repeat

Strength training, then running, then a yoga challenge, then back to lifting with a different app.

Nerd Fitness Coaching was the latest in a long line of fresh starts.

And here’s the thing: all of it was GOOD.

She was moving. She was trying new things. She was having fun (at least at first).

But she was frustrated with herself.

She felt like she kept starting over and never getting anywhere.

And she wanted help figuring out what to do.

New is fun

Let’s be real: trying something new is exciting.

A new program, a new class, a new challenge. There’s a buzz to it.

And that buzz isn’t bad. Enjoying it is a big part of what keeps people moving.

The catch is that most goals need some repetition.

Getting stronger, running farther, learning a skill. You get better at the things you practice again and again.

So the question isn’t “how do I stop getting bored?”

It’s “how do I keep some of that new-thing fun AND repeat things enough to see progress?”

That’s what we worked on together.

Here’s what we did: level-up targets

When I built her program, I kept its overall shape the same week to week.

But inside it, a few key exercises got a target. Something like:

“Once you can do 12 reps with 25 pounds, we level up.”

The targets were far enough away to stretch her, and close enough that she could hit them in 2 to 6 weeks of focused practice.

When she hit one, we leveled up that exercise: new reps or weight, or a new variation. Everything else stayed put.

So something was always changing.

There was always a target she was closing in on and a level she’d recently beaten.

But the frame stayed the same. We were still working on the same overall qualities.

And that’s what let her progress stack up instead of resetting every few weeks.

Here’s what changed

She was able to stay really consistent with her program.

She still fell off some. (Life happens.)

But when she came back, she wasn’t looking for something new.

She was excited to pick up her program and keep progressing.

And sure, sometimes her goals would change.

We spent 6 months training for her first half marathon, then switched gears to focus on building muscle.

But that was a choice, not a restart.

Try a new level before a new program

Liking variety isn’t a flaw.

If you love bouncing between things and you’re happy doing it, keep bouncing.

This is for when the bouncing starts to feel frustrating.

Before you reach for a new program, try a new level.

Pick one exercise

Set a target you could reach in 2 to 6 weeks (a rep count or a weight)

When you hit it, level up that one exercise with new reps, a new weight, or a new variation. Leave everything else alone.

Then pick the next target.

I’m excited to see what you come up with.

-Matt

Dutch Police Arrest ‘Reformed’ Hacker in Shiny Hunters Investigation

📅 Sep 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Authorities in the Netherlands have arrested a 24-year-old convicted cybercriminal on suspicion of aiding in data thefts and extortions by the prolific hacker group ShinyHunters. In the days immediately following the suspect’s arrest, remaining ShinyHunters members dramatically escalated their attacks, stealing highly sensitive data from the FBI and extorting the Russian ransomware group Cl0p.

According to three sources familiar with the matter, the Dutch man arrested by authorities this month is Pepijn van der Stap, a convicted cybercriminal from Almere and Lelystad in the Netherlands. Van der Stap was previously convicted in 2023 in connection with a string of data thefts and extortions that prosecutors said earned between €1.5 million and €2.7 million.

At his trial in late 2023, van der Stap admitted that he lived a Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde existence, secretly using the hacker handle “Umbreon” to extort victims and post their data on English language hacking communities like the now-defunct RaidForums and Breached. By day, however, van der Stap was working as a software engineer at the Amsterdam-based cybersecurity startup Hadrian, while volunteering at the Dutch Institute for Vulnerability Disclosure (DIVD), a nonprofit security research group.

Pepijn van der Stap’s alter ego “Umbreon” selling a database on RaidForums, offering information on 2.3 million people from The Netherlands in September 2021. This user’s avatar is a depiction of the Pokemon character Umbreon. Image: KELA.

Van der Stap confessed to his data theft and extortion activity, and was sentenced to four years in prison (one of which was suspended). During his trial, van der Stap opted to remain in custody for a time rather than at home, saying he could not find better treatment on the outside for his ongoing psychological issues, which he claimed included PTSD related to childhood trauma. He was released from prison in December 2025.

In an interview with KrebsOnSecurity on September 9, 2026, Van der Stap cast himself as a reformed hacker who was trying to turn his life around and make a positive contribution to society. Van der Stap is currently employed as offensive security lead at the Dutch company Neo Security, which did not respond to requests for comment.

Van der Stap said he was still dealing with civil lawsuits and restitution related to his previous cybercrime victims, and that he was trying his best to make amends. But not long after that interview, the Dutch hacker abruptly stopped replying to messages. Efforts by others close to him also repeatedly failed to elicit a response for the past two weeks.

The LinkedIn profile for Pepijn van der Stap.

According to two sources with knowledge of the matter, Van der Stap was arrested by Dutch authorities on or around September 16, and has been held in custody for questioning since. One source said a colleague of theirs personally witnessed Dutch authorities carting items out of Van der Stap’s residence.

Authorities in the Netherlands have been asking the public for help in identifying the voice in a recorded telephone call from February 2026 in which a native Dutch-speaking ShinyHunters member social engineered their way into Odido, the nation’s largest mobile telecommunications provider. In that intrusion, ShinyHunters tricked an Odido employee into logging in at a spoofed website, and then used that access to steal data on more than 6.2 million Dutch people.

Responding to Dutch news media, ShinyHunters confirmed that the suspect in the audio clip is indeed a member of the hacker collective.

“Our team member has our full support – emotionally, mentally, and financially,” the hackers said. “Everything has been arranged, including a criminal defense lawyer. We do not look down on our staff and members; we take excellent care of them,” reads a statement ShinyHunters shared with NL Times. It remains unclear if the Dutch police have matched the Odido caller to a confirmed real-life identity. The Dutch police unit handling the Odido incident did not respond to requests for comment.

The group also lashed out at the authorities in the Netherlands. “The Dutch police will need all the luck in the world – and everyone’s prayers – if they want to catch him before we carry out another large-scale data theft in the Netherlands,” the ShinyHunters statement said. “Frankly, the Dutch police are a big joke; they are incapable of doing anything. Incompetent. Irrelevant. Unimportant. Useless.”

FBI, CL0P HACKS

Just days after sources say Van der Stap was detained by Dutch authorities, ShinyHunters claimed credit for an unusually brazen breach at the FBI’s job application site apply.fbijobs.gov. According to reporting from 404 Media, the data stolen from the FBI site includes Social Security numbers and personal information on more than 5,000 officials.

404 Media and Reuters reported the FBI data included each person’s job title or team, such as special agent, threat intake examiner, major cybercrimes unit, and those investigating cyber threats from foreign state-backed actors. Reuters examined documents shared by ShinyHunters and found they included sensitive psychiatric and medical files of FBI staff. The FBI issued a brief statement confirming the hack.

ShinyHunters said it gained access to the FBI site and other victims by exploiting a recently patched vulnerability (CVE-2026-35273) in PeopleSoft, a software-as-a-service platform from the software giant Oracle that is broadly used by companies to manage hiring and human resources, benefits and payroll. Oracle quickly issued a fix for the Peoplesoft vulnerability that ShinyHunters reportedly began exploiting as a zero-day in June, and at the time Mandiant released web application firewall rules intended for organizations who couldn’t apply the security update quickly enough.

But on Friday, BleepingComputer reported that ShinyHunters used a URL-encoding trick to bypass Mandiant’s suggested web application firewall rules designed to mitigate the threat from the PeopleSoft flaw. In a report released Sept. 25, security experts at Mandiant and the Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) confirmed that ShinyHunters had mass-exploited the PeopleSoft vulnerability to steal data from dozens of systems across a range of industries, including higher education, technology, healthcare, agriculture, transportation and government.

Van der Stap’s former hacker alias Umbreon was hidden in plain sight throughout the imagery ShinyHunters used to spread news about the FBI hack: The defacement image that ShinyHunters left behind on the hacked FBI jobs site included an ASCII art design featuring the Pokemon character Umbreon. The message at the top read, “This site has been seized by ShinyHunters. rooting your systems since ’19 ;)” The image appears identical to a defacement message ShinyHunters used in their 2020 hack of the English-language cybercrime community Hackforums.

The defacement message left by ShinyHunters on the FBI jobs site included an ASCII art rendition of the Pokemon character Umbreon. Image: Bleeping Computer.

Multiple sources close to the ShinyHunters investigation said the group’s recent risky attacks against the FBI and one of Russia’s most venerated ransomware groups amounted to a major pivot away from the more measured tenor of the hacking gang’s operations. Those sources said the sudden shift came about after ShinyHunters was taken over by a teenage cybercriminal from Amman, Jordan who goes by the nickname Rey and operates as part of a cybercrime group called ScatteredLapsussHunters (SLSH), which experts say is an amalgamation of three hacking groups — Scattered Spider, LAPSUS$ and ShinyHunters.

Those sources said Rey had an ongoing beef with the Dutch hacker over control of the ShinyHunters brand and data, and that the inclusion of the oversized Umbreon Pokemon image in the FBI jobs site defacement was likely an attempt by Rey to pin the hack on the Dutchman.

Rey was first publicly identified by the cybersecurity firm KELA in March 2025. In advance of our November 2025 profile of Rey, KrebsOnSecurity messaged Rey’s father and asked for permission to interview his teenage son. Rey’s dad merely forwarded the message to his son, who admitted to participating in ransomware attacks and said he was trying to extricate himself from the SLSH hacker group.

BLAMING UMBREON

Immediately after news of the FBI jobs site hack was picked up in the media, Rey’s main account on Twitter/X (Ryan Moran/@rmoskovy) was taunting the Cl0p ransomware group and the FBI, crudely depicting them as the twin towers in New York being struck by planes labeled “cl0p drama” and “fbi breach claim.” In the foreground of the city is the giant Pokemon figure of Umbreon.

A taunting meme uploaded to Twitter/X by Rey’s now-defunct account on Sept. 22. A giant float-sized version of the Pokemon character Umbreon can be seen in the bottom left.

On Sept. 24, KrebsOnSecurity again contacted Rey’s dad, asking to interview him and his son for a story on Rey’s apparent ascendency as the head of ShinyHunters. Just hours after that request, Rey deleted his longtime Twitter/X account. Meanwhile, Rey’s dad, who works for the Royal Jordanian Airlines, has failed to respond to a half-dozen emailed requests for comment about his son’s alleged activities.

Where does the bad blood between SLSH and ShinyHunters come from? According to a story in Wired this month, ShinyHunters and SLSH members briefly partnered earlier this year to help better monetize important stolen credentials collected by TeamPCP, an upstart group that was having great success compromising global code supply chains with malicious software but hadn’t been able to profit much from their stolen data (two alleged leaders of TeamPCP were arrested last month in Australia, and in an interview the TeamPCP leader claimed they made just $20,000).

The Wired story noted how Mandiant had infiltrated TeamPCP and was secretly responsible for having the crime group’s stolen credentials burned so quickly: Mandiant was secretly feeding those credentials to the major cloud providers like Amazon and Microsoft, who quickly invalidated the stolen keys. Meanwhile, the formerly cooperating hacker groups began to blame one another for causing the credentials to become worthless.

Wired’s Andy Greenberg reported that a few weeks after partnering with TeamPCP, “ShinyHunters went rogue, carrying out its own extortions with TeamPCP’s credentials but without giving the supply-chain hackers their cut.”

Mandiant researcher Austin Larsen told KrebsOnSecurity earlier this month that ShinyHunters has been enjoying a successful extortion spree so far this year, and is on track to pull in nearly $100 million in extortion payments from cybercrime victims in 2026.

Van der Stap claims he was never motivated by money and that his earlier hacker activity was driven by a desire to have the world’s most complete collection of stolen databases. Speaking with reporters from Bloomberg in 2024, Van der Stap said that singular focus in turn fueled his desire to carry out cyberattacks.

“The hacking was very easy for me, and it wasn’t a compulsion,” he told Bloomberg. “My habit was collecting. Collecting data, organizing data, downloading data, creating folders.”

DIVD, the nonprofit security research group where Van der Stap previously served as a volunteer, disclosed on LinkedIn last week that the organization was dealing with an internal cybersecurity incident that appears to have involved the malicious use of artificial intelligence. DIVD has released few details about that incident, but a spokesperson for the nonprofit told KrebsOnSecurity it does not appear related to ShinyHunters, nor are there any signs the matter involves the work of a previous volunteer.

Yat gaw mein, also known as Baltimore yak or dirty yak, is Baltimore carryout comfort food at its finest. A Chinese American remix of Beijing’s zha jiang mian, the dish dates back to the late 19th century, when Chinese immigrants started settling in the city. Setting up shop in traditionally Black and working-class neighborhoods, Chinese cooks adapted their recipes to locally available ingredients, swapping fermented pastes for a simpler, soy-based gravy. Over time, something new emerged: savory wheat noodles, stir-fried with shrimp, beef, and vegetables and punctuated with a hard-boiled egg. Today you can find the beloved dish at counter spots around town like Jimmy’s Carryout, Danny’s, and Pimlico Chinese Carry Out.

In this at-home version from Steve Chu, the chef behind Baltimore’s Ekiben, smoky wok-charred noodles are tossed with umami-packed soy sauce and oyster sauce, rich fat, and sweet caramelized onions. What makes the dish “dirty” is the flavor that develops when soy sauce, sugar, and aromatics scorch against the hot metal of the wok, creating deep savory notes balanced by subtle sweetness. Be sure to keep the wok on high heat and stir frequently, since this allows the wok hei—which translates to “the breath of a wok” and refers to that elusive smokiness—to work its magic. The finishing squeeze of ketchup might sound unusual, but it delivers the acidic punch that makes the dish complete.

Makes: 2–4 Time: 25 minutes

Ingredients

  • Kosher salt
  • 1 lb. fresh wheat noodles, such as lo mein or udon
  • ¼ cup duck fat, chicken fat, vegetable oil, or other cooking fat
  • 1½ cups bean sprouts
  • 1 medium onion, thinly sliced
  • 1 medium scallion, thinly sliced
  • ¼ cup dark soy sauce, plus more if needed
  • 2 Tbsp. light soy sauce
  • 2 Tbsp. oyster sauce
  • 1 Tbsp. MSG or chicken bouillon powder
  • 1 Tbsp. sugar
  • 1–2 tsp. Tianjin chile flakes or crushed red chile flakes
  • Steamed or poached shrimp or medium-rare seared beef slices, for topping
  • 1 hard-boiled egg, peeled, for topping
  • Ketchup and hot sauce, for serving

Instructions

  1. Bring a large pot of salted water to a boil. Add the noodles and cook for 1 minute less than the package instructions so they remain slightly undercooked. Drain, then rinse briefly under cold water. Set aside to cool. 
  2. Heat a wok over high until lightly smoking. Add the duck fat, followed by the bean sprouts, onion, and scallion. Stir-fry until the mixture begins to brown around the edges, about 1 minute. Add the noodles, both soy sauces, the oyster sauce, MSG, sugar, and chile flakes. Stir-fry, tossing continuously so the sauce coats the noodles evenly and begins to brown against the wok, 1–4 minutes. (If a darker color is desired, add a little more dark soy sauce.)
  3. Add 1½ cups of water to the wok, scraping up the browned bits, and simmer until the noodles absorb some of the liquid, about 1 minute. (If you prefer a saucier dish, add a little more water.) Add the shrimp and hard-boiled egg, tossing gently so the egg warms through and takes on some of the dark soy color without breaking apart. 
  4. Serve immediately, with ketchup and hot sauce on the side.

A U.S. Army soldier who pleaded guilty to hacking into multiple telecommunications companies and stealing mobile call and text metadata for more than 100 million AT&T customers in 2024 was sentenced to 70 months in federal prison today and ordered to pay nearly $300,000 in restitution to victims.

One of several selfies from the Facebook page of Cameron Wagenius.

Cameron John Wagenius, 22, was stationed at a U.S. Army base in South Korea when he adopted the cybercriminal persona “Kiberphant0m.” Working with three alleged co-conspirators, Kiberphant0m downloaded data from several large customers of the cloud data storage service Snowflake that had exposed credentials and did not enforce multi-factor authentication (Snowflake has since mandated MFA on all accounts).

In October 2024, Kiberphant0m bragged on the cybercrime forums that he’d stolen the call and text metadata (e.g. source and destination number, timestamp, duration, etc.) for tens of millions of AT&T customers. Kiberphant0m claimed to have hacked into more than dozen telecommunications companies worldwide, including Verizon’s Push-to-Talk business, and publicly extorted these companies in exchange for a promise not to publish the stolen data.

In late November 2025, KrebsOnSecurity warned that Kiberphant0m was likely a U.S. soldier stationed in South Korea. Less than a month later, Wagenius was arrested and charged in two separate federal indictments, and soon pleaded guilty to all counts in both cases.

At his sentencing hearing in Seattle today, Wagenius was sentenced to nearly six years in federal prison, and ordered to pay $294,978 in restitution.

Federal prosecutors said Wagenius was assisted in his efforts to extort victim companies by Kenneth Schuchman, a 28-year old man from Vancouver, Washington who has a lengthy cybercriminal history. In 2019, Schuchman pleaded guilty to operating the Satori botnet, a vast collection of hacked Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices that was used for large-scale distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks.

Two other alleged co-conspirators of Wagenius are still facing charges in connection with the Snowflake data thefts; Conor Riley Moucka, a.k.a. “Judische,” of Kitchener, Ontario was arrested in 2024 and pleaded guilty in August 2026; and John Erin Binns, an American man currently living in Turkey who is also wanted for a 2021 data breach at T-Mobile that exposed the personal information of at least 76 million customers.

Kiberphant0m also admitted to re-extorting victims, and threatening to disclose national security secrets. Immediately following Moucka’s arrest — after AT&T had already paid the extortion group a $370,000 Bitcoin ransom — Kiberphant0m posted on hacker forums what he claimed were the AT&T call logs for then President-elect Donald Trump and for then Vice President Kamala Harris, as well as schematics allegedly stolen from the U.S. National Security Agency (NSA).

Paul Russell is a resident agent in charge at the Defense Criminal Investigative Service (DCIS), the criminal investigative arm of the U.S. Department of Defense Office of Inspector General. Russell said when DCIS received information that a soldier with secret clearance was allegedly involved in cybercrime and extortion, the agency began working the investigation alongside the FBI, the Army Criminal Investigative Division (CID), and the U.S. Secret Service.

“We don’t often get leads where there’s an active duty soldier with a secret clearance who’s creating hacking tools and trafficking in data,” Russell said. “That doesn’t happen every day, and so when that hits it really spins all of our partner organizations up. Will the AI Bubble Destroy our Retirement?

Wow, how about that stock market? 

It’s a phrase we keep having to dust off and use again, as the years go by and the market keeps surprising us. 

When it crashes, some of us worry because we see our retirement stash shrinking.

 But even when it rises to record levels and then to super-duper-crazy record valuations, we find reason to worry. Because that just means an even bigger crash is coming, right? Especially when this boom-bubble is built on the back of something as frenzied as the present Artificial Intelligence boom… right?

For those who have been happily tuned out of this drama and just enjoying life: Congratulations! Keep up the great work. But just to give you a quick background for the purposes of this article, here’s the AI story in three points:

  • Over the past few years, AI has reached a level where it can do complex thinking and reasoning tasks that most of us thought might not happen in our lifetimes. It’s shockingly useful.
  • This has led to the fastest worldwide adoption of any technology in history: over 1.5 billion people are already using it, as are most large companies 
  • And this has caused a crazy cycle of growing sales, investor enthusiasm and data center buildout that is now the largest investment cycle humans have ever made in anything: 1.8 trillion dollars has been spent since just 2022, with another trillion going out the door next year alone. Which you can compare to:

    – The cost of the 2700-foot-tall Burj Khalifa, the highest skyscraper in the world (about $2 billion in today’s dollars)
    – Or the entire US interstate highway system, 48,000 miles of wide, flat, highly engineered roads (and 55,000 massive bridges) which cross countless mountain ranges, rivers and canyons: $660 billion in today’s dollars.

So where’s the problem?

Investors worry that while AI is definitely a major invention, the mania around it is still too much, too fast, and thus we are due for an even bigger version of the dot com crash we had in the early 2000s. Which happens to be an interesting subject for me, since that was the boom that boosted my own early career and got me on the path to early retirement … before the ensuing crash almost cost me my job and my citizenship application.

As an aging Internet Financial Guru, I have the privilege of getting lots of questions about this investment cycle, just as I have about past booms and busts. And to address them, I decided to not only write this blog article but also create a little talk about it – which I already gave for the first time at an event called Camp FI Midwest earlier this month. I also hope to polish it up and deliver an improved version at the Bogleheads conference in November. 

So what that means is that today you not only get a blog post, but a few silly presentation slides to go with it for extra entertainment. All with no need for a plane ticket or all that hassle of leaving your house. So let’s get into it!

Will the AI Bubble Ruin our Retirement?

–

So I recently hit 21 years of retirement. This means I’ve grown pretty comfortable with the idea. But it still threw me for a loop one time when a friend asked me this question:

“How can you be retired, and comfortable in your retirement, and sleep at night, with everything that’s going on in the world? Aren’t you worried?”

And I was like, “No, what should I be worried about?”

But if you’re a news watcher and a worrier yourself, you can probably think of a few things I should be worried about. Perhaps stuff like this:

2026 worries

You’ve got our corrupt and/or dangerous politicians like Trump and Putin menacing the world. Inflation eroding our purchasing power, the AI Bubble, the towering US National Debt, the Iran war and the Ukraine war and all these other wars that are just on the verge of tipping us all into destruction.

But it turns out these were not the things my friend was asking about. 

Because she actually asked me this question over twenty years ago… In the year 2006. So back then we were worried about entirely different things, right? 

2006 worries

Back then we had corrupt and/or dangerous leaders like Bush, Hussein and of course Bin Laden. There was still a war in the middle east but it was Iraq instead of Iran. We were still worried about the National Debt and Inflation. And we also worried about our overvalued stock market. But back then it was because of the housing bubble instead of the AI bubble. And instead of AI taking our jobs it was going to be outsourcing to India and China. 

Oh and here’s an interesting one: There was a big debate over Peak Oil, and whether the world was doomed because fossil fuel use was always increasing while supply was bound to decrease. And it turns out the opposite happened as you’ll see below.

So then after all this worry in 2006, what ended up happening?

–

Well we did get the Great Financial Crisis, which was caused by a combination of irrational exuberance over increasing house prices combined with a foolish degree of fancy leverage in the financial instruments used to issue mortgages. And it was the biggest crash since the Great Depression.

But even in that craziest of situations, look how minor it looks in the big picture. A $100,000 investment still ended up ballooning into $852,000 today. And if you look carefully in here you can just see the Covid Crash tucked in there in 2020. Remember that?

And not only that, the rest of the world got a lot better too:

–

The portion of people living in extreme poverty dropped from 20% in 2005 to only 8% over the next 20 years. Infant mortality – children who die in their first year of life – was cut in half. Clean energy became a thing as solar panels became about 40 times cheaper. So solar generation now makes up the vast majority of all the new generation we add today (the equivalent of 647 nuclear power plants of peak capacity added last year, but with much cheaper and easier solar panels). And sales of pure electric cars have gone from zero up to about a quarter of all the cars sold on Earth last year, on its way to 100%, which is where they already are in Norway.

So if we circle back to that question my friend asked me 20 years ago: do you think I should have focused my energy on worrying about an uncertain future, or optimism?

Which of course brings up the question: Is it different this time?

Maybe the US national debt is finally big enough to really start messing with us. Maybe the AI bubble is way bigger than the housing bubble and we won’t bounce back from this next stock market crash. Or maybe AI will spin out of control into a super intelligence that takes over the planet and realizes it does not need us any more.

And the financial media loves to spin a good scary story about all of this. But you know what the fear mongers all seem to be missing?

It’s the fact that at the core, our prosperity does not come from the financial system or the stock market, which are just some made-up numbers on some computers. 

Really, Prosperity comes from Productivity.

–

We first covered this lesson right here on MMM, in the 2014 Classic entitled, “Why we are not really all doomed”. And sure enough, twelve years later the timeless lessons therein remain just as true now as they were back then. 

And the lesson is really simple: the world of capitalism is always choppy and prone to wild swings. But if you peek beneath the waves, there are real people in there, doing real work and coming up with real inventions. 

In fact, the very idea of an “economy” or a financial system is just another human invention. If the whole system crashes because we don’t like the numbers we see in there, we can literally make up some new numbers and then get back to work. Which is exactly what we did in 2008 and many times before that, and it seems to have worked just fine. 

Meanwhile, we still have every tool we ever invented to boost our productivity. And our standard of living, and our economic growth, and our possibility of retiring early, all come from high productivity.

But even Productivity and Prosperity aren’t all that important.

Because once we reach a certain standard of living, human happiness kind of tops out in that dimension. In first-world countries, we blew past those minimum requirements several decades ago. And then we start looking for other factors to maximize our overall happiness. All anybody really wants is to lead the happiest, most satisfying life they can manage

And when you think of it that way, your wealth is really just part of your life situation, which is part of this little green slice of the happiness pie. 

Approximate non-scientific factors in happiness

Genetics is unfortunately the biggest slice – some people are just plain happier than others. But there are also quite a few choices that are within your control. 

Focusing on good close relationships and being kind to people. Practicing Gratitude and Optimism as your lifelong philosophy. And making sure you pack as many healthy (outdoor) activities into your days as you can.

So, I didn’t invent anything new in this blog post. And most of it is just a repackaging of the same stuff I’ve been writing about since 2011. And yet some regular MMM readers who presumably know all this stuff are STILL afraid. Afraid to retire, afraid of things in their unknown future. Can we fix this? 

I think the biggest problem is Fear Itself.

–

The problem is that as a Human Being, you are basically a Danger Detection Machine. And this pervasive background fear is just a trait we evolved to protect ourselves from danger. 

But there’s a weird thing about our detection machinery. We can adapt and learn to function even in very dangerous environments, but when the danger goes away, we keep looking for stuff to worry about. In other words, there is a problem with fear: for many of us, it never really goes away. It just keeps moving the goalposts.

While Nature has wisely endowed us with a fear of predators and disease, if you solve those things you’ll just start worrying about whether or not you can get enough food. And supplies, and protecting yourself against scarcity. 

If you succeed, next you’ll be worrying about if you can fit in with your tribe, because if you don’t, you might be exiled. 

And if you don’t have to worry about that, you’ll go back to worrying about money for different reasons. Thanks to our tendency to use comparisons rather than absolutes (sometimes called “anchoring bias” and the “contrast effect”) when judging our lives, just getting out of poverty isn’t enough. We just move on to wanting more money.

And then, when some people get enough money to have a comfortable upper-middle-class lifestyle, they take the logical next step of starting a Homeowner’s Association, so they can worry about the unauthorized weeds on their neighbor’s lawn. Or maybe a Political Action Committee in hopes of controlling the color of their future neighbors’ skin.

And even if all the lawns and gardens are green and perfect and people get really rich, they just move on to worry about tax strategy, leaving a huge inheritance for their children, and avoiding RMDs. 

Required minimum Distributions. This is when, if you get to 75 years old and you have too much money, the government will start making you withdraw some of that money so you can spend it and pay taxes on it. And people actually worry about this. I shit you not.

But wait! You can actually fight back against fear. It’s just a bit tricky because it requires some self awareness. But you have the advantage, because you have the ability to think rationally. And your fear is quite clearly irrational.

–

And the first step is to understand your own history. Are you afraid of certain things because of your childhood? 

I might have been afraid of running out of money, because in my family there was always a perceived shortage of the stuff, possibly enforced by my Dad’s scarcity mentality. And he was aware that his own fears of scarcity came from childhood as well: he grew up in a truly low income family in the 1940s and 50s, under the care of parents who had lived through the Great Depression of the 1930s.

Even more significant is that survivors of trauma and abuse will very likely end up with fear issues in adulthood. It’s natural, and unfortunately abuse is way more widespread even in this country than we would like to admit. This makes it harder to eliminate, but it still helps to understand that your past is what is causing these feelings, rather than an objective understanding of the present.

Identify the Fear: Once you understand yourself, it helps to talk through the nature of your fear in more detail. This is also often taught in therapy. What would happen if it really came true? What would the worst case scenario be? It’s often not all that bad.

In one post, why you’ll probably never run out of money, I went to great lengths to imagine what it would mean for a wealthy person like yourself to actually let the well run dry, and let’s say you’ll probably never come close.

Once you identify the fear, you can start taking action. And one of the best and most accurate slogans ever is that action cures fear. It’s because it makes the unfamiliar, familiar. And you get confidence that you can really create change. And then that learning creates Resilience – the ability to adapt to ANY new situation, which is a trait also known as Badassity. And with sufficient Badassity, nothing is scary.

–

Think about it: if you had the skills and abilities to handle ANY situation, skills in the mental and physical and even the realms of emotion and wisdom, would you really have to worry about anything?

The next thing you can do is tune out all the unnecessary crap from your daily mental diet. And for most Americans, this means the daily news. 

The news is not helping you to keep informed, it’s just poisoning your mind with a hand-picked selection of the scariest stories of each day. If you want to learn about something, go find a book on the subject. And if you want to make a difference, the only thing that counts is not the news stories you watch – it’s only the actions that you take – in other words, your Positive Behaviors.

 As a human being, you are wired to solve problems with both your body and your mind, and you are meant to do it outdoors. So if you spend all your days in a house and a car and in an office using the computer, of course you are going to have some mental health problems. This should not be a surprising thing you need to ask your therapist about, it is an expected result of not living the life that you were literally created to live as a Human being!

 So you need to focus on learning, moving, and solving problems. And you need your food and drink intake to be in alignment with all of this too. Life will get a lot less scary as soon as you are living life in the way you were built to live it.

Then finally, you can start swapping out the fear-mongers in your life, for other positive, productive, optimistic people. And watch how much the positive mentality rubs off on you. In the FI community, at least the subgroup of people that I like to spend time with, nobody focuses on fear. It’s all just about living the best life we can, and helping other people do so as well.

So now that we know how to fight back against fear, we can start using a new approach when we go into anything new. And that approach is something I like to call Everything is an Experiment.

Everything is an Experiment

And this applies whether you are talking about something tiny like a new paint color. But it scales up to bigger things like switching to a new car, or a new house, meeting new friends, looking for new love by going through the sometimes hell of first dates, a new job, or even quitting your last job by taking an early retirement.

So let’s bring this back around to AI, the thing that everybody seems to be afraid of right now.

I think of our new AI era as just being another giant experiment. And because of this, I don’t find it even remotely scary. But I do find it endlessly fascinating.

First of all, it’s a change. A potentially unknown one, and for some people that’s scary.

But instead of just leaving it there and then doom-scrolling ominous news articles about it, why not learn why people are so excited about AI? As someone with a lifelong background in technology and economics (and a general lack of political affiliation), I can see a lot more potential upside than downside. And that mainly comes from the fact that I see AI as just a very fancy form of Cognitive Nail Gun.

–

It will boost our efficiency, which means our productivity, which means prosperity. Because it’s really just a super smart brain that we all get to tap into at a fairly negligible cost. 

Unfortunately, it will also displace workers like every other new productivity technology. It’s already doing so in things like entry level office jobs in software and finance. It will eventually replace car and truck drivers, and so on. But in exchange, these services will become cheaper, and new industries will be created because of the new technology. Much like the Internet itself, and then the enormous industries unleashed by smartphones and mobile apps.

And one unfortunate side effect of these new inventions is that they tend to concentrate their rewards on the people who own them. If you own a house building company and employ a bunch of carpenters and give nail guns to the best of those carpenters, you can now lay off the other half and still get more done. If you own a company and roll out AI to the workers, you can do the same thing. 

And if you’re a big public company, your shareholders also benefit, which happens to include most of the people reading this. As you’ve seen when looking at your portfolios over the past two years.

Artificial Intelligence is just like any other new thing that pops into your life: it’s an opportunity to make some choices. And it is up to you to decide if this is something to be afraid of, or to use as a trigger for learning, action, and living a more interesting life.

So that’s my little talk (and surprisingly long blog post) on Fear versus the AI bubble.

I hope that if you do feel fearful about this or any other issue in your future life, you’ll take it as a cue to learn more about your own fear and emotions and manage them as the root cause they are, rather than just trying to avoid the symptoms. Because in the wise words of my friends The Donegans:

–

Everything you want in life, that you don’t already have,
lies on the other side of Fear.
Because if you weren’t afraid to go out there and get it, you would already have it.

If you’ve ever been starstruck by a perfect Cinderella pumpkin or a stately stalk of Brussels sprouts, you likely understand the appeal of a book focused on a single fruit or vegetable. While books like this aren’t always long, they typically go deep, with the fervor of an author who is fully obsessed.

One charming example, The Core of an Onion: Peeling the Rarest Common Food by Mark Kurlansky, melds authority with a dose of irreverence. First published in 2023, it’s a slim 200 pages, but the writer (who’s also written nonfiction books dedicated to oysters, salt, and cod) packs a lot in. “Onions have a way of taking on unexpected significance,” he posits. The first section covers the allium’s history and cultural impact. (Did you know that ancient Egyptians tied them to mummies’ feet as a preservative?) Kurlansky then offers loosely structured recipes for breads, sauces, and more, all centering the humble bulb.

Elevating a perhaps overlooked ingredient is a core tenet of single-­subject cookbookery. In The Compleat Squash: A Passionate Grower’s Guide to Pumpkins, Squash, and Gourds, a canonical 2004 ode to members of the Cucurbitaceae ­family, master ­gardener Amy Goldman Fowler’s passion is abundantly evident. “Sometimes I don’t know where pumpkins begin and I end,” she writes, before segueing into a series of squash and gourd portraits, each showing an individual specimen more striking than the last. Fowler shares recipes too, in addition to breed spotlights, resources for seed ­buying, and advice on growing your own.

Some such tomes aim to introduce the reader to the joys of less ubiquitous ingredients. Lane Selman and Margarett Waterbury’s Radicchio: A Celebration of the Beloved Italian Vegetable tells the “human and natural history” of the bitter chicory, along with instructions for how to grow it and cook with it. Selman is the founder of the Culinary Breeding Network, an initiative that rallies scientists, farmers, and chefs to geek out over new produce varieties. The book gets weird in the best way possible, with profiles of Italian growers and recipes including gnocchi with radicchio pesto, radicchio with prosciutto ­gratin, and chocolate–radicchio cake (why not?).

Radicchio reads almost like a community cookbook, and that’s intentional. Selman’s inspiration to pursue the topic was the sheer lack of writing on this oddball vegetable: “All of these people that we’ve met on our way down the rabbit hole needed to be included,” she says. “They’re the ones who have given us all this information. Sometimes it takes a village.”

Other single-subject cookbooks showcase pantry ­staples that are rarely in the spotlight. Anna Hezel’s Little Book of Olives: A Guide to Cooking, Eating, and Drinking for the Brine-Inclined presents recipes including cantaloupe and crushed-olive panzanella, Castelvetrano and pistachio pesto, and a whole chapter of olive desserts, plus shopping tips and a breakdown of varieties. The book’s retro aesthetic lends ­gravitas to an ingredient often relegated to garnish status.

Finally, some books exist more to dazzle than to inform, as is the case with pop artist Ed Ruscha and River Café chef Ruthie Rogers’s 2025 release Squeeze Me: Lemon Recipes & Art. In the pair’s high-gloss love letter to the lemon, pop art–coded photos with cheeky captions and a suite of minimalist recipes—such as lemon–­mascarpone tart and limoncello—make this ode to the simple citrus equally at home on a coffee table or the kitchen shelf.

While styles and forms vary, these books serve a ­common goal: to illuminate the sometimes surprising pleasures of a particular fruit or vegetable. That they manage to do so in such delightful fashion is proof that, once in a while, ­working with less really is more.

Man for Man: How Brighton’s Press Suffocated Arsenal

📅 Sep 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

BrBrighton arrived at the match fresh off a 3–2 win at Old Trafford, while Arsenal boasted a clean defensive record and a reputation for controlling games and seeing them out like few other teams in Europe. The match ended 3–0, sparking immense hype around Brighton’s pressing. This analysis explores the underlying mechanisms: their man-oriented pressing across the entire pitch, their asymmetrical build-up play, their transition behavior after winning the ball, and the three goals themselves.

The Basic Idea: Intensity with Control

Arteta’s Arsenal are among the best sides in Europe at controlling games out of possession. Any opponent who sets out to establish order is playing on the ground Arsenal own. Hürzeler therefore decided against control – not control surrendered in the sense of a low block, but a chaotic game state that Brighton generated themselves and were therefore prepared for.

The thread running through ninety minutes: Arsenal were not to settle into an organised phase either with or without the ball. Three decisions delivered that. The build-up was set up asymmetrically – on the right Brighton prepared the long ball and occupied the landing zone before it was played, on the left counter-movements opened the short route. It was never long instead of short, but two prepared options that would have demanded opposite answers from Arsenal. After regains the ball was not secured but risked immediately, because a secured ball gives the opponent exactly the seconds they need to reorganise. And the press lived off intensity and constantly new starting points: when a line was played through, Brighton did not stand still but re-established their shape and triggered again.

All three decisions give up ball control in the classical sense. Control came instead from preparation – if you know where the ball will land and you are standing there beforehand, you control the situation without the ball.

What followed can be pinned to a single number: 41 Arsenal turnovers in their own danger zone. That is not a figure a side of this quality produces because they play badly, but because they never reached the game state their build-up is designed for.

Out of Possession: Man-Marking Across the Whole Pitch

Brightons manorientated press against a 4-3-3

The press was simple in principle and demanding in execution. Brighton set up with man-reference to their direct opponent and switched into pure man-marking on the trigger. Against a side that tracks from the first second, marking assignments can be pulled apart by movement before any pressure exists; Brighton refused to offer that, because their shape stayed compact until the trigger moment.

In the “mid-block,” positioned +1 on the last line, but ready at any moment to transition into a high press upon a pressing trigger.

The steering began with Ayari. He curved his pressing run so that Konsa stayed in his cover shadow and Raya was forced to the other side. Kostoulas marked Gabriel, while Chema Andrés and Groß pushed aggressively onto Arsenal’s No. 8s. Groß in particular tracked the dropping Rice and at times advanced into the first pressing line, in some sequences all the way onto Konsa. That requires Rice to be picked up by a team-mate in the same moment – the man-marking worked through handovers as much as through tracking. Centrally, the striker and the No. 10 rotated accordingly: whoever had the shorter route to the next man on the ball pushed out, while the other covered behind him and took over again on the next trigger.

It only held up because the centre-backs came with it. Vušković and Struijk stepped out onto Ødegaard and the dropping Havertz and prevented an underload in central areas. When Arsenal built with three in the first line, Brighton kept one centre-back zonal in the back line – but with permanent reference to the unmarked player in the No. 6 space, stepping onto him the moment the press was triggered. Arsenal’s numerical advantage in build-up therefore never translated into a free man anywhere on the pitch.

Raya was part of the marking scheme too. Ayari’s cover shadow left only the route through a No. 6, and under man-marking that is not an escape but a duel that begins with the first touch. Raya’s body shape reinforced it: he tended to open only half-way, which effectively removed the switch to the far side from his repertoire. Steered onto his right foot, only the right-hand build-up side remained; the left lay open for long stretches and was unreachable for the one player who could have found it.

Every back pass served as a trigger. When the ball went back to Raya or the centre-backs, Brighton pushed up as a unit and raised the defensive line. The density around the ball came less from the base shape than from the recovery runs: players who had been beaten sprinted back, and they did not run at the ball but with a cover shadow onto the lay-off option behind it. An Arsenal player with a marker in his back therefore had neither the lay-off nor the chance to turn.

This is precisely where Brighton differed from most sides who get played through against Arsenal. The usual response is to stay deep once a line has been broken and wait for the shape to settle. Brighton instead pushed up into the next line and used the mid-block to prepare the next jump back into the high press – even when that press had just been beaten. The press therefore had not one starting point per possession, but a steady supply of new ones, and Arsenal had to solve the same pressure several times within a single attack.

Arsenal’s core problem was the absence of a threat in behind. Their attempts to play through the press came without pace beyond the back line, so Brighton were never forced to turn towards their own goal. That made the press cheap: no threat in behind, therefore no need to drop the line. There are three answers to committed man-marking – movement that drags your own marker away and opens space for a third player, a dribble that beats the assignment one-on-one, or a target man who wins the long balls. Arsenal produced none of them.

The Mid-Block as a Fallback

When the high press was beaten, Brighton dropped into a 5-1-4 and inverted the priority. The five in the last line formed a deliberate overload against Arsenal’s front line – in exactly the moment a man-oriented side is most vulnerable, because its players are facing forward while the opponents are already moving. Brighton took the depth out of the situation first and only then thought about the ball again.

A “mid-block” in a 5-1-4 formation. The foremost players protected the “red zone” by using their cover shadows and shifting extremely compactly. Arsenal was mostly forced to play around the block, repeatedly giving Brighton time to reorganize.

A single No. 6 held the connection in front, while the four most advanced players stayed narrow and closed the centre with cover shadows rather than pressing the ball carrier. Arsenal found no central switch on the ball-near side and had to play around the block. Every trip around it cost seconds that Brighton used to shift across, so the end of the route offered the same congestion as the start. In some phases six players stood in the last line.

The block was not a terminus. At every Arsenal back pass Brighton jumped out again and restarted the high press – the same trigger as in the high phase, only from a deeper starting position. The spare man in the last line made that possible, usually Vušković: because the overload left him without a fixed opponent, he could step out, cover the depth and pick up a man himself a moment later. That freed a midfielder to push forward without leaving an assignment open behind him – precisely the point at which sides with a similar approach usually come apart.

In the “mid-block” phase, the “+1” players organized themselves to execute early handovers and jump into pressing situations. Here, Vuskovic—acting as the “+1” player from the defensive line—steps up to pick up the Arsenal player operating in the number 10 position.

On top of that came the trap on the touchline. Once Brighton steered the ball wide, players jumped in from every direction: the ball-near winger closed the route back, the No. 6 covered inside, the back line stepped out. The back pass, the switch and the ball in behind did not disappear one after another but at the same time. Take away only the back pass and a side switches play; take away only the switch and they play backwards and start again. When all that remains is an individual action against several opponents with the touchline at your back, it almost always ends with the opposition.

Transition: Risk Over Retain

After a regain there are two basic options: risk the ball immediately to launch the attack, or secure it to establish order. Hürzeler consistently chose the first, and it follows directly from the overall idea – a secured ball would have given Arsenal exactly the seconds in which their structure reorganises.

When a high press was bypassed, Brighton succeeded in forcing Arsenal into congested situations in the area in front of the back four. In doing so, the players who had been bypassed shifted to block the passing lanes of the players positioned behind the ball. This created additional pressure on the ball carrier, who was simultaneously facing pressure from the front via a “negative” pressing movement (i.e., pressing towards their own goal).

The counter was prepared before the regain. When Arsenal found a player in the No. 6 space after beating the press, Brighton immediately closed his back pass, so the turnover inevitably occurred in a moment when Arsenal were all ahead of the ball. The density around the ball, a defensive necessity, meant in attack that Brighton were central and therefore on the shortest route to goal. The first ball went to the deepest player, followed by a dribble at speed into the box, with no intermediate step and no square pass for safety.

In Possession: Bait Them In, Then Go Over the Top

In the build-up phase, Brighton utilized a diamond shape. De Cuyper and Boscagli engaged in a 2v2 duel on the left flank, while Kostoulas, Gomez, and Kadioglu created a high 3v3 situation on the right. Briefly, both center-backs and a defensive midfielder would spread out to widen the formation. Additional support occasionally came from Groß, dropping back from the number 10 position. Ayari occupied Konsa and Gabriel through constant runs into the open spaces ahead of him.

In deep build-up Brighton baited Arsenal in deliberately. Verbruggen put his foot on the ball while the centre-backs split and lengthened the pressing runs of their opponents. On paper that produced a 3v2 against Arsenal’s first line – except Hürzeler had no intention of playing through that overload on the ground. 25 of 40 goalkeeper passes were long, 62.5 per cent, and they went overwhelmingly to the right.

The structure allowed for a long ball to the right flank, feeding into the 3-3 formation. This created opportunities to contest the second ball or flick on the aerial pass. Additionally, Vuskovic offered a short, low passing option as the free player.

There, Brighton consistently created a 3v3 – an intact structure for the second ball regardless of whether the first contact was won. That is exactly what separates it from an ordinary long ball: the action was not judged by the aerial duel but by the situation that followed it. Kostoulas dropping added to the disorganisation of Arsenal’s high line, because a centre-back had to decide whether to follow him between the lines or let him turn. After winning second balls Brighton played on at high speed and looked for an early shot or an early cross, even against a defensive overload – again the decision for tempo over the clean attack.

Once again, the 3-3 structure on the right flank. Here, from open play.

On the left Brighton worked through pinning rather than through length. Boscagli stepping inside dragged Saka into central areas and took him out of the wide zone Arsenal needed him in defensively, while De Cuyper dropped and offered the mid-range diagonal once the assignments had been pulled apart. The asymmetry was a personnel decision: the players for second balls were on the right, the players who could drag markers out of position on the left. Arsenal would have had to condense against one side and hand over assignments cleanly against the other.

The team’s build-up play was asymmetrical, featuring a high, wide approach on the right contrasted with the left flank: Boscagli often played an inverted role—supplementing the existing diamond formation—which opened up space for de Cuyper. Meanwhile, Groß and Kostoulas occupied the players on Arsenal’s right defensive flank, enabling Brighton to find low-ball solutions against the press.

In Midfield: Stretching the Block

With the ball in midfield there were two patterns. The first was the deliberate back pass to Verbruggen to pull Arsenal’s block out, followed by the same long-short structure as in deep build-up. The back pass was not a safety option here but the tool that created the space behind the pressing line in the first place.

The second pattern worked through central and wide tension at high speed, supplemented by rotations within their own structure. Arsenal repeatedly failed to hand over their pressing assignments in a coordinated way against it – the same weakness Brighton had solved cleanly in their own press. In possession Brighton’s shape approached a 4-3-3: Chema Andrés as the deepest No. 6, Groß with freedom from the left half-space, Kadıoğlu pushing up early to support Gómez, Boscagli starting slightly deeper on the left.

The Goals as Proof of the Idea

The 1-0 by Groß in the 31st minute is the whole plan in one sequence. Brighton play back to the goalkeeper from midfield, go long, lose the first contact and win the second ball. After a phase of circulation the next attack meets a back line that has already been destabilised: Kostoulas drops, Arsenal’s midfield and Konsa step out, and Kostoulas lays the ball back against the direction of the pressure to Groß. Because Ayari runs in behind at the same time, Arsenal’s defenders drop – and Groß has the time for the shot from around 20 metres. In the build-up Brighton had used rotations to drag Guimarães over to the left.

The opening goal stems from a positional attack. Kostoulas spots open space and makes a run into it. With a pass played across his standing leg, he feeds the ball to Groß in the space between the lines. As Groß takes a few quick touches and dribbles toward the defensive line, Gabriel and Calafiori drop back together, thereby opening up a shooting lane.

The 2-0 by Kostoulas shortly before half-time follows the same logic from the opposite direction. After a set piece of their own, Brighton clear an Arsenal counter and transition again immediately, 4v5 in Arsenal’s half, and once more the shot comes from distance while the centre-backs drop off.

Much like the goal that made it 1–0, both defenders went to ground near the ball carrier, thereby opening up space for the goalscorer—this time arising from a transition situation.

Both goals share the same cause: Arsenal’s centre-backs stepped out too late to press the man between the lines. React only when the shooting motion starts and you cannot get into the block from 20 to 25 metres. What is striking is that this passivity is the same one Arsenal showed in their own build-up – a side that does not leave its position to offer itself does not leave it to close down a shooter either.

The 3-0 by Chema Andrés came from a corner: Groß delivered from the left, Brighton had created an overload at the far post and protected it with two blocks, and Chema rose completely unmarked to head in off the inside of the post.

Second Half: No Game Management

Two goals up, many sides would have defended deeper. Brighton came out with unchanged and at times higher pressing intensity, and the coordination was visibly drilled: marking assignments were swapped cleanly even in dynamic situations, and Arsenal’s structural adjustments were absorbed.

Arsenal had managed a few organised possession phases in Brighton’s half in the first period; in the second it became harder. In those phases they built with a back three, Rice as the third centre-back, Ødegaard dropping alongside Guimarães and the full-backs occupying the half-spaces. Arteta reacted around the hour: Zubimendi and Merino for Timber and Guimarães, with Rice moving to right-back, followed shortly by Gyökeres and Eze for Ødegaard and Tzolis. There was no noticeable effect, because the problem did not lie in the shape but in the assignments in front of it.

There was also a layer that usually goes missing in analysis: Brighton used tactical fouls deliberately to stop Arsenal finding a rhythm. Arteta admitted afterwards that the game had been “very difficult to control”, pointing to lost first and second balls and to Brighton’s directness.

Conclusion

Arteta’s Arsenal being outplayed this clearly is rare. That it happened proactively and aggressively is rarer still – most sides who take something from Arsenal do it with a low block and efficiency. Hürzeler deliberately stepped back afterwards and praised his players’ courage, which gets to the heart of it: the plan only works if eleven players are willing to carry it through in every single sequence.

The interesting question is where the idea came from. Chelsea were a common opponent: Brighton had 74 per cent possession there and lost 3-4 against a counter-attacking side – control right down to the numbers, without return. Alonso’s Chelsea tried the opposite route against Arsenal, a deliberate surrender of control, also without success. The likely derivation lies between the two: neither give up control nor chase it, but generate the unsettled game state yourself and be prepared for it.

There is no reason for panic at Arsenal, but Arteta will need answers against a high press. The build-up was predictable for long stretches, and above all the direct option with pace in behind was missing as a threat. As long as that does not exist, a man-oriented press costs very little against this side.

For Brighton the open question is repeatability. Man-marking of this commitment, collective recovery runs with cover shadows and a touchline trap that requires everyone to trigger at once are hard to produce twice in an English week. Whether the plan works was answered on this afternoon. How often it can be produced will decide the season.

There are precious few food products that have been enjoyed for as much of human history as Prosciutto di Parma. The signature silky texture and buttery, sweet-savory flavor of the “King of Hams” is recognized around the world as the result of carefully controlled production methods, dedication to quality, and traditions continued for centuries.

The history of Prosciutto di Parma reaches back more than 2,000 years, to Roman times, when the art of preserving pork through salting and natural drying was already well established in the Po Valley. As early as the 3rd century BC, Cato the Elder described methods for curing pork legs with salt and allowing them to dry and mature. Today, Prosciutto di Parma is produced exclusively in a strictly defined area of northern Italy, in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region, where local know-how combines with distinctive climatic conditions. Humid breezes from the Tyrrhenian Sea become drier as they flow over the Apennines and down onto the hills. Prosciutto producers discovered that this created an ideal environment for dry aging, and they built storerooms with tall windows that could be opened and closed as necessary, allowing the air to circulate through the racks of hams for gradual drying.

The specifics of aging, like every step in the process, are governed by a consortium—the Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma—that was founded in 1963 to protect the authenticity of the product. In 1996, Prosciutto di Parma obtained the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO). These have a red and yellow seal to show that a product is made in a certain geographical area using time-honored, traditional methods. Grana Padano, a nutty, aged hard cheese also from Northern Italy that’s often paired with Prosciutto di Parma on a charcuterie plate, was awarded a PDO the same year.

Grana Padano PDO and Prosciutto di Parma PDO also share the distinction of being fire-branded—a sign for consumers that you are buying the real thing. Only Parma hams that meet the PDO’s specifications can receive the five-pronged Ducal Crown that signifies a true Prosciutto di Parma. 

Well before that brand is applied, production starts with the pigs. Which breeds, where they can be bred and raised (in 11 Italian regions), and what they are fed (a list that also includes whey from local cheesemakers) are all precisely detailed. Within the 28th day from birth, the piglets receive a breeder tattoo that will also appear on the final product along with the crown brand. The fresh pork (hind legs only) sent to prosciutto producers must weigh between 26 pounds and 40 pounds, with a set amount of fat coverage. After they arrive at the production facility, the legs are trimmed to form the characteristic rounded shape, chilled to a precise temperature, and salted by hand with sea salt, which joins the pork as the only two ingredients that go into the making of Prosciutto di Parma PDO.

Following a resting period of at least 90 days, the hams are washed then hung in windowed storerooms to dry slowly. The next step is called sugnatura: The parts of the ham not covered by rind are rubbed with sugna—a mixture of pork fat, salt, and pepper—to keep the meat from drying too quickly. For final aging, the hams hang in cool, dimly lit cellars. From start to finish, the process cannot last less than 14 months (many producers continue aging for 24, 30, or even 36 months and beyond). The last step before inspection may be the most fascinating: The hams are tested using a needle made from horse bone, selected because the bone is able to absorb the aroma and quickly shed it. By inserting the needle into different parts of the cured meat and smelling it, experts can determine if a whole ham leg has the qualifications to be named Prosciutto di Parma PDO. 

The Consorzio, which started with 23 producers, now numbers 125, and in 2025 they turned out 6.75 million Prosciutto di Parma PDO hams. Beyond its economic significance to the region, the “King of Hams” is a cultural icon and a tourism magnet. The annual Parma Ham Festival is held in September, and there’s even an entire museum dedicated to the region’s signature product—with a tasting room, of course. 

However you experience the exceptional flavor and texture of Prosciutto di Parma—on a visit to the museum or from your local specialty store—look for the crown and PDO seal, and take a moment to remember that you are tasting a distinctly delicious slice of history.

Quick Summary

  1. The drill sergeant version of accountability (“why did you skip that? do you even want this?”) questions YOU instead of helping you. If being hard on yourself worked, it would have worked by now.
  2. Real accountability is built on two things: forward momentum (get the next available win, then look ahead) and honest feedback (a neutral observer reading the evidence, not a judge handing out punishment).
  3. You can run it on yourself: win first, plan the rest of the week, then look back at what’s working and what’s not.

Here’s what that looks like inside real coaching conversations.

When most people picture “accountability,” they picture a drill sergeant.

Someone standing over you going “What were you thinking? Why did you skip that? Do you even want this?“

(And if there’s no drill sergeant handy, we do a pretty good job of beating ourselves up all on our own.)

Here’s the thing: if being hard on yourself worked, it would have worked by now.

Great accountability isn’t built on being a hard-ass.

It’s built on forward momentum and honest feedback.

The drill sergeant questions YOU. Your motivation. How much you want it.

Real accountability helps you try again quickly.

Then helps you see what you’re doing well and what can improve.

Here’s what that looks like:

Forward momentum

A client of mine had a hectic week. Travel, out of their routine, workouts missed.

The drill sergeant move is “why didn’t you do your workouts?!” at the exact moment they were already struggling.

Here’s what I sent instead:

“Hey! I know this has been a hectic week. Let’s do some push-ups together. Do a set next chance you get and shoot me a . I’ll do one too.”

This got a and a “Thanks for the encouragement! This week has been so hectic my schedule got away from me.”

That win got them back on the routine.

Then we build off of that by looking ahead to the next few days.

“How’s the rest of this week looking for strength training? I went ahead and put together a few variations in case you’re short on time.”

When you’re already struggling, the question isn’t “hey, why didn’t you do that?”

It’s getting the next available win.

Then looking ahead to what’s coming up after that.

Honest feedback

But forward momentum is only the first part of the equation.

The second step is giving honest feedback on both the wins AND the challenges.

That’s my job as a coach.

To be a neutral observer. To point out what’s going well, what’s not, and suggest how we can improve it moving forward.

Based on the actual human in front of me: their unique goals, motivations, and whatever flavor of chaos they have going on in their own life (work, family, travel, illness, injury, etc.)

Here’s a recent example:

“Hey! I reviewed the strength workout on Friday. Solid work all around here. I can see from your form check video that you’re doing a better job staying balanced across your whole foot on the squat.

I also noticed that the middle of the week has been tougher to get consistent workouts in. You’ve hit 2 out of the last 4 midweek workouts. I know work has been crazy with the project deadline you have coming up, so my guess is you’re getting buried and it makes it hard to get to the gym. Am I on the right track? I have some ideas for how we tackle that next week that I’ll drop down below in a video for you.”

This is the honest part. It didn’t go anywhere!

But instead of berating you for missing a workout, the goal is to look at all the evidence and suggest next steps.

Including if the evidence says that something BIG needs to change. It’s all on the table.

Accountability isn’t punishment for what you didn’t do.

It’s helping you decide what you do next.

Here’s how you can try it on your own

If your week went haywire, before you beat yourself up, take this approach:

  • First, get your next win. A set of push-ups. A 10-minute walk. The short version of your workout. Give yourself the . Then ask “how’s the rest of the week looking?” Plan ahead instead of fixating on what you missed.
  • Then, once you’ve collected some data, look back like a neutral observer. What’s working? What’s not? Not “I’m a piece of crap for missing my workout.” Instead, “I had a really hard time getting my workout in when the kids have after school activities.” That’s something we can work with.
