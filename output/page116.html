Noah Baker isn’t your typical young hunter. The Pike County, Missouri youngster is a hunting prodigy most sportsmen would be proud to call their son.

“He shot his first deer at age six,” his dad, Jared Baker, tells Outdoor Life. “He lives to hunt and fish. And we’d have to think about just how many deer he’s taken since that first one when he was still in first grade.”

October 25 was the last day of the Missouri early two-day youth gun deer season. Noah and Jared chose a hunting spot over a cut corn field late that afternoon near their home in Frankfort.

“The wind wasn’t great, and we just set up in a hillside haystack that overlooked a cut corn patch that was well below us,” says Jared, a 37-year-old heavy equipment operator. “We set a tripod rifle stand in the haystack, put Noah’s 7mm suppressed Remington rifle on it, and watched and waited.”

They got on the haystack about 4 p.m. on a hot afternoon. They hadn’t waited very long before a few small bucks and does started filtering into the corn to feed about 60 yards from where they were positioned.

“We were waiting for a big buck we knew was around that I named ‘Dwayne The Rock Johnson’, because he was so big and strong,” Noah says. “The deer’s head was huge, and I wanted him.”

Photo courtesy of Jared Baker

The father-son team had two big target bucks they’d watched and planned to take this year. They’d also found shed antlers from the deer. Noah had shot the smaller of the two bucks — a 155-inch 10-pointer — with his crossbow on Oct. 2.

Now they were after the second buck – “Dwayne The Rock Johnson,” and it just so happened that the deer showed at 6:20 p.m.

“It happened fast,” Jared says. “We saw him at 80 yards, and he went into a grass patch to look at a doe.”

“I saw him first, dad was looking farther away when I spotted him,” says Noah. “Dwayne started to walk away, turning toward some smaller bucks. But dad used his grunt call, and the buck turned and looked at us.”

Noah had crosshairs settled on the deer’s chest and fired at the buck standing 80 yards away. The 140-grain Berger bullet put the buck down quickly.

“Noah and I just started fist pumping in the air,” said Jared.

Most hikers have experienced a natural high at some point in their outdoor adventures. Whether it’s the buzz of an athletic flow state or being straight-up stupefied by an amazing view, we’ve all been there. Endorphins fire, oxytocin floods the brain, and you’re overwhelmed with energy, good vibes, and a general sense of peace.

It’s also not news to anyone that some hikers use drugs in nature. People smoke weed on the trail, eat edibles, drink beer, and even take psychedelics. Every year it seems like we publish at least one story with a headline like, “Hikers Rescued After Mushroom Trip Goes Bad” or “Two Hikers Reported Their Friend Dead. It Turned Out They Were Just on Mushrooms.”

But how many hikers are actually hitting the trail under the influence? Those questions and several others led the team at Climbing Kilimanjaro, a Tanzanian guide company, to conduct its “Hiking While High” survey. It polled U.S. adults who hike to find out once and for all how many of them use cannabis, mushrooms, alcohol, and other substances, and importantly, in what circumstances.

The Climbing Kilimanjaro team (Photo: Climbing Kilimanjaro)

The results surprised the folks at Climbing Kilimanjaro. Not just because of how many hikers admitted to using substances on the trail, but because those who do are “surprisingly disciplined about where and when,” Brian Raffio, Kilimanjaro consultant for Climbing Kilimanjaro, says.

“On one hand, we expected to get some respondents who were very into using substances while hiking, and others who were as sober as sober gets,” Raffio says. “What we ended up with was much more of a grey area.”

For the survey, Climbing Kilimanjaro polled 1,008 U.S. adults between September 1 and 3, 2026. The sample was 52 percent women and 46 percent men, all of whom counted hiking as a core interest or hobby. They also grouped respondents by experience level. Hikers self-reported as casual hikers (people who hike a few times a year), regular hikers (people who hike roughly monthly), or avid hikers (people who hike weekly or more).

The Results

Of the 1,008 U.S. hikers surveyed, 44 percent said they have hiked under the influence.

Cannabis is far and away the most popular trail substance. A notable 35 percent of respondents admitted to having used it while hiking. Of hikers who take anything, 80 percent said they are reaching for weed. Behind cannabis, alcohol was the second most popular, with 23 percent percent admitting to having drunk beer or liquor while hiking. In third were magic mushrooms, with 15 percent admitting use (and another 24 percent saying they are curious to try psychedelics in nature but never have).

(Photo: Climbing Kilimanjaro)

However, a majority—56 percent of hikers—said they have never used any substances on the trail. And while 7 percent of hikers who enhance their hikes say they have felt judged for doing so, surprisingly more of the sober hikers (9 percent) said they have also felt judged, but for their choice to abstain.

“Hikers feeling judged for staying clear-headed rather than for using something was a bit of a head-scratcher for sure,” Raffio says. “Apparently ‘no thanks, I’m good’ raises a few eyebrows.”

What surprised the Climbing Kilimanjaro team even more wasn’t these reported use rates. Instead, it was the level of caution users claim to have when they’re hiking high. More than half (51 percent) said they seek out shorter, easier hikes, and would never try to hike under the influence at high altitude. And nine out of 10 who hiked under the influence said: “There’s at least one place they would never use a substance.”

(Photo: Climbing Kilimanjaro)

“In the end, hikers seem to be a fairly self-policing bunch,” Raffio says. “The summit push is where folks want to stay sharp and have their senses fully alert. Whatever [substance] a hiker packs, the trail rewards you when you know the terrain, and you understand your limits.”

The final result that stuck out to Raffio was how many people use hiking to recover. Roughly half of the hikers surveyed said they hit the trail sometimes “to reset after drinking or a night out.”

The Takeaways

According to the Outdoor Industry Association (OIA), there are roughly 63 million hikers in the U.S. That makes this sample size anything but definitive. The most obvious takeaway is that people like to enhance their hikes with substances—whether it’s cannabis, magic mushrooms, or something else.

Climbing Kilimanjaro’s results also suggest that hikers are fairly split when it comes to substance use on the trail. While the majority of the respondents had never used anything while hiking, a 56/44 split isn’t that wide of a margin.

For those who do partake, it’s clear (and unsurprising) that cannabis is hikers’ substance of choice—by a long shot.

The Hiking While High survey didn’t poll hikers on their feelings around the topic, and that was intentional. The guide company didn’t want to present opinionated data. It just wanted to open up an honest conversation around the rates of drug use on the trail.

“We asked ourselves: What if this was discussed more openly?” Raffio said. “We wanted to come from a place of curiosity, not judgement.”

Remote code execution on Linux systems with multipath TCP

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

On October 27, LLM provider Anthropic reported on a remotely exploitable use-after-free bug affecting systems with multipath TCP listeners open. The bug works by triggering a response to a retransmitted SYN packet at the same moment that the TCP connection is established in another thread, causing the first thread to access the connection's options structure after the second thread has freed it. The first thread can then disclose kernel memory or cause a buffer overflow, writing an attacker-controlled payload. The fix was to only free the structure after an RCU grace period. On October 29, the Go security team advised that all programs written in Go use multipath TCP by default, so many systems may be unknowingly vulnerable. And, of course, there is always the possibility of related bugs in other sections of the TCP stack. Users may be wise to update to a patched kernel as soon as one becomes available for their distribution.

The Met Museum and Vacheron Constantin Unveil Artworks Produced Through New Residency

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Earlier this month at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the three artists chosen for the Artisan Residency Programme, a collaboration between the museum and Vacheron Constantin, unveiled the works that each has created through the program.

The Swiss luxury watch company launched the 18-month residency program with the Met in May 2025, making the third such partnership for the company. (The company also has partnerships with the Louvre, dating to 2019, and the Palace Museum in Beijing, established in 2024.)

The program is meant to pass on, preserve, and reinterpret traditional craft techniques. As part of the program, artists spent considerable time at both the Met and the Vacheron Constantin Manufacture in Geneva, and then used those experiences as launching points for their own works. The three artists selected were Aspen Golann, Ibrahim Said, and Joy Harvey.

For the residency, Golann produced a monumental wooden vessel adorned with nature motifs carved in bas relief. For the piece she drew inspiration from a series of wooden chests from the 17th to the 19th century in Connecticut and Pennsylvania. Those works and other similar ones in the Met’s collection exemplified a neoclassical New England style that informed her work, titled Vessel. During her time at the Manufacture, Golann worked with artists on enameling and engraving techniques.

Said’s work, Shaping Time, is a three-tiered vertical sculpture also adorned by relief carvings, as well as piercings and glazed surfaces that draw upon ancient Egyptian and Islamic objects in the Met’s collection. At the Manufacture, Said also worked with the artisans on enameling and engraving, as well as lacquer painting.

Harvey’s Ruined splendour is a bronze diadem composed of forged rhomboid bronze interlaced with panels inspired by the fine filigree seen on ancient Greek necklaces. After creation the work was pierced with rods in order to create its “ruination.” For the work, Harvey drew on ancient jewelry in the Met collection from Greece, India, and China, and then spent time in the Manufacture learning guillochage, a technique for engraving intricate patterns like leaves and flowers onto metal.

In a statement, Met CEO Max Hollein called the program “a collaborative initiative that embodies our mutual commitment to supporting artistic innovation and cultural dialogue.”

With less than a month left until the 61st Venice Biennale closes on November 22, the United States pavilion commissioner Jenni Parido has pleaded for additional donations to ship Alma Allen's sculptures out of Italy.

This week, the New York Times reviewed a letter by Parido, who founded the American Arts Conservancy (AAC) and commissioned Allen's exhibition in Venice. She told potential donors that the Trump administration-aligned arts nonprofit was “facing an urgent funding need associated with the final deinstallation and safe return of the artworks to the United States.”

The time-sensitive fundraising effort falls in line with Parido's financial model for this year's Biennale presentation. After months of opacity surrounding the selection process and funding ventures for the pavilion, Parido told Hyperallergic in April that Allen's exhibition, Call Me the Breeze, received no “corporate or foundation funding,” and that it was facilitated through the “generous support of private citizens who believe in the importance of American artists and cultural exchange.” The AAC also had an online donations portal to help fund the pavilion ahead of the Biennale's opening in May.
