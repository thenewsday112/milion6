A Comeback? No, Dane Tudor Is in His "Fourth Prime"

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Somewhere in Colorado, hundreds of miles away, Dane Tudor held an umbrella aloft and peered down at me.

He strolled about and patiently answered my many questions, a gray sky creeping into the edge of the frame behind him. I’d signed in to the video call to talk about his new movie, The Fourth Prime, but first I wanted to figure out where he’d been the past few seasons. The answer? Still skiing.

Now 37, the Rossland, British Columbia, and Palmer, Alaska, raised skier lived through and embodied a rapid evolution in snow sports. Skis got fat, twin tips went big, and techy tricks started showing up everywhere. Tudor did it all. 

People continually refer back to his 2009 Everyday Is a Saturday segment with Poor Boyz Productions, a stunning mishmash of everything that was—and still is—sick in freeskiing. To kids of a certain generation, Tudor was one of those figures: above reproach in the style department yet more than handy on skis when he wasn’t flying through the air.

Pillows and flips. Two of Dane’s specialties.

Koover Schmidt

After winning the iF3 Standout Male Skier of the Year award in 2022, though, Tudor parted ways with his sponsor, Scott, and went, for a time, without brand support. He last appeared in a ski movie for Blank Collective’s Feel Real, which, confoundingly, also earned him the iF3 award. You’d think that would be worth a contract or three. 

But it isn’t uncommon for gifted skiers to fade in and out of the film scene. There are only so many brand dollars to go around, talent keeps skyrocketing, and social media has completely changed the ski movie- and magazine-driven environment Tudor came up in. During our chat, he wondered aloud if not knowing how to crack the algorithm held him back. 

While he’d grown tired of pitching sponsors, the same wasn’t true of skiing. Tudor kept ripping around his home mountain, Red, and, though he didn’t want the “break” from being a professional, he found inspiration in the downtime.

“I was able to really think about my philosophy in skiing and work on the next phase of my riding style,” Tudor said, his umbrella hovering in the background. “Rethinking everything from how I ride to what is challenging and what is pushing the limits or boundaries, and how to balance all those things while working on mastering the craft into the future.”

Last winter wasn’t great, apparently?

Koover Schmidt

The Fourth Prime is part of that evolution. With help from the athlete management crew Pangea Collective, Tudor recently signed two new sponsors, Fischer Skis and the outerwear company Nivis. That unlocked the budget he needed, and last winter Tudor shot with longtime ski filmmaker Jeff Thomas. Fellow skier and up-and-comer Jonathan Rollins coined the film’s name. While building a jump together last winter, Rollins kept saying Tudor was now in his “fourth prime.” It stuck.

“The project means a lot because I’m still in the game and I still get to enjoy the backcountry and ride with friends and practice my style and create content,” Tudor said. “It’s just that creative process that’s so fulfilling.”

Given the duration of Tudor’s career—he blew up at 19—you could probably slice it into any number of “primes.” He’s filmed street, pillow lines, and big mountain, plus covered most of the snow in between. Tudor admitted that he didn’t know what Rollins’ breakdown of the “primes” was. His assessment, though, was in part guided by the ski movie production outfits he worked with, starting with Poor Boyz, bouncing between Teton Gravity Research and Sherpas Cinema, and ending with Blank. Well, not quite.

“Now this is the fourth prime, which is pretty cool,” Tudor said.

The trailer is packed with his usual wizardry: dubs, hefty backcountry jumps, and capped blunt grabs. It might come as a surprise, then, that when I asked Tudor about his highlights from filming The Fourth Prime, he nodded to a jibbier, lower-impact trick: the humble but flexible butter. 

One flavor of the move appears early in The Fourth Prime when he locks into an extended butter 360. Perfecting it is, in a way, emblematic of the direction he wants to go. A fat backflip is mind-blowing, sure. But a well-executed butter—no small feat, mind you—can also get the neurons firing. 

“That’s how I’ve been working on progressing my skiing: how can I do things differently than everybody else and make it look cool while not taking massive risk?” he said.

Playing it smart and getting inverted aren’t mutually exclusive.

Koover Schmidt

For Tudor, it slots into a broader approach: squeezing the most out of his environment. Or, turning less into more. Last winter royally sucked for much of the West. That impacted Tudor, who regularly dealt with rain and the crusty layers it brought while filming The Fourth Prime. In the past, understandably, Tudor always wanted the perfect conditions. But he said he’s gotten better at tailoring his skiing to what the mountains provide.

Choosing those features wisely and not taking chances, along with the usual maintenance hacks—eating well, going to the gym, swimming laps—is Tudor’s strategy for staying healthy. He’s had only one season-ending injury: a hip dislocation in 2013. Tudor thinks that’s a product of his careful approach, though he also cited another reason he stays away from the burliest stunts: nerves.

“I have a certain level of conservatism in my riding. I’m not a total wild guy,” he said. “I do battle with more internal fear than maybe some other guys do … I think that’s part of what’s helped me along the way.”

Fear for Tudor and fear for the average skier look a little different. In the spring, Tudor ventured to Blackcomb’s imposing terrain park alongside Kye Petersen. He wanted to revitalize his freestyle skills, getting them close to what they were in 2012, when Tudor’s time spent training in the park coalesced. That involved going upside down twice. Then, on one air, he slipped up, missing his pop, landing sideways, and twisting his ankle pretty good. He offered the moment as an example. It showed that even when you’re careful, mistakes can happen. 

Outside of skiing, Tudor works for the bike park company Gravity Logic, but he’s also into trading, buying and selling stocks. In trading, success isn’t just about “win rate,” the percentage of trades that turn a profit. It’s also about managing risk and avoiding catastrophic losses. Tudor has found that the same principle applies to skiing.

“You can take many small hits and stay in the game,” he said, drawing the comparison. “But if you go massive and you blow up a couple times big, that could be the end of it.”

As of this fall, it’s fair to say Tudor’s in the green, with the latest video—his first in years—premiering across North America on Pangea’s Freeride Film Tour. He doesn’t take this second round in front of the lens for granted. Whenever he gets the chance to shoot, he said, he gives it everything because he doesn’t know if it’ll happen again—the ski industry remains fickle, after all, perhaps more than ever. 

Still, Tudor spoke excitedly about the momentum around The Fourth Prime, and is hatching plans with filmmakers to make another movie happen this season. He’s even considering, if only in a distant way, getting back into the streets. However the future looks, Tudor’s having fun. That isn’t contingent on the ski career thing.

“I ski the way I do because I enjoy that,” he said, adding that he’ll continue venturing into the mountains regardless. “I grew up as a passionate skier—it’s what I love to do.”

On Thursday night, the smear at the heart of the mysterious transgender mailers that have rocked the final weeks of the midterm elections in Texas completed its journey. What began as baseless social media whispers quickly ascended the ladder of conservative influence, jumped into mailboxes across the Lone Star State and ultimately into the comforting prime-time embrace of Fox News.

At a town hall in Fort Worth, a Texas mother of six named Rebekah Smith told Vice President JD Vance that her 11-year-old daughter had received a letter explaining how to transition without her parents ever knowing, and asked how Republicans would keep the government out of her family. “I don’t know who sent that letter to your child,” the vice president said, “but I think the person who sends a letter to an 11-year-old about gender transition, what I call that in my family is a pervert, and that person ought to go to prison.” Host Jesse Watters, never one to let a grievance go to waste, promised that Fox would investigate “this perverted letter.” Vance asked Smith to talk to his staff because he would “like the Department of Justice to investigate that” as well.

Watters rushed to promote the spectacle. “WHOA! Texas MOM tells JD Vance her DAUGHTER received a letter telling her TO TRANSITION TO A BOY,” he posted on X, followed by Vance’s promise. Missing from that framing was the question that should have governed the segment: Who actually sent the material?

Nobody on that stage mentioned that the pamphlet was a fake. Nobody mentioned that the real transgender advocacy group named inside it had disavowed the mailer. Nobody mentioned that sworn testimony filed that same week had tied the text messages that accompanied it to a national Republican organization. The vice president was asking federal prosecutors to chase a forgery that may well have originated in his own party’s orbit — and he was doing it on a network whose viewers had been marinating in that forgery for weeks.

The race between Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton and Democratic challenger James Talarico has become a crucible for dark-money maneuvers. As polls show the Republican grip on the Lone Star State slipping, conservative campaigns and their surrogates have resorted to increasingly desperate tactics. In early October, for example, Talarico took a brief hiatus from the campaign trail to recover from a severe case of the flu. Rather than accepting a routine human illness, the right-wing echo chamber treated a temporary absence as evidence of mental illness. Within days, the fabricated scandal has collapsed — yet the machinery of right-wing media simply pivoted to amplifying a more insidious operation designed to trigger visceral outrage.

By flooding the electorate with extreme material disguised as progressive advocacy, right-wing operatives appear to have sought to create a fake controversy that could be weaponized in attack ads and campaign speeches. Vance, for instance, told the Fox News audience that Talarico has “a weird fascination with trans kids,” and that “he’ll probably defend the right of the person sending the letter, not the right of the parent who is rightfully creeped out by this.” Vance claimed that Talarico “has actually called it abusive when parents try to prevent their children from transitioning” and that Paxton “has fought hard to prosecute those people, whereas James Talarico has fought hard to protect those people.” Even the distortion had been laundered by then — Talarico called denying a child’s gender identity abusive, a different claim from saying parents must permit medical transition, but at the top of the ladder the difference no longer exists.

That is how a smear works in the closed ecosystem of the American right. It does not need to survive scrutiny. It just needs to circulate upward. An anonymous account posts it; an influencer adopts it; a donor-funded outlet reports the influencer’s outrage as news; an elected official demands an investigation, and the demand becomes proof the story matters; cable news books the controversy; a vice president promises federal action. Each rung launders the one below it. The correction, when it comes, travels in the opposite direction and arrives nowhere.

The paper trail looks like what you would expect from a covert operation. Texas Transgender Student Union LLC was formed in Wyoming on Sept. 2 through a commercial registered agent that helps owners stay anonymous. After CBS News Texas visited the agent’s address in Sheridan, Wyoming, the agent announced it was resigning. The mailers listed a Houston-area post office box. The Texas Observer reported that the barcode on the envelopes traced to a mail-tracking company that has taken federal campaign money from only one group this year, the National Republican Congressional Committee. The committee said it had nothing to do with the mailers, and the company would not say who paid. CBS News Texas reported that the bulk-mail permit belongs to an Illinois printer owned by the Zerrusen family, who have given heavily to Republican PACs and been photographed with Donald Trump.

When the real Transgender Education Network of Texas filed a petition in Travis County court to unmask the perpetrators behind the impersonation campaign, the partisan safety nets of the Texas judiciary eventually snapped into action. A deposition of Infobip, the messaging company that delivered the texts, which a Travis County judge ordered made public, indicated that the texts were sent on behalf of the Republican Women’s Caucus Fund and a man named John Braun. But on Friday, the Texas Supreme Court, composed entirely of judges appointed by Republican Governor Greg Abbott, issued an administrative stay that blocked its release. Chris Hilton, an attorney who previously served under Paxton, defended him during his impeachment trial and was later pushed out of office amid sexual harassment allegations, argued on behalf of the company that the mailers were not political speech because they named no candidate or party.

Dirty tricks are an old tradition of American politics. This cycle, Republican PACs in Nevada mailed a flyer calling a nonpartisan candidate, Danielle Ford, the “AOC of Nevada,” hoping voters would mistake her for the actual Democratic nominee, Aaron Ford. This week also brought word that the group that funded Cornel West’s 2024 ballot drives raised every dollar through a dark-money network bankrolled by Elon Musk.

The president’s party whines about decoy candidates in Alaska while running them at home. Ratf**king is a long and proud Republican tradition, and the Texas operation fits inside perfectly.

Arsenal news: Arsenal vs Liverpool. Arteta targets Anfield record as Liverpool prepare for Premier League clash

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Arsenal vs Liverpool: Arsenal travel to Anfield on Sunday looking to maintain their strong start to the Premier League season and end a long wait for victory at Liverpool’s home ground.

A blistering start to the season has seen Mikel Arteta’s side win seven of their first eight league games, leaving them tied on points with Manchester City at the top of the table and six points ahead of Liverpool going into the game.

However, the Gunners have not beaten Liverpool in the Premier League at Anfield since 2012/13. Arteta will be aware that the Reds are starting to gel under Andoni Iraola.

They have won three of their last four league games after a lacklustre start and had their first league win at home this season with their 2-0 win over Brighton.

Arsenal face Liverpool side finding its attacking rhythm

After replacing Arne Slot over the summer, Iraola brought his impressive three-year spell at Bournemouth to Anfield. However, Liverpool have struggled to adjust to the new manager, losing 3 of their first 4 league games.

But they have recently improved and are looking to play a much faster style of football. As Adrian Clarke notes, they are pressing more, not sitting back as much and wanting to attack quickly when they win the ball.

They have been the most direct attackers in the Premier League. They have pace to spare within their attack, with players like Alexander Isak and Cody Gakpo providing a threat in advanced areas.

Isak comes into the game in good form after finding the back of the net six times in his first eight league games. He has scored in three successive games in the Premier League for the first time since joining the club.

Florian Wirtz will also play a key role in Liverpool’s attacking play. The German has been consistently creating chances for the team playing behind Isak in a 4-2-3-1 system. He has created nine chances against Brentford.

Arsenal will need to limit the space available to Liverpool’s forward players. Allowing Isak and Gakpo to combine freely could make an already difficult away fixture even harder.

Arteta receives positive Arsenal injury news

Arsenal have had some positive news on their squad ahead of their Merseyside trip. Declan Rice and Riccardo Calafiori are both back in training.

Christos Tzolis has also trained after missing October. Noni Madueke has also not been ruled out by Arteta ahead of Sunday’s match after he missed the Fleetwood game.

They also have their own fitness issues to contend with. Iraola confirmed that Dominik Szoboszlai and Victor Munoz are both available after suffering injuries earlier in the month.

Jeramie Frimpong will be missing due to a hamstring issue but should be back after the international break. Hugo Ekitike, Geovanni Leoni and Conor Bradley are all still sidelined with longer-term injuries.

The presence of Rice will be key for Arsenal. He has been one of the best progressive midfielders in the Premier League, along with their captain, Martin Odegaard.

Arsenal aim to end lengthy Anfield winless run

Despite losing just one of their last eight Premier League clashes against Liverpool, Arsenal are not as strong on the road.

The Gunners have not won any league games in their last 13 trips to Anfield. They have drawn five and lost eight. Their last win was a 2-0 victory back in the 2012/13 season.

They have also never gone 14 games unbeaten away from home in the league. That would be a great achievement.

However, there is good reason to believe they can do it. They have only conceded five clean sheets in the league this season. They have faced the least number of shots on target and the fewest expected goals.

Liverpool have also been on a strong run defensively with four clean sheets in their last six league games, so Arsenal must make their chances count.

A win would provide another key result against a direct rival, improving their position at the top of the Premier League table and finally putting an end to one of the clubs’ longest-running away day woes.

About Supabase

Supabase is the Postgres development platform, built by developers for developers. We provide a complete backend solution including Database, Auth, Storage, Edge Functions, Realtime, and Vector Search. All services are deeply integrated and designed for growth.

About the Role

We’re looking for a Product Manager, Administration & Governance to join our Product Team and make Supabase the default starting point for software built inside a company.

Supabase already spreads from the bottom up: someone has an idea, creates a project, and builds something useful. Inside most companies, Supabase starts the same way: someone has an idea, creates a project, and builds something useful. The next step is making that momentum company-wide: one platform where anyone can start building, and where IT can see what’s running, who owns it, and what it costs.

You’ll own how companies run Supabase as their internal development platform: approved by IT and easy for anyone in the company to use. You’ll make it possible for people and the agents they work with to start building without waiting on infrastructure, while giving platform, IT, and security teams the controls they need to say yes. The goal is to make the approved path the easiest path, from an early experiment to a production application, with the controls companies need built in.

What You’ll Be Responsible For

  • Own the product strategy and roadmap for internal innovation, from a company’s first IT-approved rollout to adoption across teams and business units.

  • Make getting started self-service, whether someone creates a project in Supabase, through an internal developer portal, or with an agent. Give builders the access and infrastructure they need without a ticket for every project.

  • Steer scattered usage into a manageable company-wide platform. Give companies one view of everything they run on Supabase. Shape how ownership, delegated access, and boundaries work so teams can move independently as usage grows.

  • Partner closely with platform, IT, security, and finance teams to understand what it takes to approve Supabase and keep it approved. Translate their needs into practical identity, policy, audit, and spend controls that don’t slow builders down.

  • Define the path from experiment to production, including project ownership, access, and the guardrails that change as an application becomes important to the business.

  • Work with Engineering, Design, Sales, and Customer Success to turn early customer rollouts into a repeatable experience, rather than a new custom integration for every company.

  • Define what success looks like, instrument it end to end, and let the data guide what gets built next—from time to first project and self-service adoption to production usage and expansion across the company.

You Might Be a Good Fit If You

  • Have 7+ years of product management on developer tools, infrastructure, or internal developer platforms.

  • Have built self-service products that developers love and platform or IT teams trust. You understand both the person trying to ship an app and the team responsible for how it runs.

  • Can reason about identity, permissions, organization boundaries, and usage attribution, and turn those systems into workflows people can actually understand.

  • Have a strong point of view on how agents change who can build software inside a company and what an internal development platform needs to make that possible.

  • Know your way around controlled experimentation and data analysis, and can connect easier adoption to sustained product use.

  • Are biased toward speed and would rather ship, learn, and iterate than perfect a spec.

  • Build with what you own and form opinions through first-hand use, from creating a project to configuring the policies around it.

  • Use AI tools to compress research, drafting, and feedback synthesis so you can focus on judgment calls.

  • Work async by default, making decisions in writing in a fully remote team.

  • ESOP

    Every team member receives ESOP (equity ownership) in the company. We want everyone to share in the upside of what we’re building together.

  • Tech Allowance

    Use this budget to set up your ideal work environment—laptop, monitor, headphones, or whatever helps you do your best work.

    Supabase covers 100% of health insurance for employees and 80% for dependents, wherever you are. Your wellbeing and your family’s health are important to us.

  • Annual Off-Sites

    Once a year, the entire company gathers in a new city for a week of connection, collaboration, and fun. It’s a highlight of our year.

  • Flexible Work

    We operate asynchronously and trust you to manage your own time. You know what needs to be done and when.

  • Professional Development

    Every team member receives an annual education allowance to spend on learning—courses, books, conferences, or anything that supports your growth.

On a fair afternoon in Queens, New York, a woman in a red dress crosses the street in front of me with an actual axe under her arm. Her face is stern, her step fast, and you can tell from the blood on her axe that she wasn’t using it to chop wood. A police siren wails in the distance, but that doesn’t seem to bother her. 

I reach to lock the car and then remember it’s Halloween. 

At a warm and lovely dinner at Cabinet Magazine, founder and editor-in-chief Sina Najafi shows us a photograph of tightrope walker Margret Zimmermann balancing on a high wire above the bombed-out ruins of the Haymarket in Cologne, Germany, in 1946. She looks her finest in her red lipstick, but a treacherous wind is blowing in her hair as a crowd watches from far below.  

Outside, Brooklyn seems to be getting richer and poorer at the same time. A man wearing a bejeweled crown asks me for money. In this city, performance artists are beggars and beggars are performance artists. 

At Hyperallergic’s art-themed Halloween party, dozens of guests show up with impressive costumes and really commit to the act. Claire Valdez, an artist and organizer who’s running for Congress, drops by and serves as a judge in our art costume contest. I don’t have a costume, but I perform all night. 

“To live is to believe,” Marcel Duchamp says in a video on my phone screen. A little girl dressed as Lady Liberty crosses the street with her mother, a torch in her hand. She keeps the torch held high as they walk off, and that makes me smile.
