FBI Arrests Executive at Ransomware Negotiation Firm

📅 Oct 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agents with the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) on Thursday arrested an executive at a Canadian cybersecurity firm in connection with an investigation into the ShinyHunters hacking group that recently relieved the FBI of sensitive data on thousands of agents, multiple sources tell KrebsOnSecurity.

The New York Times reported today that the FBI has arrested a Canadian man in Pennsylvania on suspicion of assisting ShinyHunters. The Times story did not identify the man, nor did a statement on Twitter/X about the arrest from FBI Director Kash Patel.

One source close to the investigation told KrebsOnSecurity the Canadian person arrested this week was visiting Pennsylvania for a cyber insurance conference, and that the suspect’s company specialized in handling ransomware negotiations with cybercrime groups. Another shared that control over the ShinyHunters investigation has been centralized at an FBI field office in Texas.

An online search reveals the Cyber Risk Summit was held at the Loews Philadelphia Hotel between Oct. 5 and Oct. 7. The conference had several sponsors, but according to the summit’s website its biggest sponsor was a Canadian security company called Cypfer.

According to LinkedIn, one of Cypfer’s “ex-founders” was Edward Dubrovsky, who is now associated with another Canadian security firm and sponsor called CyberSteward. In a post to LinkedIn approximately one month ago, Dubrovsky said he had plans to attend the Cyber Risk Summit with the rest of the CyberSteward team.

[Update: Oct. 10, 9:58 a.m. ET: A spokesperson for Cypfer said Dubrovsky was not a founder or co-founder as his LinkedIn profile claims, but instead served as a managing director before resigning in December 2025].

Edward Dubrovsky’s LinkedIn profile.

“Looking forward to continuing conversations around strategy & compliant driven coercive (ransomware, extortion) advisory, negotiations and settlement services that are global and truly agnostic,” Dubrovsky wrote.

Federal court records show that on October 8, an Edward Dobrovsky (note the slight misspelling of the last name) was arrested in Pennsylvania on cyber extortion and conspiracy charges. Several of those documents — including the core complaint — are now sealed. But a handful of them were indexed at Courtlistener.com, including a summary of the complaint, which charges the defendant with “conspiracy to threaten to impair the confidentiality of information with the intent to extort money,” and “interference with commerce by threats.”

Image: Courtlistener.com

The inmate locator at the U.S. Bureau of Prisons website reports that a 54-year-old Edward Dubrovsky is currently being held at a federal facility in Philadelphia. But the court records indexed by CourtListener include a notice filed on October 9 that moved the case to the Eastern District of Texas, which sources say is now the epicenter of the FBI’s ShinyHunters investigation. The FBI declined to comment for this story.

Dubrovsky’s LinkedIn profile states he is the author of Cyber Extortion Strategic Response, a 252-page book that promises to “take readers beyond the ransom note and into the decisions that determine how an organization responds, recovers, and protects what matters.”

Edward Dubrovsky’s book, which centers on the intricacies of ransomware negotiations.

“At the heart of the book is a critical distinction: communicating with a criminal is not the same as negotiating a payment, and negotiating is not a commitment to pay,” reads an excerpt from the book’s listing on Amazon. “Engagement can serve other objectives, including testing claims, gathering information, creating time, and preserving options while the organization evaluates its next move.”

Mr. Dubrovsky could not be immediately reached for comment. KrebsOnSecurity also sought comment from other executives at CyberSteward, and will update this post in the event they respond. The available court records in Dubrovsky’s case show that he does not currently have an attorney and has yet to be appointed a public defender by the courts.

ShinyHunters typically uses phishing and stolen credentials to siphon data from corporate accounts at software-as-a-service companies, and then threatens to publish the stolen data online unless a ransom demand is paid. According to the FBI, the group has extorted more than $70 million from victims so far this year.

Sources tell KrebsOnSecurity the FBI has been poring over devices that were seized last month when the Dutch police arrested the convicted cybercriminal Pepijn van der Stap in connection with the ShinyHunters investigation, and that charges against principals at other companies that specialize in ransomware negotiation may be forthcoming.

Immediately after Van der Stap’s arrest, another member of ShinyHunters named “Rey” assumed control over the group and began taunting the FBI over data the group stole from the agency’s online recruitment portal, which included each’s person’s unit and specialization, as well as medical and psychiatric records.

Last week, Reuters reported that Rey — identified as a teenager named Saif Al-din Khader — had been detained and was cooperating with FBI investigators. On October 7, we detailed how Rey was apprehended as the cybercrime group allegedly sought to extort a navigation and digital aviation unit that was divested by Boeing in late 2025.

Stephanie Monohan

This is Amused Bouche, SAVEUR’s food questionnaire that explores the culinary curiosities of some of our favorite people. This interview series dives deep into their food routines, including dinner party strategies, cherished cookbooks, and the memorable bites they’d hop on a flight for.

Comedian and actor Hasan Minhaj is known for quippy questions, biting comebacks, and colorful commentary, but his most delightful soundbites are about food. During a recent  hour-long chat, he named a wide range of favorites, including bananas and oranges (“they come prepackaged and are great for traveling”); macaroni and cheese (“I love every category and version of it, from the box of Kraft to anyone’s Thanksgiving mac ’n’ cheese”); and dal chawal (specifically his mom’s version of the lentils and rice dish, “pure comfort food and my love language”).

These are the best-of-the-best bites for Minhaj, which is coincidentally the name of his new movie—Best of the Best, streaming now on Netflix—that he cowrote and produced with Prashanth Venkataramanujam and stars in as a professor and former dancer. The film pays homage to real-life Bollywood competitions, following collegiate dancers Maya and Anjali (played by Maitreyi Ramakrishnan and Priyanka Kedia, respectively), whose characters were inspired by “deeply passionate college kids in the biggest world that a lot of people have never heard of.”

Fiery passion is at the center of Minhaj’s life, especially when it comes to actual spice. “Some people want to drive at 85, some like a nice even 65, and some take it five miles below the limit,” he explains in a speedy analogy. “My spice level? I want to feel things in all caps. Life is to be lived. So I’m like, ‘Give it to me!’ Give me the full robustness of flavor that’s possible. I am a maximalist and love it all!”

His other spice of life is chai, “a form of commiseration, community, and love in Indian households—and an entry point to gossiping.” He loves that everyone does chai differently (he and his wife, Beena, prefer a more ginger-forward profile, while his parents like it sweeter). That’s why he became an investor and partner in the New York City-based Kolkata Chai in 2024, working with brothers Ani and Ayan Sanyal to expand the reach of a drink so engrained in their lives and culture. “It’s a welcome change to a lot of people’s only chai experience being at Starbucks in Middle America. It’s really amazing how you can change the complete perspective of an entire culture because of a drink that people can explore and make their own.”

Read on for Minhaj’s answers to our Amused Bouche questionnaire—it’s no surprise they’re chock-full of equally heartwarming and appetizing soundbites.

If you could only eat one thing 24/7/365, what would it be?

America is celebrating its 250th-year anniversary, and I think as a country, America’s greatest accomplishment is what we brought to cereal. I have been caught many a time red-handed with my full paw inside of some Golden Grahams, going to town. I will kill half a box, straight up, no milk. It is a snacking cereal. I’ll be watching a prestige drama and stress-eating Golden Grahams. Sometimes I’ll even drizzle honey over a bowl. I’ll also do the same with Honey Bunches of Oats. There’s a Montagues-and-Capulets debate here of blue box or orange box. I’m a blue box guy, because I love the almonds, extra crunch, and sweetness. It was my granola mix before I started eating granola, and it’s like trail mix without dastardly raisins coming in and f—ing shit up!

What’s the first thing you learned how to cook?

A strawberry Toaster Strudel, one of my all-time favorites. I popped it in the toaster as a kid, and it was an eye-opening experience. When I came home from elementary school, it felt like I was a master chef making myself this crisp little strudel cake and frosting it. I felt like a confectionery master. It was incredible. I toasted it on a level three and would sometimes pop it up and flip it midway through.

How about your latest kitchen adventure?

I actually didn’t hit my growth spurt until much later in high school, so I was quite undersized and malnourished-looking when I was younger. I was bullied a lot. My mother would make me eat three hard-boiled eggs on the way to school. And I hated hard-boiled eggs. Now that I’m a parent of small children, I understand the efficacy of hard-boiled eggs. But now I will pan-fry hard-boiled eggs, because really my hatred of them came from texture and temperature issues. But if you cut them in half and fry them yolk side down, there’s a crispness that makes it turn into a crunchy, almost taco-like topping that you can put on top of bread and butter. 

It’s a really delicious, warm-and-gooey snack that I started making when I tried to give my daughter a hard-boiled egg and she immediately spit it out. I’m like, “Yeah, the cycle of torture continues! There needs to be a better way! Let me figure out a way to make this delicious.” The secret was making a quesadilla as the on-ramp she needs to get eggs in her. It was an experiment I worked backward from, kind of like writing a joke, telling a story, or working on a screenplay. My daughter loves quesadillas but hates eggs, so by making it into crunchiness that would blend right in, it wouldn’t feel like a squishy thing that I’m force-feeding her.

What’s your treat-yourself splurge?

Growing up, we were never allowed to order dessert from restaurants. It is my greatest joy to order dessert from restaurants. I’ll tell the missus to order whatever she wants. Sometimes she’ll be indecisive. She always wants the chocolate lava cake, but she’ll be eyeing a berry tart. It’s a distant second, but I’ll take the bullet and order both for us to share. Sometimes when I do it, I’ll look over my shoulder to be like, “Are mom and dad going to find out?” But now I’m dad. We’re mom and dad!

What’s your most cherished cookbook?

I do not cook from cookbooks, but I will cook from YouTube Shorts. There’s just something about the looping nature of it breaking down the recipe. It’s tickling something in my mind and letting me know, “Hey, it’s not that hard. You can totally do it! You’ll get to completion, and it’s going to look pretty good.” And then as soon as doubt kicks in, the video starts playing again and snaps me out of it. It’s like the feeling of playing a song that you love over and over again, like “This Is How We Do It” by Montell Jordan. Play it again! “Men in Black”—play it again! I want to listen to it again, and that’s the feeling I get when I search “chicken tinga recipe” on YouTube Shorts.

Is there a cooking disaster that made you swear off a dish forever?

My wife does most of the cooking, but I want to put on the record that I try. I do cook, but a lot of the reaction is like, “Oh, you tried. I appreciate the effort. And that’s great for you. Why don’t you enjoy the omelet that you’ve made?” But I think I make a great soft-scramble, and what I bring to the breakfast table is pretty solid. I used to burn all of my food because I got impatient and cranked up the heat too fast. I learned you can’t bring microwave energy to a stove. Once I understood that foundational element in not burning your eggs, it was a huge unlock. Then I got to experience the full variety of eggs that aren’t charred.

Which nostalgic foods from childhood bring you the most comfort?

Dal chalwal: just lentils and rice. It has always been the first thing my mom makes for me when I get home to Sacramento, from college until now. It’s this beautiful gesture of love that I’m eating dal chawal with my mother, and her grandchildren are eating it with her at the same time. It’s pure comfort food and my love language.

When you’re playing dinner-party DJ, what’s spinning?

It’s a seasonal thing. Beena is really the entertainer and has been an incredible host since we met. For a holiday party, she’ll queue up a Pentatonix holiday playlist. Thematic, but a little left of center and unexpected.

What is your biggest entertaining flex to impress guests?

Beena loves a theme. We host Halloween on our block every year and call it Boo Bash. We go all out with a Halloween-themed live band, fog machines on the dance floor—aka the driveway—children’s face painting, bounce house, fake blood as drinks in IV bags. Her level of joy and imagination is unparalleled. She’s incredible. I add pizzazz, but I’m like, “Go off, queen!” She is the show. She’s the show creator, and I’m the showrunner.

Tell me about a meal so good you would hop on a flight to relive it.

My grandparents on both sides of my family have passed away, but I’m lucky enough that I was raised by my grandmother on my mother’s side from third to fifth grade. There’s a specific memory of when my grandmother made biryani. When the lid came off the pot and the steam and aroma of all the flavors escaped for the first time, she would put a ladle in and scoop it out for me, with meat that is so tender because it’s been cooking for so long. That’s the feeling that I would chase and book a flight immediately for, but it’s just a feeling. A feeling of love from it taking all day to make biriyani and that she loved me enough to put in that time and energy. Goat biriyani especially was a special-occasion dish, like when a cousin was getting married, and I associate it with being with family and something happy happening. In Hindi you call it ghar kha khana, the food from home. Biryani is the crème de la crème and the chef’s kiss of Indian cuisine for me. Especially my grandmother’s.

Andrew Reiner

Mexico City is a colossal, beautifully overwhelming metropolis and an absolute paradise for those who live to eat. Across thousands of restaurants, markets, street stalls, bakeries and fondas, food from every corner of the country converges. So deciding where (and what) to eat can feel daunting.

I’ve spent over a decade writing about Mexico’s food scene, along with popping up on TV shows to talk about it occasionally. My favorite way to navigate it all is still right here on the ground with my food tours. Sidestepping the obvious spots and encouraging travelers to go deeper into the food culture here constantly reminds me of the fascinating nature of how we chilangos eat.

Andrew Reiner

To me, that means having the flexibility to move between completely different culinary worlds. We can spend lunchtime standing on a crowded sidewalk, leaning over a taco drenched in salsa and lime juice, then dress up that same evening for a multicourse dinner at a dimly lit restaurant. We’re fiercely loyal to the neighborhood places that have fed us for years and know our names, but we remain insatiably curious about the next opening or dish worth crossing town for. 

That same flexibility shapes the city’s food. CDMX knows exactly how to pull tradition into modernity without losing its spirit. These 12 spots offer a taste of the city’s versatility: dishes and bites deeply rooted in history but shaped by the wonderfully chaotic, ever-evolving way we eat today. 

Scrambled Eggs at Fonda Margarita

Adolfo Prieto 1364 B, Tlacoquemecatl del Valle, Benito Juárez
+52 55 5559 6358

Andrew Reiner

I remember being dragged out of bed early on a weekend as a teenager to have breakfast here. Although I was still drowsy, the smell of the wood-fired stoves, the café de olla (traditional “pot coffee”) bubbling away, and sounds of the older musicians playing classic tunes on their guitars were enough to bring me around. I was fully awake by the time a plate of refried beans with scrambled eggs and a stack of warm tortillas made it to the table. It’s the kind of humble combination you’d find in almost any Mexican home. At Fonda Margarita, these eggs, which are cooked over a wood fire and enriched with lard, take on a different life. Getting here early is a requirement, as they open around 6:30 a.m. and by 10 a.m., all the food is gone.

Chilaquiles at Chilpa

C. Chilpancingo 35, Colonia Condesa,
+52 55 5264 4976

Andrew Reiner

Few breakfast menus are complete without chilaquiles. What started as a way to use up day-old tortillas is now one of the country’s quintessential breakfasts: triangles of tortillas, deep-fried until crisp, then bathed in salsa. At Chilpa in leafy Condesa, several salsas are available, from a red, tomato-based version to the bright, tangy tomatillo number, all with different spice levels. Pile on your choice of cecina, shredded chicken, or fried eggs. Crema, grated cheese, and chopped onion are standard, making it easy to order a different version every visit. 

Chocolate Concha at Panadería Gala

Tonalá 143, Roma Norte

Andrew Reiner

If you asked my Mexican grandma what breakfast looks like, she’d say coffee and a pan dulce—more often than not that would mean a concha. The quintessential Mexican pastry makes an appearance in virtually every bakery in town, from the most traditional versions made with a combination of lard and shortening to the tender butter-forward renditions found in most modern bakeries. At Panadería Gala, a speakeasy-style bakery, the extra-bouncy conchas get an extra layer of indulgence when topped with a white or dark chocolate ganache quenelle.

Margarita at Despacho Margarita

Córdoba 115, Roma Norte

Andrew Reiner

The Margarita may be known worldwide as the quintessential Mexican cocktail. Legend has it that the iconic drink was first conceived in Ensenada, Baja California, and its ties to coast and borderlands are still strong. But chilangos tend to save it for specific occasions and places such as a beach vacation, a favorite cantina, or a particular restaurant where we know they’ll make a killer one. At Despacho Margarita, the classic shaken version gets a new life, alongside spicy and frozen variations. In the slushy machine there are usually two flavors: a classic tequila and lime version and a seasonal mezcal-based one that changes monthly, with unique flavors such as nopal and shiso or raspberry and cayenne pepper. 

Banderas at Cantina Covadonga

Puebla 121, Roma Norte
+52 55 5533 2701

Andrew Reiner

Cantinas have long been places to gather over good food and several rounds of drinks. In these laid-back establishments, both are given equal importance. At Covadonga, a cantina without background music, generations mix at the tables, from the young and cool to sixty-somethings who come to play dominoes (available on request). 

Banderas have been a popular order here from the beginning: three shot glasses lined up, one with tequila, another with lime juice, and a third with sangrita, a red-hued blend of tomato and fruit juices. Together they mimic the three colors of the Mexican flag (hence the name). They’re not meant to be knocked back but rather sipped slowly, alternating between glasses.

Tlacoyo de Haba at Tlacoyos Medellín

Eje 3 Poniente Medellín, Roma Sur

Andrew Reiner

There’s a certain type of stand you’ll see repeatedly throughout the city centered around a large, round comal, usually fired with charcoal, with a bucket of corn masa and, more often than not, two women working side by side. Gorditas, quesadillas, and sopes are all on the menu, but the tlacoyo may be the most emblematic of central Mexico and Mexico City. 

Football-shaped and stuffed with your choice of fava bean purée, requesón (a soft cheese similar to ricotta), or refried beans, tlacoyos are typically topped with cactus salad for a tangy contrast, grated queso fresco, and red or green salsa, depending on your preference. They’re the kind of morning or afternoon snack you might grab during errands or before wandering into the adjacent market.  

Torta de Pastor con Queso at El Vilsito 

Petén 248 y, Avenida Universidad, Narvarte Poniente, Benito Juárez
+52 55 5536 3060

Andrew Reiner

The idea of stacking meat on a rotating spit was brought to Puebla by Lebanese immigrants. Once it reached Mexico City, it evolved into one of the city’s great late-night bites: the taco al pastor. At El Vilsito, a garage by day that moonlights as one of the city’s best taquerías, they take the pastor one step further, turning it into the most Chilango version possible by making it into a torta. A warm, lightly toasted telera gets a smear of refried beans, avocado, onion, tomato, and freshly shaved pastor, all finished with melted cheese. 

Fresh Oysters at Taller de Ostiones by FISM

Calle Versalles 113, Casa Versalles, Juárez
+52 55 8568 7397

Andrew Reiner

You can’t talk about Mexico City without talking about its obsession with seafood. It may seem strange for a city far from the coast, but seafood from both of Mexico’s coastlines makes its way here through the second-largest seafood market in the world (surpassed only by Tokyo’s Toyosu) before being distributed across the rest of the country.

At Taller de Ostiones, clams, barnacles, and tuna arrive three times a week by air from Baja California Sur, alongside sustainably farmed oysters. Those bivalves pair perfectly with a house-made sauce that’s rich, briny, deeply umami, and slightly spicy.

Comida Corrida at Nuevo Tehuacán

C. Dinamarca 47, Juárez
+52 55 1313 8562

Andrew Reiner

Mexico City runs on fondas and street food. These casual eateries serve homestyle food that’s quick, affordable, and feels like a warm hug. Stepping inside a fonda means being a part of a daily ritual known as comida corrida, where a meal typically unfolds in four acts: a steaming bowl of soup or broth to start, followed by your choice of rice or pasta. Next comes the main course, which at some places, like Nuevo Tehuacán, is what dictates the price of the meal; meatballs in chipotle, pork with tomatillo and purslane, and chicken in peanut sauce are classics. Everything is washed down with a pitcher of freshly made agua fresca and finished with a sweet treat like a cup of gelatin. Look around, and you’ll see schoolchildren sitting elbow to elbow with office workers all savoring the comfort of one of the city’s most democratic meals.

Flan at Siembra Tortillería

Avenida Isaac Newton 300, Polanco

Andrew Reiner

Mostly homemade, flan is a staple of casual Mexican cooking, but it’s one of those desserts that look deceptively simple. Getting the texture just right (silky, firm, not overly sweet) is way harder than it seems, which may be why the best versions are often found in homes. Siembra may be a temple dedicated to heirloom corn masa, tacos, and antojitos, but its flan has all the right qualifications to receive just as much attention. 

Nieve de Mamey at Nevería Roxy

Fernando Montes de Oca 89, Cuauhtémoc, Colonia Condesa
+52 55 5286 1258

Andrew Reiner

Ice cream parlors used to be an almost daily afternoon ritual for families and couples alike. Nevería Roxy was one of the early ones, and its 1950s-diner palate of teal and red has barely changed, even in the newer locations. Alongside the classic milk- and cream-based flavors, sundaes, floats, and banana splits, you’ll find nieves. 

The word literally translates to “snow,” and the closest comparison might be a sorbet, though the texture tends to be a little coarser and the flavors typically center around seasonal fruits. Mamey, an endemic fruit, may have a short season, but Roxy keeps its nieve de mamey year-round. With a faintly nutty flavor reminiscent of baked sweet potato, it’s become a local favorite. And thanks to mamey’s lush, natural creaminess, it’s easy to forget there’s no dairy in the cup. 

Candied Lime Peels With Shredded Coconut at Dulcería de Celaya

Avenida 5 de Mayo 39, Centro Histórico de la Ciudad
+52 55 5521 1787

Andrew Reiner

Stepping into Dulcería de Celaya feels more like entering a 19th-century jewelry store than a candy shop. Founded in 1874, this Centro Histórico institution still retains its original art nouveau interior, complete with French mirrors, tiled floors, and hand-carved oak cabinets, all filled with traditional sweets made in-house. Among the colorful sweet treats are candied lime skins stuffed with shredded sweetened coconut. The peel is candied and tender and, unlike fresh lime rind, is not bitter, so you can go ahead and bite into the whole thing at once.

These Are What I’ve Been Eating For Breakfast Every Day

YUM these little meal prep apple muffins have been such a treat for my mornings!

Open windows! Crisp fall air! Hot coffee! A warm, streusely, lightly cinnamony apple muffin with tons of wholesome stuff in it and a generous pat of butter on top! It’s what I’m doing every day right now, and it’s my dream life.

The main ingredients here are shockingly basic:

  • oats
  • apples
  • eggs
  • yogurt
  • vanilla
  • cinnamon

I threw some brown sugar streusel on top, because streusel increases my interest in any muffin by about 500%.

I’ve been packing one of these in my girls’ lunchboxes almost every time we pack a cold lunch! I also bring them for a car snack after school pick up. My kids honestly eat one of these (or these pumpkin ones, or these zucchini ones) once a day at this point.

Hope you love them!

Just Some Things I Tried and Have Opinions About

I don’t know that anyone cares about this, but I spend too much time thinking about these things.

  • This recipe works best with Greek yogurt rather than regular yogurt! It gets more evenly brown and has a nice rise like you see in the pictures, whereas regular yogurt is wetter and makes the batter more dense. (It does still work, if you’re in a pinch.)
  • You want rolled old fashioned oats for this one – don’t use the Costco sprouted oats, and do not use quick oats. They both absorb liquid in a different way. I just buy the Quaker old fashioned rolled oats!
  • I did try this with grating the apples, but I prefer just throwing them into the blender at the end of blending. 1) It’s easier, and 2) I felt like the muffins got a better rise with the apples being more incorporated.
  • Our previous muffin recipes similar to this have used maple syrup, but it gets really expensive to use that much real maple syrup, so I switched to brown sugar for this one!
  • The streusel is optional – they’re still pretty and delicious without!
  • The batter will bake differently the longer it sits! I find he sweet spot is about 10 minutes. You may find that your second batch gets a bit more puffy than your first.

Apple Streusel Meal Prep Muffins

5 Stars 4 Stars 3 Stars 2 Stars 1 Star

4.8 from 5 reviews

  • Author: Lindsay
  • Total Time: 33 minutes
  • Yield: 16–18 muffins 1x
Print Pin

Description

These apple streusel meal prep muffins are super wholesome, loaded with streusel, and ideal for prepping ahead! My kids love them!

Ingredients

Units USM

For the Muffins:

  • 1 cup whole milk Greek yogurt
  • 3 eggs
  • 3 1/2 cups old-fashioned oats (I don’t recommend the Costco sprouted oats or quick oats)
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar, lightly packed
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons baking soda
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 2 cups roughly chopped Honeycrisp apples (I did not peel mine)

For the Streusel:

  • 4 tablespoons butter, melted
  • 1/3 cup brown sugar
  • 1/2 cup + 3 tablespoons all-purpose flour
Cook Mode Prevent your screen from going dark

Instructions

  1. Prep: Preheat the oven to 350 degrees. Spray a muffin tin with avocado oil.
  2. Blend: Add ingredients to the blender in order, but leave out the apples for now. Blend in short bursts until it starts to move (you may need to help it along initially). Once it gets going, blend for 15-20 seconds, until mostly smooth. There should still be some flecks of texture, similar to a bran muffin batter.
  3. Apples: Add the apple chunks; blend again for 15-20 seconds until they are worked into the batter in small flecks.
  4. Streusel: Mix the streusel ingredients in a small bowl until it resembles small pebbles – I start with a fork, and then mix with my hands. (The batter can rest for 10 minutes during this time.)
  5. Assemble: Pour batter in the muffin tin – filling each cup 2/3 of the way full, but not all the way to the top. Add a spoonful of streusel on each one. (I typically get 16-17 muffins from this recipe, so I bake in two batches.)
  6. Bake: Bake for 16-18 minutes, until the tops spring back when gently pressed. Cool for 10-15 minutes so they are easier to remove from the pan. I love eating these warm with a shmear of butter!

An authentic wheel of Grana Padano carries a signature brand burned into its rind—so you know that if you see a diamond pattern and the cheese’s name, it must have been produced in the Valle Padana (Po River Valley) of Northern Italy according to strict rules with roots in a 12th-century Cistercian monastery. At the time, the region surrounding the city of Milan was primarily soggy marshland. The monks who built the monastery—Chiaravalle Abbey—were followers of the Rule of Saint Benedict, which, along with devotion to prayer, mandated that they practice agriculture with a focus on responsible, sustainable use of land. They first figured out how to turn the marshy landscape into arable fields so they could grow high-quality feed for dairy cattle. Since milk (and fresh cheese) didn’t last long, the next challenge for the monks was how to preserve this nutritious, valuable food source to nourish themselves and their community.

The method they developed—heating the milk in copper cauldrons, adding rennet (a coagulating enzyme), cutting the curds into tiny pieces, and molding, salting, and aging the cheese—remains largely unchanged today. By producing the first iteration of what is now known around the world as Grana Padano, the monks of Chiaravalle Abbey earned the distinction of operating the world’s first cheese dairy.

Easy to like, portable, and a good source of protein, the cheese became an emblematic food, enjoyed by Italians at all levels of society through the Renaissance and into the modern era. Its iconic name, meanwhile, developed over time. The monks called it “caseus vetus”—Latin for “old cheese”—but since the peasants didn’t know Latin, they referred to it as “grana” for its distinctive grainy texture. As production spread well beyond the monastery, each province had its own grana: Cheese made in Milan was “grana Milanese,” and “grana Mantovano” was from Mantua.

It wasn’t until 1954 that the Italian government codified the name Grana Padano to unite the different varieties of grana cheese under a single wider geographical indication and that the Grana Padano Protection Consortium was established. In 1996, Grana Padano was awarded the European Union’s Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) designation. Today the consortium incorporates 134 member dairies, who together produced over 6 million wheels of cheese in 2025. In addition to safeguarding the name, the organization is also responsible for the global promotion of Grana Padano PDO and governs every detail of production to ensure that each wheel meets the strict standards established under the PDO. 

The specifications start with the cows’ diet: All fresh forage and at least 75 percent of dry fodder (such as hay) must be grown within the cheese production area. Raw milk must be collected within 24 hours of milking and be partially skimmed, allowing for natural separation of the cream. After a natural whey starter is introduced to the copper (or copper-lined) milk vat, calves’ rennet is added for coagulation. The cheese is then broken into rice-size curds using a spino, which resembles a large whisk, and packed into round molds, where it sits for at least 36 hours. The wheels are immersed in brine for anywhere from 14 days to a month and aged for at least nine months and up to 24 months or longer. Wheels aged over 20 months can be labeled Riserva.

Bite into a chunk of Grana Padano PDO, and you taste the sweetness of fresh milk coupled with a savory hint of toasted nuts. Riservas may be flakier and more crystalline in texture than younger versions of the cheese but never dry or crumbly, and they must still have a delicate flavor. Grana Padano PDO is an exceptionally versatile cheese, equally delicious on a cheese board, wrapped with a slice of Prosciutto di Parma PDO and drizzled with aged balsamic for a sophisticated appetizer, as a filling for ravioli, or grated over soup or pasta.

It’s good for you, too. Lactose- and gluten-free, Grana Padano PDO is low in fat and an excellent source of protein, calcium, and vitamin B12. A 30-gram piece (just over one ounce) has as much nutrition as a pint of milk, making it a beneficial as well as tasty solution to the “afternoon slump” for both kids and adults. Whoever you share Grana Padano PDO with, be sure to tell them what they have in common with Italians from more than 1,000 years ago—that they’re savoring a taste of European culinary history.
