Metal fabricator NIC Global to cut 103 jobs near Seattle, move production to Eastern Washington

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →
NIC Global is closing its 100,000-square-foot manufacturing site in Woodinville, Wash., as demand shifts to Eastern Washington. (NIC Photo)

Metal fabricator NIC Global plans to close its Woodinville, Wash., plant, eliminating 103 jobs. Declining business from local customers is prompting the company to shift production primarily to Eastern Washington to meet data center-related growth.

The layoffs are scheduled to begin Jan. 18, 2027, with final departures Aug. 31, 2028, according to a notice filed with the Washington Employment Security Department. Affected jobs include assembly, engineering, quality assurance, metal fabrication specialists, and forklift positions.

The building will be returned to its landlord when the lease ends in September 2028.

“This is a sad day for our company. Woodinville is where NIC was formed — three Seattle-area sheet-metal shops brought together under one roof in 2001 — and the people in that building built the foundation of everything NIC is today,” Troy Wood, company president and CEO, said via email.

The 100,000-square-foot site mostly served energy and medical device customers. Wood said the volume of work at the plant has declined while demand has increased in Eastern Washington for customers building power distribution technology needed by data centers.

“NIC is a supplier of sheet-metal fabrications and assemblies, and in our business, being close to our customers is what keeps us competitive,” Wood said.

While most of the work from Woodinville will go to Spokane, and a smaller portion will go to NIC’s Gallatin, Tenn., facility. The company also has manufacturing in Pullman; Chillicothe, Ohio; and Denton, Texas. The work outside of Washington is focused on the heavy truck sector. The company’s headcount is nearly 600.

Laid-off workers will be encouraged to apply for openings at other plants.

There are smaller fabricators remaining in the Seattle area, including JEMCO Components & Fabrications, which provides precision machining and sheet-metal fabrication for customers in medical, aerospace and defense sectors, and QUAL-FAB serving medical and dental customers.

NIC’s legal and financial headquarters will stay in the Woodinville area, where the company plans a new office for its finance, IT, sales and West Coast support teams.

Wood praised the quality and reliability of the Woodinville employees’ work.

“I am grateful to every one of them,” he said, “and especially to those who chose to stay with us through two and a half years of uncertainty and kept doing their jobs with integrity.”

Power outage takes down KEXP streaming for Seattle radio station’s worldwide audience

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Seattle independent radio station KEXP, based in Lower Queen Anne, lost power early Friday morning. (GeekWire Photo / Kurt Schlosser)

KEXP’s website and online streaming were down Friday morning after a power outage hit the Seattle radio station, though its over-the-air broadcast was back and running.

The outage started around midnight, and the station was on and off for a while before it was “knocked offline by a major power outage” between 2 and 3 a.m., said Dashel Schueler, KEXP’s director of marketing and communications. Schueler didn’t know the cause of the outage.

The station’s backup power system, known as an uninterruptible power supply (UPS), also failed.

“There’s no power to the server room infrastructure because the UPS that is supposed to protect all of that gear has failed,” Schueler said. Staff are trying to power critical devices to restore services while they wait for the UPS to be repaired or for a workaround that bypasses it.

There’s no estimate yet for when streaming will return.

Listeners in the Seattle area can tune in at 90.3 FM. The outage affects the KEXP.org stream and the station’s mobile app. KEXP estimates about 175,000 people stream its live broadcast each week, and another 85,000 listen to its shows on-demand.

The outage also took down 92.7 KREV, the station KEXP now owns in the San Francisco Bay Area, which relies on KEXP’s Seattle-based digital infrastructure. Because KEXP’s DJs pick songs live rather than playing a preset playlist, underwriting breaks vary in timing and length, Schueler said. DJs fire the separate Seattle and Bay Area spots manually and at the same time.

KREV was back by around 8:30 to 8:45 a.m., running on a barebones setup KEXP typically uses for remote broadcasts, Schueler said. Listeners may notice some issues while the station gets everything put back together.

Around 7:30 a.m., KEXP DJ John Richards read messages on air from listeners across Seattle who were happy to have the station back — terrestrially at least.

A few posts from listeners on X and Bluesky referenced the streaming outage.

Update: Late Friday afternoon KEXP said it was still working to restore services, but it did manage to put up a temporary livestream on its site.

United Airlines CEO Says Critics Don’t Understand His American Airlines Merger Vision. I Think We Do.

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Scott Kirby thinks critics misunderstand his vision for merging United Airlines and American Airlines. I understand why he wants to do it. What I reject is his premise that a better airline would make up for the higher fares passengers would face once a major competitor disappears.

A United-American Merger Makes Sense For Scott Kirby. That Does Not Make It Good For Passengers.

One Mile At A Time brought a revealing Dallas Morning News interview with Kirby to my attention. The United Airlines CEO remains convinced that his proposed combination with American could have been a tremendous success, including for customers.

Before getting into that, a word about why Kirby appears so frequently on Live And Let’s Fly. Unlike Ed Bastian and Robert Isom, he puts himself out there constantly. Every few days, it seems, there is another interview or podcast with something worth discussing.

I don’t know whether this is laying the groundwork for a presidential campaign after he retires from United, but I am not going to ignore his statements simply because his competitors maintain a lower profile.

Kirby Thinks Critics Are Using The Wrong Lens

In the interview, Kirby argues that skepticism about regulatory approval viewed his proposal through a “traditional commodity lens.” He maintains that only he knew exactly what he intended to build.

“We were disappointed that it leaked and then I saw all of the feedback of what people said about how possible it was. All of the criticism about this would not get through regulatory muscle was looking at it through a traditional commodity lens, and I am the one person who knows what we were planning to do for 100% certainty because I’m the one that was going to do it.”

He also describes receiving encouragement from people at his children’s soccer games in Dallas who wanted him to buy American. I can certainly believe there are frustrated American customers who would welcome United taking over.

But enthusiasm for better service does not establish that eliminating American as an independent competitor would benefit the traveling public.

In case you missed it, there is no announced merger agreement. American rejected the proposal earlier this year, and Kirby acknowledged in his April statement that a “willing partner” was essential.

I Understand Exactly Why Kirby Wants American

From Kirby’s perspective, the strategic appeal is obvious.

American would bring a vast domestic network and a huge customer base. Combining that with United’s international reach would create an extraordinarily powerful airline, with more opportunities to sell connecting itineraries and persuade travelers to concentrate their spending in one loyalty program.

Kirby could extend United’s product strategy across American’s operation. I can imagine many American passengers appreciating better technology and a more consistent premium experience. This fits what Kirby told me in Denver when I asked about his merger ambitions. He wants enough scale to compete with the best Asian and Middle Eastern airlines on product as well as network.

That is an understandable corporate ambition. A larger airline could have more resources to invest, and some customers could enjoy more convenient connections.

But greater pricing power strikes me as the primary driver for this merger: Kirby has repeatedly said that he believes fares are too low. The ability to sell a better product and the ability to charge more because a competitor has disappeared can exist at the same time…in Kirby’s mind, he can only invest more if his margin his higher and a merger with AA would enable that to happen.

More Dots On A Map Do Not Replace Competition

Consider Chicago. Today, each airline has a reason to respond when the other offers a better fare or schedule. A merged company would decide what both networks should charge and how much capacity to offer…and we’d quickly see higher fortress hub pricing that Delta is able to extract from Atlanta,Detroit, and Minneapolis (or American is able to extract from Charlotte or Dallas or United itself from Houston).

It goes beyond nonstop routes. A passenger traveling between smaller cities may compare an American connection with a United connection. Putting both itineraries under one carrier removes the rivalry between the companies selling them.

Kirby says his vision centers on growth. Even if the combined airline initially retained every flight, which would be stupid, the competitive incentive would still change. And as we saw when Spirit went bust, higher (or lower) fares are not necessarily confined to the merged carrier. Other airlines would have less reason to discount aggressively if a major independent rival disappeared.

I don’t think anyone can put a credible percentage on the fare increases without a specific proposal and market analysis. But I would expect substantial upward pressure on prices in affected markets. Passengers would get a better app and more destinations within one program while paying considerably more to travel, which is not a tradeoff I would make.

That’s the underlying concern. Most travelers are trying to get somewhere at a reasonable price. A more impressive airline is of limited comfort if it prices people out of traveling.

Regulators Would Need More Than A Promise Of Better Service

The central merger standard under Section 7 of the Clayton Act concerns whether a transaction may substantially lessen competition. A compelling product presentation does not satisfy that test.

The JetBlue-Spirit case is instructive: the argument for creating a stronger competitor to larger airlines did not overcome the competitive harm the court found and that was a merger I thought should have passed regulatory scrutiny despite my personal opposition to it (for the sake of JetBlue’s deteriorating financial health).

Kirby has acknowledged that divestitures would be necessary. But selling some gates would not automatically recreate an independent nationwide airline with American’s ability to compete. Any remedy would need to preserve effective competition and I don’t think that is possible right now.

Opposition is also broader than Democrats. Republican Senator Mike Lee joined Elizabeth Warren in an April letter questioning a United-American merger and warning about its consequences for consumers.

A Friendly Administration Would Not End The Risk

If Democrats take control of one or both houses of Congress, I would expect more scrutiny of any attempt to advance this combination. Hearings and investigations alone could not derail the merger, but might create a powerful disincentive. Enforcement agencies and courts would remain central. But assuming political support today guarantees success years later is a bad assumption.

Senator Cory Booker has already introduced the CLEAN Mergers Act, proposing retrospective review and potential unwinding of major Trump-era transactions. In announcing it, Senator Chris Murphy explicitly threatened future breakups of deals Democrats regard as corrupt monopolies. That is just proposed legislation that could not overcome Trump’s veto at this point, but a broader political warning that might be enacted down the road.

Indeed, a Democratic administration taking office in January 2029 could revisit an allegedly anticompetitive transaction. As the Justice Department explains, closing a merger does not immunize it from a subsequent antitrust challenge. Unwinding an integrated airline would be difficult and contested, but the possibility is real.

I Am No Longer Against Every Airline Merger

Had you asked me a few years ago, I would have been flatly against any merger…I miss US Airways, Continental, and Northwest and the competition they brought. But times have changed.

I wanted Spirit and Frontier to combine because I saw the potential for a stronger nationwide budget competitor. I would also welcome a serious look at whether a combination involving Frontier, Allegiant, and Sun Country could create a more effective low-fare alternative. I also woudl love to see a JetBlue-Alaska merger if JetBlue could overcome its debt issues.

Those deals would still deserve scrutiny. Smaller does not automatically mean harmless, and preserving low-fare competition would be essential. But combining smaller carriers to challenge the largest airlines presents a different competitive proposition from allowing two mammoth network carriers to eliminate their rivalry.

The question is whether consolidation strengthens competition or removes it. In a United-American combination, I find the latter risk overwhelming, not matter what Kirby says, because he has also often repeated that he wants fares to rise.

CONCLUSION

I understand Kirby’s vision. He wants a larger network and the resources to build an airline that competes with the best in the world. I can see why American would be an attractive acquisition. I think we all understand him…

But I think he underestimates both the regulatory obstacles and the cost to passengers, even if he insists we are all wrong. Better service would be welcome. Eliminating a major competitor would give the combined airline less reason to offer that service at a competitive price.

More dots and lines on one map would make United more powerful, but I remain convinced that consumers would ultimately lose.
