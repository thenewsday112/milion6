Southwest Performance Business Card Review: Is The $299 Annual Fee Worth It?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Southwest® Rapid Rewards® Performance Business Credit Card is the most premium card in the Chase and Southwest co-brand card portfolio. This card currently has a $299 annual fee and comes with many helpful perks when you fly Southwest, including a free checked bag, complimentary upgrades to Preferred seating when available, and an anniversary bonus of 9,000 points every year.

If you frequently travel with Southwest and qualify for a small business credit card, this card is worth a closer look. That’s especially true now, because not only is it offering a welcome bonus of 80,000 bonus points, but now is also your last chance to earn a Southwest credit card welcome bonus that counts toward the Companion Pass. I’ll get into all of that and more below, as this is my in-depth Southwest Performance Business Card review.

Southwest Performance Business Card summary & key details for November 2026

Before I cover the Southwest Performance Business Card perks in detail, here’s a high-level view of this business credit card’s key details:

  • Southwest Performance Business Card annual fee: $299
  • Southwest Performance Business Card welcome offer: 80,000 bonus points after you spend $5,000 on purchases in the first three months from account opening
  • Southwest Performance Business Card earning rate: 4x points on Southwest purchases, 2x points at gas stations and restaurants, 2x points on hotel accommodations booked directly with the hotel, 2x points on local transit, and 1x points on all other purchases
  • Southwest Performance Business Card foreign transaction fee: None
  • Southwest Performance Business Card benefits with Southwest: First checked bag for free, complimentary Preferred seat when available at booking, extra legroom upgrade when available within 48 hours prior to departure, board no later than Group 5, 25% back on inflight purchases, earn 2,500 Tier Qualifying Points toward status for every $5,000 spent on the card (through 2026), get 9,000 points every year on your cardmember anniversary
  • Southwest Performance Business Card travel perks: Up to $120 statement credit for Global Entry, TSA PreCheck, or NEXUS every four years
  • Best for: Frequent Southwest flyers who qualify for a business credit card and want as many perks with the airline as possible

Southwest Performance Business Card bonus, eligibility, & fees

When applying for a credit card, major considerations include the welcome bonus, the eligibility rules, and the fees, so let’s start by taking a look at those details for the Southwest Performance Business Card.

Southwest Performance Business Card welcome bonus

The Southwest Performance Business Card currently has a welcome bonus where you can earn 80,000 bonus points after spending $5,000 within the first three months.

Southwest Rapid Rewards is a revenue-based frequent flyer program, based on my latest miles & points valuations, I consider the points to be worth 1.2 cents each. That means I consider the 80,000 points to be worth $960, which is a solid bonus for a card like this (though admittedly we’ve seen slightly better limited time offers in the past).

Earn lots of points with the Southwest Performance Business Card

Southwest Performance Business Card eligibility restrictions & rules

When it comes to Southwest credit card eligibility rules, you’re potentially eligible for the Southwest Performance Business Card (including the welcome bonus) if you don’t currently have this card, and if you haven’t received a new cardmember bonus on this card in the past 24 months. You can have any of the Southwest personal cards, and/or the Southwest Premier Business Card, and they won’t impact eligibility for this card.

Beyond that, be aware of the general restrictions on applying for Chase cards. This includes the Chase 5/24 rule, whereby you generally won’t be approved for a Chase card if you’ve opened five or more cards in the last 24 months. That said, business credit cards aren’t always included on your credit report, so they often don’t count toward 5/24.

Southwest Performance Business Card annual fee & authorized user fee

The Southwest Performance Business Card has a $299 annual fee, making it the highest annual fee Southwest Airlines credit card. However, it also comes with plenty of perks that can justify it, as I’ll explain below.

There is no cost to add authorized users on the card, so you can add authorized users to your card, and be rewarded for their spending.

Southwest Performance Business Card foreign transaction fee

The Southwest Performance Business Card has no foreign transaction fees, so it’s a good card to use for purchases abroad.

Use the Southwest Performance Card with no foreign transaction fees

Southwest Performance Business Card rewards structure

One of the main reasons you’ll want to consider picking up a card is because of its return on spending, so you’re sure it aligns with what you’re looking for. In the case of the Southwest Performance Business Card, it has a limited number of bonus categories, and spending can also help you toward earning elite status with Rapid Rewards. So let’s cover those details.

Earning points on spending

The Southwest Performance Business Card offers some potentially useful bonus categories, as follows:

  • Earn 4x points per dollar spent on Southwest Airlines purchases
  • Earn 2x points per dollar spent at gas stations and restaurants
  • Earn 2x points per dollar spent on hotel accommodations booked directly with hotels
  • Earn 2x points per dollar spent on local transit and commuting, including rideshare
  • Earn 1x points per dollar spent on all other purchases

These are some pretty solid bonus categories, though in general, I would only use this card for everyday spending if trying to earn Southwest Airlines status. Based on my valuation of 1.2 cents per point, this is like earning a 4.8% return on the 4x points categories, 2.4% return on the 2x points categories, and a 1.2% return on the 1x points categories.

Earn unlimited Tier Qualifying Points toward status

The Southwest Performance Business Card lets you earn unlimited Tier Qualifying Points (TQPs) toward elite status. Specifically, you can earn 2,500 TQPs toward A-List status for every $5,000 spent on the card.

Southwest A-List status offers perks like priority boarding, a 25% points bonus, same-day standby, priority check-in and security lane access, and a dedicated phone number.

A-List status ordinarily requires earning 35,000 TQPs in a calendar year, so spending on a Southwest Performance Business Card could help you earn that. Whether or not that’s worth it depends on how much you’d otherwise fly with Southwest.

Do note that Southwest recently announced some big changes to its loyalty program, including new status level names and the introduction of a third tier called Platinum. Starting in 2027, A-List status will become Silver status, and earning it will require 35,000 TQPs or 25 qualifying flights.

According to the Southwest Performance Business Card’s terms & conditions, cardholders can earn TQPs on card spending at the current rate of 2,500 TQPs per $5,000 spent through December 31, 2026. After that date, A-List status will become Silver status, and every co-branded Southwest credit card will earn 1 TQP per dollar spent, so $35,000 in spending would get you to Silver.

No more Companion Pass qualification through welcome bonuses

One of the best values in travel is the Southwest Airlines Companion Pass. For those of you not familiar with Southwest’s Companion Pass, it allows someone else to travel with you for the entire year anywhere Southwest flies.

You still have to pay the taxes on their ticket, but the Companion Pass is valid on both award and revenue tickets. You can change your designated companion three times in a year, so you can potentially even use this for different people.

Historically, you’ve been able to get the Companion Pass by earning 135,000 Rapid Rewards points in a calendar year — and Southwest credit card welcome bonuses counted toward this, along with referral bonuses, anniversary points, bonus points from spending on the card, and more. Starting January 1, 2027, this will no longer be the case.

Southwest is updating its elite status program to have three tiers, the highest of which is called Platinum and gets you unlimited Companion Pass trips. At that time, credit card welcome bonuses and all the other points earning mentioned above will no longer count toward qualifying for the Companion Pass (or any level of status).

To earn Platinum, and therefore the Companion Pass, you’ll instead need to earn 140,000 TQPs or take 100 qualifying flights. This change isn’t specific to the Southwest Performance Business Card, as it applies to welcome bonuses across all Southwest credit cards. But the good news is that welcome bonuses earned before the end of 2026 do count toward Companion Pass qualification, so now is your last chance to take advantage before this opportunity is closed.

Redeeming points from the Southwest Performance Business Card

I have an entire post on how to redeem Southwest Rapid Rewards points for maximum value, but I’ll briefly cover how redemptions work and how to get the most value out of your points here.

Rapid Rewards is a revenue-based frequent flyer program, which means that the value you’ll get from your points is tied to the cash cost of Southwest tickets. That said, there are a few different levels, ranging from Basic tickets to Choice Extra fares, with Basic awards usually offering the highest redemption value.

In my experience, the best-value Southwest point redemptions are usually for international routes to destinations like Costa Rica and Aruba. Southwest also flies to Hawaii, which can be a good use of points, but I’ve found that the redemption value is often a bit lower on these in-demand routes. And of course, if you have the Companion Pass your points become twice as valuable because a friend can travel for free minus taxes and fees.

There are no change or cancellation fees on Southwest award bookings, and rebooking a fare when the price drops gets you a refund equal to any difference in points. Just note that Basic fares can only be changed by upgrading to a higher fare bundle, so for a cheaper Basic award you’d want to cancel and rebook rather than change your existing itinerary.

Southwest Performance Business Card perks & benefits

While the Southwest Performance Business Card has a great bonus, the main reason you want to get this card is because of the perks that it offers. For those who fly Southwest with any frequency, the perks should more than cover the annual fee. In fact, for those who are frequent Southwest flyers, this card is almost too good to be true.

9,000 anniversary Rapid Rewards bonus points

Just for having the Southwest Performance Business Card, you’ll receive 9,000 Rapid Rewards points on your account anniversary every year. As I said above, Southwest points can be redeemed for ~1.2 cents each toward airfare, so that’s the equivalent of ~$108 toward airfare annually.

Get a free first checked bag

The Southwest Performance Business Card offers a free first checked bag for you and up to eight additional passengers on your same reservation. If you usually check bags on Southwest — especially if you don’t have Southwest status, which gets you a free first checked bag — this benefit alone can quickly help you recoup the annual fee.

For non-elite Southwest travelers, a checked bag costs $45 one-way. If you maxed this benefit out with eight other travelers on a round-trip itinerary with checked bags, you’d be looking at over $800 in value.

Complimentary Preferred seat selection at booking

When you book a Southwest flight as a Performance Business cardholder, you can select a Preferred or Standard seat at the time of booking, if they’re available. This benefit also extends to eight travelers on your same reservation.

The cost for a Preferred seat can vary from $28 to nearly $50 depending on the flight, so this benefit can also save you some real money.

Unlimited upgrades to extra legroom seats

The Southwest Performance Business Card also offers unlimited upgrades to extra legroom seats for cardholders and up to eight travelers on the same reservation. These upgrades are space available and will start within 48 hours of departure. This is another nice elite-style benefit that can noticeably improve your travel experience on Southwest.

Early boarding

Southwest Performance Business cardholders can board with Group 5, or earlier if they have status or a fare that entitles them to a higher boarding group. Once again, this perk extends to eight additional travelers on the same reservation.

25% savings on inflight drinks & Wi-Fi

The Southwest Performance Business offers 25% savings on inflight purchases of Wi-Fi and drinks. To take advantage of this, simply pay with your card. You’ll automatically receive a statement credit on eligible inflight purchases.

Global Entry, TSA PreCheck, or NEXUS credits

For having the Southwest Performance Card you receive a credit towards TSA PreCheck, Global Entry, or NEXUS. You can take advantage of this benefit once every four years. (up to $120 as of 10/1/24)

If you are going to use this perk, I’d highly recommend registering for Global Entry over TSA PreCheck. For those of you not familiar, TSA PreCheck gets you expedited US security screening, while Global Entry gets you expedited US immigration clearance.

Why register for Global Entry and not PreCheck? Because Global Entry also comes with TSA PreCheck, while the inverse isn’t true (signing up for TSA PreCheck doesn’t get you Global Entry).

On an ongoing basis, the card is worth it as well. It has a $299 annual fee but offers 9,000 points per year (which I value at ~$108) and more. This card should pay for itself for anyone who flies Southwest often.

A new pathway to treat depression? Psilocybin may rewire the brain without psychedelic effects

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Psychedelic drugs such as psilocybin can help brain cells form lasting new connections, raising hopes for new depression treatments.

But scientists have struggled to determine whether those changes depend on the mind-altering effects of the drugs.

Now, a study in mice suggests that the brain’s rewiring and its response to psychedelics can occur through separate cellular pathways.

Researchers at the University of California, Santa Cruz gave mice one dose of psilocybin, the compound in magic mushrooms.

Then they watched individual connection points on living brain cells for three weeks. They also took the receptor psilocybin acts on out of one group of cells, and put it back in another group.

The rewiring and the drug’s signature behavior came apart.

Everything here happened in mice, and the behavior in question is a head twitch, not a hallucination.

Jacob Baker and Emily Kogan share first authorship, with senior author Yi Zuo, in the Department of Molecular, Cell and Developmental Biology at UC Santa Cruz.

New connections stuck around

Brain cells pass signals across junctions called synapses, and each one sits on a small bump on a branch of the receiving cell.

The team used a microscope that sees those bumps through the skull of a living mouse, so the same branch can be checked again days later.

Within 24 hours of a dose, new bumps appeared faster than in mice given saline. Of the three doses tried, the middle and high ones worked about equally.

The new connections were also sturdier. In untreated mice, most new bumps start out as short stubs that rarely last, and 40.6% survived a week. After psilocybin, 61.3% did.

The gap widened with time: three weeks out, 48.0% of the new bumps in treated mice were still there, compared with 29.3% in controls. More of what psilocybin built stayed built.

Shape tracked that durability. Only 2.2% of new bumps in control mice grew the broad mushroom head that marks a mature, strong synapse, compared with 28.4% after psilocybin.

“These newly formed connections can integrate into existing neural circuitry and persist over time, leaving a lasting structural trace of psilocybin’s effects on the brain,” said Baker, a doctoral student in Zuo’s lab.

The old wiring thinned out

A rewiring drug that only added connections would leave the brain denser every time, and that is not what happened.

The burst of new bumps was brief, and over three weeks the drug also sped up the loss of older connections.

Pre-existing bumps in treated mice were less likely to survive than the same bumps in control mice, and the net density drifted back toward where it started.

So in a healthy mouse brain, psilocybin did not inflate the wiring. It swapped some of it out.

Stress changed the response

The team then put mice through 21 days of unpredictable mild stress, a standard way to produce something like a depressive state in a rodent. The dose came the day after the stress stopped.

Stress had been stripping connections away, and that loss kept running after the stress ended in mice given saline.

In mice given psilocybin, the rate of connection loss returned to the level seen in unstressed animals, while new connections formed in greater numbers. The result was a bigger net gain.

In psilocybin-treated mice, 7.8% of connections lost during stress reappeared, compared with 4.8% in saline-treated controls.

That looks like a side effect of building more everywhere, because new bumps were no likelier to appear where old ones had been lost.

“These findings challenge the prevailing view that psilocybin simply increases neural connectivity and suggest that its effects depend on the brain’s underlying condition,” Zuo said.

One receptor, two jobs

Psilocybin acts on a serotonin receptor called 5-HT2A, thickest in one class of output cells in the cortex. The team built two kinds of mice to ask what those cells do.

In the first, the receptor was missing everywhere except those cortical output cells. Psilocybin still drove new connections and still made them stick.

The mice did not show the head-twitch response.

In the second, the receptor was deleted from those cells and left everywhere else. Psilocybin did nothing to the wiring. The head twitching came through intact.

That pattern points to the receptor on those cells being both necessary and sufficient for the structural changes, and neither necessary nor sufficient for the twitch.

The authors found one limit on it. In both engineered strains, psilocybin stopped accelerating the loss of old connections, so clearing out the old wiring appears to need other cell types too.

A twitch is not a hallucination

The head-twitch response is a rapid shake of the head, counted by an observer, and it is the field’s standard stand-in for whether a serotonin psychedelic is hallucinogenic in people.

The authors are blunt that it measures a movement rather than an experience, and write that their results should not be read as directly addressing what causes hallucinations in humans.

The caveat matters, because the result is being framed as a route to drugs without the trip.

Chemists have already built non-hallucinogenic relatives of several psychedelics that promote the same kind of connection growth in animals, and the paper names four of them.

“By showing that therapeutic circuit remodeling and hallucinogenic effects operate through distinct cellular pathways, our study provides a promising blueprint for designing non-hallucinogenic drug analogs,” Zuo said.

Beyond the mouse brain

The imaging covered the somatosensory cortex, the part that handles touch.

An earlier study of the frontal cortex reported that psilocybin’s new connections were not preferentially stabilized, and this paper reports the opposite. The authors put the disagreement down to the region.

None of it has been shown in a human brain, and nothing here tested whether the mice behaved less like animals under stress.

Depression is diagnosed by how a person feels and functions, and a count of connection points on a mouse neuron is a long way from that.

The findings identify a specific receptor and class of brain cells responsible for some of psilocybin’s lasting structural effects.

Whether scientists can use that knowledge to develop treatments for depression or PTSD without psychedelic experiences remains uncertain.

For now, the study offers a clearer picture of how psilocybin changes the brain, and a possible path toward drugs that preserve those changes without producing the same mind-altering effects.

The full study was published in the journal Nature Neuroscience.

Butterfly wings create a stunning illusion that fools predators

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Bold stripes make a butterfly easy for a bird to spot. Once the wings start flapping, though, those stripes create a motion illusion that can make an attacker aim behind the butterfly or off to one side.

That could help explain why relatively few predators specialize in catching butterflies in flight, even though they’re among the most visible insects around.

A wide range of birds hunt night-flying moths in the air, and those moths tend to have duller patterns and faster wingbeats.

Butterflies, by contrast, generally have brighter wings and slower wingbeats.

George Hancock, a researcher in the Centre for Ecology and Conservation at the University of Exeter, led the work.

The team included Anna Hughes of the Department of Psychology at the University of Essex.

No birds took part in the study. Instead, the researchers analyzed butterfly footage using a computer model of bird vision, then asked human volunteers to play the role of predators in a touch-screen game.

Stripes create false motion signals

The illusion works much like a barber pole. The striped pole only spins in place, but the stripes seem to climb.

“Butterfly wings deform as they fly – clapping together on the upstroke and peeling apart on the downstroke – so their stripes shift angle and point in different directions as they flap,” said Jolyon Troscianko, a co-author at Exeter.

Inside an eye, simple motion detectors compare one moment to the next and report which way things are moving.

Hancock’s team measured two kinds of false signal those detectors could pick up, which the researchers call motion confusion.

Backward motion is likely to make a predator misjudge how fast the butterfly is going, so it strikes behind it. Sideways motion is likely to throw off its aim to the left or right.

The first clue looked like a software bug.

“The first slow-motion video of a butterfly I put through our bird-vision computer model was glowing with downwards motion even though the butterfly was moving up,” Troscianko said.

“First I checked this wasn’t a coding error, that I’d swapped up and down somehow, but then it dawned on me that this would be a perfect way to confuse attacking predators.”

Filming butterflies at take-off

The team filmed real butterflies, including the red admiral and two swallowtails, taking off in front of a green screen at 1,057 frames per second.

Most predator attacks start on a butterfly that’s sitting still, so the first fraction of a second of flight is the moment that counts.

The researchers converted each clip to match how a small songbird’s eye sees brightness, then blurred it to match how quickly the bird’s vision can update.

The researchers then swapped each butterfly’s pattern for plain gray, black, or white. The patterned wings produced more of both kinds of confusion than any of the plain versions.

In an interview with Earth.com, Hancock was asked what a person would see watching the illusion in slow motion.

“When viewed in slow motion and when the eye has enough time to track the butterfly’s position there is no obvious illusion, though some butterflies are certainly hypnotic to watch,” Hancock said.

The patterns interfere with motion detection at the earliest stage of vision, Hancock explained. That makes a butterfly harder to catch during take-off, when it moves too fast for a bird to follow it by turning its head.

“To understand how animal patterns act as a defense requires looking at them through the lens of their predators, not just for the colors that they might see but also for the speed that they can see,” Hancock said.

European peacock butterfly spreads its wings against the green screen researchers used to film take-offs and analyze how wing patterns distort motion. Credit: Dr. George RA Hancock. Click image to enlarge.

The illusion is common in Europe

Next, the team checked how common the effect is. They scanned the illustrations in a field guide covering 397 European butterfly species.

That came to 757 wing forms once males, females, and subspecies were counted separately. Each form was then animated in a three-dimensional flight simulation.

Several features of butterfly wing patterns stood out. For the backward illusion, high contrast on the front wings mattered most, followed by vertical stripes on the back wings, a single bold band, and the tails some species carry.

Swallowtails, all of them striped, scored consistently high on both kinds of confusion. Brush-footed butterflies, the family that includes the red admiral, ranged from the highest scores to almost none.

The main surprise, Hancock said, was how widely the strategy was spread across European butterflies.

“It might seem strange that a butterfly would evolve to be so visible, especially as very few European species are toxic or unpalatable,” Hancock said.

“But our study reveals the wide variety of different patterns butterflies can use to create illusions that confuse predators.”

Digital wings resembled real ones

Genes and growth constrain how butterfly wings develop, so a pattern that appears to create motion confusion could still arise by coincidence. To test that, the team had a computer program evolve its own wings.

The program started with populations of 24 random butterflies on three different wing shapes.

In each generation, the eight that produced the most motion confusion survived. The survivors produced mixed and mutated offspring that replaced the other 16.

Over 20 generations per run, the program produced 57,600 patterns in all.

Wings bred for the backward illusion came to resemble real European butterflies more closely than the random starting wings did, or wings that survived by chance alone.

Selecting for strong forward motion produced plain white butterflies, much like the ones commonly called whites. In the authors’ view, that look would lead a predator to overestimate a butterfly’s speed and strike too far ahead.

Much of the past work on butterfly wings has focused on warning colors, mimicry, and camouflage. The authors wrote that motion confusion may work alongside those jobs rather than replace them.

Butterfly illusions shifted human aim

To see whether the measurements predicted missed attempts, the team built a fast touch-screen game.

One hundred volunteers at Exeter’s Penryn Campus in Cornwall played it. Together they made 3,000 attempts.

Animated versions of five butterfly species flew across the screen along wavy paths, and players tapped to catch them.

People see motion more slowly than birds do. So the butterflies flew at about 16 inches (40 centimeters) per second, but carried the wing blur of one moving twice as fast.

The species with the strongest backward illusion drew taps that landed farther behind them and spread out more along their path.

Between the most and least confusing species, the center of the taps shifted by about 38% of the average butterfly’s body length.

Birds and butterflies both live at a much faster pace than people, Hancock said, and butterfly defenses like these illusions are tailored to that speed.

“By using these illusions, butterflies can redirect attacks from birds by interfering with their ability to track motion at high speeds,” Hancock told Earth.com.

Study limitations and future research

Hancock said the strategy is likely most common in butterflies because their wings are larger and flap more slowly than those of other insects.

Day-flying moths such as the Jersey tiger moth might use it too, though that still needs testing.

Lengthwise stripes on lizards, snakes, fish, and bird wings could work the same way, Hancock said. “Within all these groups there is a correlation between moving a lot and being stripier!”

Broadclub cuttlefish already pass dark stripes down their bodies while stalking crabs, and the motion model used here was first tested on them. Shiny, reflective animals confuse predators with a different kind of optical illusion.

No real birds were tested, so whether they miss striped butterflies more often in the wild is still an open question. The simulations also used only the upper sides of the wings, one style of flapping, and plain backgrounds.

The authors call for future simulations that add the undersides of the wings, a wider range of flight, other viewing angles and distances, and more kinds of background.

For butterflies, bright wings may do more than attract attention. Once those wings begin moving, their patterns could help keep predators from landing a successful strike.

The full study was published in the journal Nature.

Verifiable AI Needs Receipts, Not Confidence Scores

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ask an AI agent whether a treasury really bought back $50M of its own token and you get a fluent yes, usually with a source that cannot be opened. No block height. No transaction hash. No payload with a timestamp. In a market where money moves on claims, that is the whole problem in one sentence.

What a receipt has to contain

For a stranger to check a claim without trusting me, the record needs six things.

  1. The primary source. An RPC call, a transaction receipt, an official API response. Not a screenshot, not a summary, not another agent's post.
  2. A pin. A block height, a timestamp, or a content hash. Without a pin, a balance claim becomes unfalsifiable the moment the balance moves.
  3. The exact payload and its sha256. If the source changes, the hash changes, and the record shows it.
  4. The rule that turns the payload into a verdict. "The Transfer log amount must equal the published amount to the wei" is a rule. "It looks consistent" is not.
  5. An objection. Something in the pipeline has to be allowed to attack the evidence and send it back.
  6. The verdict, with the reason it was reached.

Five stages, one ledger

ClaimAudit runs a question through five stages. Each stage appends to H-MEM, an append-only JSONL ledger, so a run can be replayed record by record.

  • Planner splits the question into atomic claims and classifies each by shape: balance, tx, tvl, audit, partnership, free text. A claim with no machine-readable source is classified that way up front.
  • Retriever fetches primary evidence for that shape. For a balance it is eth_call balanceOf; for a transaction it is eth_getTransactionReceipt. It records the endpoint, the call, and the sha256 of the response.
  • Verifier attacks what came back. It compares the claim to the payload, checks that the read was pinned, and can reject the evidence and force a second fetch.
  • Adjudicator maps the result to one of four verdicts and writes the reason.
  • Writer renders only what survived. Nothing the adjudicator refused appears in the report as fact.

One record, verbatim

This is a real run from the repository, claim c1 of a FlapJax treasury question, trimmed to the fields that carry the argument.

{"memoryId":"c1","action":"create","actor":"planner","newState":{"confidence":0.5}, "details":{"step":"plan","status":"classified","detail":"kind=balance params=['amount','decimals','holder','token']"}} {"memoryId":"c1","action":"read","actor":"retriever","newState":{"confidence":0.7}, "details":{"step":"retrieve","status":"fetched","detail":"balanceOf(0x7c34e9e2...) at latest via https://bsc-dataseed1.defibit.io","evidence_digest":"9f401ecbdcf1680f..."}} {"memoryId":"c1","action":"feedback","actor":"verifier","newState":{"confidence":0.5}, "details":{"step":"critique","status":"rejected","detail":"balance mismatch: claim 266765359.134056992117230299 vs chain 271513297.483779726019304006 (relative 1.779818%); claim is not block-pinned; the balance may have moved since it was written"}} {"memoryId":"c1","action":"update","actor":"adjudicator","newState":{"confidence":0.5}, "details":{"step":"adjudicate","status":"unverified","detail":"evidence exists but does not settle the claim"}}


Read the second record closely. The retriever asked for the balance at latest, not at a block. It got 271,513,297.483779726019304006 against a claim of 266,765,359.134056992117230299, a 1.78% difference. The verifier rejected the evidence for two reasons: the numbers do not match, and the read was never pinned. The adjudicator wrote UNVERIFIED rather than BROKE, because an unpinned balance cannot prove a claim false. It can only fail to prove it true.


That distinction is the difference between an auditor and a search engine. A search engine returns the closest number. An auditor says which number it can defend, and why.

The verifier has to be allowed to say no

In October I audited a token treasury's daily buyback on BNB Chain. The claim was narrow: 0.01 BNB spent, a specific amount of tokens received, no sales into the pool. Across 60 transactions, the figures matched. Two residuals did not: 0.0000005546 BNB and 0.000001196712783217 BNB of non-buy outflow. Small numbers, but exactly the kind of thing a receipt is supposed to account for. The operator accepted the finding and paid the claim. A later claim was rejected because its evidence did not settle the question. Both outcomes are the product working.

What it checks, and what it refuses

  • balance: eth_call balanceOf on BSC. Working.
  • tx: eth_getTransactionReceipt. Working.
  • tvl: DefiLlama protocol API. Working, labelled a secondary source.
  • audit: needs an issuer or auditor URL. Blocked by default.
  • partnership: needs a statement from both sides. Blocked by default.
  • free text: blocked by default.


BLOCKED is the verdict most agent products are missing, and it is the one that keeps a system honest when the source is a press release or a partnership announcement. If there is no auditor URL and no statement from both sides, the correct output is not "likely true". It is "I cannot verify this".


Refusal has a cost: coverage. An agent that blocks everything is useless in a different way. That tension does not get resolved by a better prompt. It gets resolved by deciding which claim shapes have a primary source at all, and being explicit about the rest.

What this does not do

It does not establish truth in general. It checks claims that have a machine-readable primary source. TVL pulled from an aggregator is a secondary source and is labelled as one. It does not remove the human on the other side either: the operator still decides whether the finding matters. And the critic layer is a single adversarial pass today. Multiple critics, and a way to score their disagreements, is an open problem I have not solved.

Run it

The code and the ledger output are public: github.com/kaminariouji/claimaudit. The repository has deterministic fixtures, a three-pane UI, and a selftest. Every record quoted above came from out/selftest-flapjax.hmem/ledger.jsonl. Clone it, run the selftest, and check that the numbers match.


Verifiable AI is not a model that says "trust me". It is a system that produces a record a stranger can replay. The record does not have to be a blockchain. It has to be pinned, hashed, and complete.


The next generation of AI infrastructure will be judged less by how often it is right and more by whether someone else can check it. Build for the second test.

Comparing Lamine Yamal’s incredible 2026-27 stats to Lionel Messi’s breakout Ballon d’Or 2008-09 season

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Something special is happening with Lamine Yamal. And the longer his extraordinary start to the 2026-27 season continues, the harder it becomes to avoid those inevitable comparisons with Lionel Messi.

Ever since Yamal burst onto the scene as a 15-year-old, the Messi comparisons have followed him. They’ve often felt unfair – impossible to live up to.

“I don’t compare myself to him, because I don’t compare myself to anyone – and much less with Messi,” the Barcelona wonderkid told reporters back in 2025.

“I don’t think the comparison makes sense, with Messi even less – I’m going to enjoy myself, and be myself.”

But the parallels are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore. Yamal has already won a European Championship and World Cup with Spain, back-to-back La Liga titles with Barcelona and finished runner-up in the Ballon d’Or. He’s still only 19.

If you take a step back and look at it objectively, he’s actually considerably further ahead of where Messi was at the same age and stage of his Barcelona career.

There’s a fascinating parallel between where Yamal finds himself right now and where Messi was in the summer of 2008. Both had already established themselves among the world’s best, with a Ballon d’Or runner-up finish to show for it.

Messi had finished second to Cristiano Ronaldo, laying the foundations for a rivalry that would define the next decade and a half. Yamal, meanwhile, finished second to Ousmane Dembele last year and then finished third behind Harry Kane and Kylian Mbappe.

The La Masia graduate was already brilliant before Pep Guardiola arrived. His 13 goals and 16 assists in 2007-08 were impressive, but they didn’t quite reflect the full extent of his talent.

Then came the 2008-09 treble-winning season. Guardiola built a team that allowed Messi to flourish, and the Argentinian responded with 38 goals as Barcelona won the treble. That was the season he became truly unstoppable, the eight-time Ballon d’Or winner who’s long been spoken about in the same breath as Pele and Diego Maradona in ‘greatest of all time’ debates.

Yamal has been similarly brilliant without yet producing those outrageous Messi-esque numbers on a consistent basis. But 12 goals and 11 assists in his first 14 appearances suggest something might be changing. He’s actually ahead of Messi’s output at the same stage of 2008-09, when the Argentinian had 11 goals and eight assists to his name.

It’s early days, obviously. But if Yamal can maintain anything approaching this level, a third successive Ballon d’Or runner-up finish might soon look like the final stepping stone before his own era of dominance.
