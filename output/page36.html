GOG wants to bring back physical PC games, just like in the good old days—but the logistics are ‘very complicated’

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The death of physical media is truly a sign of the times. In a world of eroding privacy, ownership rights, and consumer satisfaction, any morsel of good service is seen as God himself reaching out. The world we were born into is no longer here, but so long as companies like GOG exist, perhaps the future is not so grim.

As reported by Games Radar, GOG would love to bring back physical PC discs, something that hasn’t been around for well over a decade now. Sure, collector’s editions and unique prints exist, but physical PC games are, put simply, a thing of the past. Digital stores dominate this side of gaming, and when we had 15 years of lightning-fast development bringing us huge and incredibly quick SSDs, as well as amazing internet connections, why wouldn’t they?

Steam is pretty much as good as a digital store can be regarding ownership and DRM-free copies. Things simply fell into place like a jigsaw puzzle, and so here we are with the vast majority of PC gamers not even owning disc drivers anymore. In spite of that, though, GOG remains committed to reigniting this lost art, with its senior business manager Marcin Paczynski saying it’s his “dream” to see it come to fruition.

Good old days. Photo by Destructoid

“Ideally, my dream would be to be able to buy physical versions of those games on GOG. You go to your shelf on GOG, you click on the game that you would like to receive a physical copy of, you order it, and it gets to you. That would be a perfect scenario.”

In this case, you would actually get the best of both worlds: a digital copy, as well as a physical one if you want to REALLY keep it stored in a safe space. Keep in mind that GOG sells DRM-free copies only, meaning no publisher can take it away from you, nor do you need any underlying software to play these games, turning the disc into a sort of collectible if anything.

Creating a whole business based on this idea is “a very, very complicated thing,” Paczynski told Games Radar, not least because it’s “totally different from what we are currently doing.”

“Because we are talking about physical media, we need to think about logistics. We need to think about storage,” he said. Getting licensing deals and approvals from respective publishers is yet another hurdle to surmount, but the company remains very keen on the idea. “I really hope that at some point we will be able to do it. We’ll see,” Paczynski concluded.

Physical games are on their way out—not because no one wants them, but because publishers merely want to cut costs and save money on providing you the one service they have. We saw that even in the case of Sony, a company incredibly devoted to killing off not only physical games but perhaps even Blu-ray as a technology, its own first-party games had 30 percent of total sales be physical. Of course, Sony has to relinquish a 30-percent cut taken by retailers and must fund the production of boxes and, well, the printing of the discs themselves, which comes at a great overall cost at such a high volume.

So, physical media is very much beloved by the gaming world. But with everything being stupid expensive nowadays and corporations becoming greedier and greedier, it’s become excess fat that must be trimmed.

Ozone pollution reached dangerous levels for billions of people in 2026

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The ozone most people have heard of sits miles overhead and blocks the sun’s ultraviolet light.

The same gas also forms near the ground when pollutants from vehicles, factories and other sources react in sunlight.

Down there it irritates lungs instead of protecting them.

A new analysis from Europe’s atmosphere monitoring service estimates that only 5 percent of the world’s people breathed ozone at a level the World Health Organization calls good this year.

Almost 40 percent were exposed to the highest category the service tracks, and Asia was hit hardest. The figures come from a global model fed with satellite and ground readings, not from a monitor outside every home.

Global pollution remains widespread

The Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, or CAMS, is run by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) for the European Commission.

Its new report, Atmosphere Watch, covers ozone, wildfire smoke and desert dust from January 1 to September 15.

Laurence Rouil directs CAMS. In written answers sent to Earth.com through the Copernicus press office, Rouil said the year’s surprise was how many big pollution events piled up at once.

“When we brought together the huge amount of data produced every day by CAMS, the most striking finding was that more than half of the world’s population was still exposed to unhealthy levels of air pollution,” Rouil said.

Heat and sunlight make ozone

Nothing emits ground-level ozone directly. Nitrogen oxides from burning fuel react in sunlight with organic vapors from traffic, industry, household solvents, plants and wildfires.

Hot, still summer weather speeds the reactions and lets the gas build up.

Ozone lasts a few days near the ground and a few weeks higher up. That is long enough to drift across borders and oceans.

During the heatwave of July 28, CAMS calculated that ozone arriving in Bergamo, Italy, from elsewhere in the Northern Hemisphere mattered as much as every human source in Europe combined.

The report measures exposure the way the World Health Organization does. It takes each day’s highest eight-hour ozone reading and averages it over the six worst months of the year.

The WHO guideline is 60 micrograms per cubic meter. Above 110, CAMS calls the air extremely poor, and that is the band almost 40 percent of people fell into.

Asia breathes the worst ozone

CAMS estimates that six in ten people in Asia experienced extremely poor ozone levels, while nine in ten experienced air rated poor or worse.

Delhi, Cairo, Milan, Mexico City and Rio de Janeiro had some of the highest exposure on their continents.

Europe has cut every pollutant its air quality law regulates except ozone, the service’s annual assessments show. This summer’s repeated heatwaves pushed half of Europeans into poor air or worse.

European ozone standards limit how often concentrations can exceed 120 micrograms per cubic meter, and much of the continent struggled with elevated levels.

In North America, more than two thirds of people breathed poor ozone or worse, mostly on the two U.S. coasts and in big cities.

Decades of emission cuts have left ozone there largely unchanged, the report says, partly because wildfires supply the ingredients.

Oceania, South America and much of Africa did far better. Everyone in Oceania, 81 percent of South Americans and 56 percent of Africans had good or fair ozone levels.

Smoke and dust crossed oceans

On July 22, fires in France’s Gironde and Landes regions burned close to 124,000 acres (50,000 hectares) and forced 40,000 people from their homes.

Their smoke fouled the air hundreds of miles away. Fires near Ávila and Madrid in Spain burned a similar area and sent 56,000 people fleeing.

Belgium’s High Fens fire in August was the country’s largest in a century, and its smoke crossed Germany into Poland.

In July, wildfire smoke from Canada’s Northwest Territories and Ontario spread over the northern United States.

Cities from Chicago to Philadelphia issued air advisories days before the World Cup final in New Jersey, and storms washed the air clean just before the match.

In Indonesia, peat fires on Borneo, fed by a building El Niño, pushed fine particle levels to several hundred micrograms per cubic meter.

Saharan dust crossed the Atlantic toward the Caribbean, and the Canary Islands topped Europe’s list of cities with dust alerts.

Advice for bad ozone days

Asked by Earth.com what someone in a high-ozone region should do on the worst days, Rouil pointed to the WHO.

Ozone forecasts are now widely available and increasingly reliable, Rouil said, so people can act before an episode peaks.

“WHO advises reducing prolonged or intense outdoor physical activity, especially in the afternoon,” Rouil said.

Further guidelines recommend that children, older adults, and people with respiratory or cardiovascular conditions take extra precautions, he told Earth.com.

The director also said that the fixes are known. Decades of European rules have brought down sulfur dioxide, fine particles and nitrogen dioxide, Rouil said, and the job now is to keep that momentum.

Tracking ozone exposure

The exposure figures are model estimates rather than direct measurements at every location.

CAMS combines satellite and ground observations with a model that tracks more than 50 chemicals, then matches the results to population maps.

A model fills the gaps where no monitor stands, but it isn’t the same as a sensor on the street outside.

The report stops at September 15 and doesn’t count deaths from this year’s ozone. For scale, the WHO estimates that outdoor air pollution causes more than 4 million early deaths a year.

The Health Effects Institute’s State of Global Air report finds ozone exposure in Asia has risen in recent years.

How much of Europe’s summer ozone came from its own wildfires is still an open question, and CAMS says it is actively researching it.

With a strong El Niño building and fires already burning in Indonesia, the service expects more fires to make headlines in the months ahead.

Atmosphere Watch 2026 is a monitoring report published by the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service on September 30.

Assessing Common-Mode Failures in Redundant Power Distribution Systems

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In August 2011, a data center in Dallas lost power for roughly five hours. The facility had the usual markers of resilience — multiple utility service entrances, backup generators, and UPS systems — yet a single automatic transfer switch failed, and the building went dark anyway. Restoration was slow for a telling reason: as the operator later explained, bypassing a transfer switch "is not a simple fix." The redundancy on the one-line diagram was real. It just did not sit where the failure occurred.

That is the uncomfortable pattern behind many critical-infrastructure outages. The Uptime Institute, which tracks major outages across the industry, consistently finds that power remains the single largest cause — and that the failures rarely trace to a shortage of generating capacity. They trace to the distribution, transfer, and control path between the sources and the load, and to the people operating it. Two utility sources, two transformers, two switchgear lineups, and a standby generator can make a facility look impressively resilient on a single-line diagram. Yet a deceptively simple question is still hard to answer: can the loads that matter actually remain energized after the failure of any one relevant component?

The answer depends on far more than the number of sources. Resilience follows the entire power path — from the upstream supply through protection, switching, controls, distribution, physical rooms, and the equipment being served. A second feeder does not create meaningful redundancy if both paths converge through an unrecognized common point of failure.

Redundancy is a system property, not an equipment count

Engineers use concepts such as N+1 or 2N to describe capacity and redundancy arrangements, but the label needs a defined boundary. Two incoming services do not necessarily mean two independent distribution paths to the final load. Even when each service has sufficient capacity, both paths may share a transfer switch, control power supply, communications device, physical space, or operational procedure.

A good review begins by identifying what must survive. Is the requirement uninterrupted power to particular loads, rapid restoration within an agreed interval, or only the ability to move noncritical demand after an outage? These are different design objectives. The answers affect the selection of equipment and the acceptance testing that proves the design works.

A power system can also be electrically redundant but operationally fragile. Protective devices must coordinate adequately for the available source and configuration; transfer equipment must work in the credible conditions it will encounter; operators must be able to execute the sequence safely; and temporary conditions during maintenance must be understood.

A 700-kW critical-load example

Imagine a hypothetical facility with 700 kW of coincident critical demand. It has two supply paths, each with equipment nominally capable of supporting the critical demand under the assumed operating conditions. A designer might reasonably describe the upstream arrangement as redundant.

Now follow the path downstream. Both supplies terminate at a single automatic transfer assembly, which feeds a common critical-load bus. That transfer assembly also depends on one control-power circuit. The upstream supplies are diverse, but a failed transfer mechanism or lost control supply can leave the downstream loads without the intended alternate path.

Nothing in this example means that a transfer switch is inherently inappropriate. Shared transfer equipment is common and may be entirely suitable where the agreed reliability objective allows it. The lesson is narrower and more useful: if a component is common to all paths feeding a load, it must be included in the reliability claim and evaluated against the actual performance objective.

Now consider maintenance. The facility plans to replace a section of switchgear. During the work, one supply path is removed from service. The remaining path still supports the load on paper, but a protective-device maintenance bypass, an unavailable tie breaker, or an untested switching sequence might change how the system performs under a fault. The normal-condition one-line is no longer enough; engineers need a validated temporary-condition one-line as well.

Worked failure assessment: two 1,000-kW paths, one transfer dependency

Here is a concrete expansion of the 700-kW example. Every rating, load, duration, and outcome below is fictional and assumed, supplied to demonstrate an assessment method rather than to describe a facility incident. Source A and Source B each feed a common automatic-transfer assembly, and each has a hypothetical 1,000-kW usable capacity under the modeled operating conditions. The downstream bus serves a constant 700-kW coincident load. Of that, 150 kW is supplied through a short-duration UPS with an assumed 10 minutes of usable autonomy at that load; the remaining 550 kW has no ride-through. A single transfer controller supervises the transfer assembly, and a latent controller fault is present but has gone undetected.

At 14:00, Source A becomes unavailable. Source B is healthy and has adequate steady-state capacity, but the transfer controller fails to initiate the permitted transition. The shared downstream bus becomes de-energized. The UPS temporarily carries only its 150-kW protected portion. At minute 10, its assumed usable reserve is exhausted. Authorized personnel restore the bus at minute 35, after verifying the condition, following an approved recovery procedure, and confirming the receiving source and equipment are suitable. The 35-minute restoration is an illustrative assumption, not a prediction of field response.

Equivalently, the 550-kW group loses 35 minutes (320.8 kWh) and the 150-kW protected group loses the final 25 minutes (62.5 kWh). This is an accounting illustration, not a validated availability, cost, battery-discharge or energy-not-served study; real demand, transfer transient behavior, UPS derating and approved operating modes would change the result.

The five dependency layers I would examine

1. Electrical capacity. Each expected operating configuration needs an appropriately evaluated load flow, equipment loading review, and assessment of voltage and power quality where relevant. Nameplate totals are a starting point, not proof that a source can accept a particular group of loads. Starting currents, load steps, derating, and equipment duty may govern.

2. Protection and selectivity. Different source configurations can change available fault current and the operation of protective devices. The objective is to understand whether a localized fault can be cleared as intended without unnecessarily removing a larger portion of the system. Detailed studies are required; a blog-level diagram cannot establish selective coordination.

3. Control and communication. Remote supervision, transfer logic, interlocks, sensing, and control power have to be treated as equipment dependencies. An independent protective relay may continue to operate when communications are lost; a remote transfer command may not. The design documents should make that distinction explicit.

4. Physical separation. Two circuits routed through the same vulnerable space or the same cable tray may not offer the desired protection against a localized incident. Fire, water, impact, thermal conditions, and access constraints can defeat pathways simultaneously. Physical diversity needs to be evaluated against plausible hazards, not assumed from different circuit labels.

5. Human and maintenance dependencies. A system that requires a complex manual transfer within minutes should be evaluated against realistic staffing, procedures, switching authority, lockout/tagout requirements, and communication between teams. A second source that nobody can safely place in service is a limited resilience resource.

Map the failure, then map the consequence

A useful method is to build a dependency table for each essential load group. The rows are credible component failures or planned outages; the columns capture load consequences. For example:

This table does not predict numerical reliability. It does something more immediate: exposes the claims that have not yet been demonstrated. Numerical availability calculations can be useful, but only when failure data, repair assumptions, common-cause effects, and operational boundaries are credible.

Cyber-physical dependencies belong in the same review

A modern distribution system may include networked metering, supervisory controls, remote access, and integrated alarm platforms. These technologies improve situational awareness but can also create misleading assumptions about independence. A compromised dashboard may not interrupt power, while compromised supervisory control could matter enormously if it can issue commands. The distinction must be established through the actual architecture.

NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 emphasizes OT security with attention to reliability and safety. NIST SP 800-160 Volume 2 also promotes engineering systems that can withstand and recover from adverse conditions. Applied to electrical power, these principles argue for reviewing digital and physical dependencies together rather than allocating one set of drawings to electrical engineers and another to cybersecurity staff.

One practical deliverable is a cross-domain dependency register. For each essential load, show its electrical source path, switching devices, control power, communications connections, required operator interventions, and known shared physical locations. When a change order adds a monitoring gateway or combines two control cabinets, the register should be updated. Otherwise, a small integration decision can quietly invalidate an earlier redundancy assumption.

Make failure testing part of the design narrative

A one-line diagram shows connections. A commissioning plan should show behavior. I would ask the project team to define several credible loss-of-function tests: loss of primary source, loss of a monitoring network, loss of transfer-control indication, restoration after a disturbance, and operation during a planned source outage. Each test needs safety precautions, permitted methods, responsible personnel, and measurable acceptance criteria developed for the specific facility.

Tests should not involve live hazardous switching or intentional disruption without an approved engineering and operational procedure. Where full functional testing is unsafe or impractical, analyses, factory tests, simulated inputs, and partial demonstrations can provide supporting evidence—but their limitations should be recorded.

An important question for every redundancy review

The phrase "we have two sources" should be the beginning of the discussion, not the conclusion. The question worth asking is: Which single component, shared condition, or human action could still take down this supposedly redundant load?

Good electrical design is not only about adding capacity or equipment. It is about understanding dependencies so that the facility's performance claim survives the operating conditions for which it was intended. That is where redundancy becomes resilience.
