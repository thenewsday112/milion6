When Jimmy Chin was in his twenties, living out of a 1989 Subaru Loyale somewhere between Yosemite and the Tetons, he kept trying to explain climbing to his father. His father, a librarian at Mankato State University in Minnesota, would listen, and then say: “Our language is 5,000 years old, and there is no word for what you do.”

Chin has spent the decades since building a vocabulary for it anyway, as a climber, a photographer, and the Oscar-winning director of Free Solo featuring Alex Honnold, and his latest film, Everest: The Other Side, which documents Jim Morrison and Hilaree Nelson’s attempt to climb and ski down the North Face of the world’s tallest mountain.

His new book, Rules to Live By (Celadon), presents other people’s words, but is also part memoir. It’s built from ten conversations with people who have lived on their own terms: Yvon Chouinard, Yo-Yo Ma, Simone Biles, Ridley Scott, Kelly Slater, Annette Bening, Lynsey Addario, Kris Tompkins, Alex Honnold, and Bill King, a retired command master chief of SEAL Team 6. He asks each of them some version of the same question: In the end, what really matters? And in the end, we learn what matters most to Jimmy Chin as well.

Earlier this month, I sat down with Chin onstage at the Bell House in Brooklyn. It was his first book event, and he confessed he hadn’t reread the book since turning it in. “There’s a reason I’m a filmmaker,” he told the crowd. “Writing is really, really difficult.” What followed covered violin practice, an undisclosed military base, and a missing sock. The conversation has been edited and condensed.

Jimmy Chin in conversation with Outside Editorial Director Kevin Sintumuang. (Photo: Daniel Dumas)

“If you’re going to do it, do it well”

OUTSIDE: You write that you’re “the only son of an only son of an only son.” That sounds like a lot of pressure.

Jimmy Chin: I started violin when I was three, and it became two hours of practice a day. Then martial arts, two hours a day. Then homework. That was my weekdays, and it was all I knew. But I learned discipline, and there was a very high expectation for excellence. My dad always said, “If you’re going to do it, you might as well do it well.” I hated that. Sometimes I still struggle with it.

There’s a scene in the book where you’re practicing violin, staring out the window at the forest. When did the forest win?

Chin: Anytime I did well in school, my reward was running off into the woods. There were no rules out there. My parents were librarians, so I always had a lot of books, and later, when they asked how I ended up doing this, I said, “It’s your fault. You gave me all these books.” I found climbing in college, and it was the first thing that felt like a calling. Growing up, I literally thought there were three jobs: lawyer, doctor, or professor. So after graduation I told my parents I’d take one year off, get it out of my system, and then pursue my real career. Which was a total lie. From 21 to 28 I lived out of my car, or in a cave in Camp 4, or in a chicken coop in Jackson.

What were those phone calls home like?

Chin: My sister, who went to Stanford and worked at Yale for 18 years, was my huge advocate. I’d ask her how Mom was doing, and she’d say, “Mom just keeps saying, ‘I raised a homeless man.” With my dad, I deeply wanted him to understand me. People see the films and photographs and ask about the greatest risk I’ve ever taken. It definitely wasn’t a mountain. It was being in my early twenties, full of doubt. There are a lot of storm days, sitting in a cave with water dripping, soaking wet, thinking, What am I doing here? Meanwhile your friends are going to grad school and starting careers.

And during this time, you were in a reggae band.

Chin: I dropped violin after high school and picked up the guitar. The furthest thing I could think of from a concerto in D minor was covering Bob Marley. The whole band was classically trained kids. Everybody was technically skilled, so we could play whatever we wanted.

Did you ever put out an album?

Chin: [Laughs.] No…we weren’t that good.

A Constellation of Answers

How did you choose the ten people in the book?

Chin: My documentaries take four, five, six, seven years. You have to bleed for them. You can only make so many films in a lifetime, and there are so many people I admire who I’ll never make a film about. I wanted the book to be a constellation: totally different backgrounds, totally different careers. After these extraordinary lives, what have you discovered really matters? What did success look like when you started, and what does it look like now? I had just turned 50, so I was looking back at my life in phases and thinking about what was to come.

An Anomaly Among Anomalies

You almost didn’t make Free Solo.

Chin: After Meru, producers kept asking what was next. I mentioned to my friend Evan Hayes, who became a producer on Free Solo, that I’d been thinking about a documentary about Alex. I hadn’t even finished his name before he said, “That’s it.” National Geographic green-lit it immediately. I hadn’t even made a pitch deck.

I’d been filming and photographing Alex for ten years. I’ve worked with some of the greatest adventure athletes of our time, people at the pinnacle of their sports. All of them were anomalies within their sport. In my mind, Alex was an anomaly among anomalies. Even athletes at that level were confounded by what he was doing.

So I called him: “Good news, the film’s green-lit.” He said, “Great, because I’m thinking about soloing El Cap.” I said, “No. Not making that film.” I literally called Evan and said, “I’m out. Done.”

Why?

Chin: Among his friends, none of us would even talk about that idea. We didn’t want to put it in the ether. We’d seen the trajectory. He soloed Moonlight Buttress, which was outrageous. Then he soloed Half Dome. We were talking about breaking speed records on Half Dome, not free soloing it. It wasn’t hard to see what came after that. We just didn’t want to say it out loud. And then he dropped it in my lap. My fear was obvious: What if filming him causes him to fall? That’s not something I could live with.

What changed your mind?

Chin: He was going to do it anyway. I knew Alex really well. I took him on his first international expedition, and we’d done so much together. And I trusted him. Alex was the most calculated person I’d ever met. He had instinct, he was extremely smart, and it was never about external validation. He’s also straightforward, to a fault. He’s been well trained by now, with all the awards. But when I first met him, he’d say things and we’d be like, “You can’t say that.” If he isn’t comfortable doing something, he’ll tell you, and tell you why it’s stupid.

So we moved forward, and I gave my crew three rules. They were almost all elite professional climbers, because that’s the only team you could have: a short list of people who are both. Number one, we’re his friends first. Second, we’re climbers; you think like a climber before you pull out the camera. Third, we’re filmmakers. The film’s needs can never supersede Alex’s needs.

People saw the brain scan in the film and decided Alex was superhuman. You write that he resents that.

Chin: Because it’s just not true. He says, “I feel fear. I’ve always felt fear.” One of the reasons he started soloing was that he was so shy he was too scared to ask people to climb with him. So he climbed by himself. His process was to step outside his comfort zone a little at a time, in a regimented, disciplined way, until the things that scared him were well within it. The film took almost four years. But he’d been training for that climb his entire career. We left the brain scan open to interpretation, but that’s his process.

Alive, and Alive Inside

In 2017 you were trying to dial down the risk in your life. Then Conrad Anker and Alex asked you to come to Antarctica, and you went. You argue in the book that children need parents who are alive, but also parents who feel alive inside. Is that an argument or a good excuse?

Chin: Those are really personal decisions. It’s impossible to justify to a child that their parent died in the mountains doing what I do. I don’t know if there’s a right answer. I’ve lost a lot of friends in the mountains, some of them parents. After my friend Hilaree Nelson died, Bill King wrote to me, and I wrote back: “The mountains give us so much life, yet they take so much life away. It never makes sense, but we understand.” He quoted that line in his retirement speech. So it’s not an excuse. I believe your kids want you alive physically, and alive as yourself. For better or worse, this is my calling.

How do you tell fear that’s protecting you from fear that’s psyching you out?

Chin: Fear can sharpen your senses or paralyze you. When things are going sideways, panic never serves you. So you break it down: How much of this is perceived risk, and how much is actual risk? That exercise alone brings you back into the present. You set the perceived risks aside, focus on what’s real, and mitigate it. Then you make a plan and execute.

Jimmy Chin at work on the Northwest Face of Half Dome. (Photo: Courtesy of Mikey Schaefer via Celadon Books)

The Question at the Undisclosed Location

I love how all Bill King wanted to know was why you turned around 100 meters from the summit of Meru.

Chin: I got a surprise call from SEAL Team 6 asking me to come talk at the base. They gave me a date, a time, and an airport. I got on a plane, they flew me to an undisclosed location, took my phone. I hadn’t been nervous speaking in years, but these guys roll in and size you up. Someone told me every member of Team 6 had watched Meru. When I finished, a guy in the back cut to the chase: “How did you decide to turn around 100 meters from the summit?” I knew the entire reason I was there was to answer that question. That was Bill.

When you’ve put years into an objective and it’s within sight, and things aren’t right, it takes extraordinary discipline to walk away. On Meru we had seven days of food and were on the wall for 19. We crossed so many of our own lines, deliberately. You set a line, reach it, evaluate, set another. The more you do that, the more invested you are, and the harder it is to turn around.

You and the SEALs share a no-asshole rule. Tell me about that. 

Chin: I asked Bill what trait gets people through training. He didn’t flinch: “Humor.” If a guy is getting his ass handed to him and he’s still cracking jokes, they’re not worried about him. It’s the same in the mountains. Humor changes the environment and brings you back to good, rational decisions. My selection process isn’t as serious as theirs, but these are the people holding a rope for you. You can’t have people who only think about themselves. And you can feel it. In conversation, it’s “I, I, I, me.”

Yvon’s Wisdom

Yvon Chouinard is the closest thing you have to a guru. He tells you, “It’s so easy to complicate your life. It’s so much harder to simplify it.” Have you managed it?

Chin: On expeditions, yes. That’s part of why I love them: the best version of me comes out. We’re brutal about simplifying, because every ounce counts. Everything is about efficiency, even how much stress you allow, even your heart rate. I can stay really calm in the worst situations. But if I can’t find my matching sock at home, it’s all over. In the mountains, when things are going sideways, I’m like, oh, this is casual.

You have kids. Do you worry about whether the wild places you’ve spent your life in will still be there for them?

Chin: It’s what the last pages of the book are about. I think of it with Yvon on one end and Kris Tompkins on the other. They’ve both had a tremendous impact on my life, and I’ve talked with both of them about this a lot.

Yvon always says, “Everybody has different tools. Whatever tool you have in your toolbox, whatever you’re good at, that’s what you should contribute.” It’s not only activism. His example is that if you’re an accountant, there are a ton of grassroots organizations that need accountants. So you ask: What can I contribute? Contribute what you have.

And what’s in your toolbox?

Chin: Storytelling, and a platform. That’s why I made Wild Life after Free Solo. I had the chance to finance a film about conservationists, which I probably wouldn’t have had otherwise. It’s about Doug and Kris Tompkins, who preserved something like 17 million acres over a 25-year dream of creating national parks. I’ve brought my kids down there. Those are the things I hope will make a difference: telling these stories, and putting them in a book. What Yvon and Kris have to say is so much more compelling than what I have to say about it.

The coffee cups were almost dry again, and so were the hunting stories. Milt’s electric clock showed 6 a.m.—time to go. Moments later, the six of us were swallowed up in the gloom of a dense fog. Four inches of fluffy new snow muffled our footsteps as we began the trek to our deer stands.

There was a nervous excitement in the air that morning. We knew we were going to see deer. Rarely had we been so confident on an opening day. Almost 200 acres of willow swamp and fields belonged to us that morning and the preseason scouting we’d done plus the new snow added up to nothing but good news. The anticipation was almost crackling in the air around us.

I reached my stand first. I brushed off the loose snow and stood listening for a moment. The absolute stillness meant that my ears would be about as helpful as my eyes for the first few hours—longer if the fog remained. An inch of frost covered every limb of the big willow I occupied. I backed into a collar-high branch and a handful of the frozen dew fell, shaking any remaining sleep from me.

To my left was a standing field of short corn. To my right, the edge of a brushy swale wound its way down to the big willow swamp 200 yards away. In front of me, a fallen willow limb blocked my view. I settled comfortably against the trunk of the big tree and surveyed the fields. Sometimes the fog would allow me 80 yards of view, at other times less than 40.

When at last, according to my watch, it was 8 a.m., I knew that the sun had to be up. The light through the fog, however, had changed little. From the cornfield to my left came sounds of movement—short crunching noises. My ears strained to identify them. A cock pheasant crowed not far away and I relaxed a bit. A few moments later I watched a bluejay working over a cob. Birds were making the sounds, I told myself.

For nearly 30 minutes I continued to hear the sounds: a few crunches followed by silence. Then I saw the outline of a deer’s back moving slowly through the rows. Head down, the deer came closer, quartering across the field. At last it raised its head. For the first time I could see its crown of antlers. Not again, I said to myself, a bit of dread mixing with my excitement.

Seven times before, I’d managed to get shots at trophy bucks and seven times I’d missed—sometimes with a bow, sometimes with a shotgun. I’d got my chance nearly every season I’d hunted, and I’d gone home empty-handed every time. My hunting companions marveled at my incredible knack for being in the right place at the right time and at my equally incredible bad shooting. Despite hours of practice, something always seemed to go awry at the moment of truth. Not again, I groaned inwardly.

Outdoor Life

When it seemed that the buck was coming no closer, I raised my shotgun. The iron sights of my new slug barrel came to rest on the target—still more than 60 yards away. I fired.

The blast shook frost from the willow branches. For a moment, the buck disappeared. When I could see again, he was on the move.

Again I fired and again and again—as the buck crossed from left to right. As the big deer quartered toward me, he was hidden behind the frosty fallen branches of the willow. Suddenly he reappeared before me and I shot once more. The buck stopped just 40 yards away, his wide, dark rack swiveling like a radar antenna.

My gun was empty. I had to reload. Frantically I fumbled deep in my pocket for the shells. They were still in the box. This one time I hadn’t opened a second box just in case. Almost hysterically I tore the cardboard and threw a slug into the chamber. I took aim at the buck’s neck and fired. I thought I saw the deer cringe before he loped away down the swale toward the big swamp. Then I lost sight of him in the fog and frosty willows.

I can’t adequately describe my feelings. Despair is as close as any named emotion comes. I was beside myself. I couldn’t believe I’d missed again. The deer was right there, at the range I’d sighted-in my new barrel for, and still I’d missed. I scrambled down off the stand and began to search for the deer’s trail in hopes of finding blood, hair, anything that might mean the buck was hit. I reloaded the shotgun then and, to my disgust, found two shells in the magazine. In the excitement, I’d evidently put more slugs in the gun than I knew. I could have shot again—more despair.

In the snow, I followed the buck’s tracks across the swale, along the edge of the cornfield on the other side, way past the point at which I’d lost sight of him. For more than 200 yards I moved slowly beside the tracks. Then I was close to the swamp and my friend Steve’s stand. He’d heard the shots, but hadn’t glimpsed any deer passing by. “Maybe it was hidden in the fog,” he said.

I walked the trail many times that morning, tracing and retracing the deer’s movements in the snow. A sad realization was weighing more heavily with every step. Grudgingly, I had to admit that I must have missed number eight.

The next day the hunt ended and I went home, but the memory of the hunt stayed with me. When I tried to sleep, that morning’s events played back like a worn instant replay. Again and again I saw the buck cross and again and again I watched my shots as if I were looking for my mistake. I tried to spot and correct my error, but even in my dreams the buck escaped.

Five days later, a friend and I took to those same willows in search of pheasants. He’d heard my tale and seemed to understand. As we approached where I’d last seen the buck, I took a breath to call my friend. I wanted to tell him where the deer had been when my shots had missed. But before I could form the words, Tim stopped dead in his tracks.

“Oh no,” he said with a groan. “Oh no! There’s a huge buck lying over here.”

My first thought was that he was joking. But he wouldn’t have been, couldn’t have been. I was trembling as I waded the deep grass toward him. A sick sort of feeling began to knot my stomach. The sight of a shining antler almost put me to my knees.

There in the thin yellow grass lay my buck.

He lay there as he must have lain after the final shot that Saturday morning. He was crouched like a sleeping dog, facing the trail that I’d mistaken for his. My last shot had gone very low, into the left hindquarter and up into the body cavity. The wound hadn’t bled. That was why I’d found no blood in the snow.

I stood there in the silence thinking quickly, eyes burning. Tim shared the silence. He understood. I must have passed within a dozen yards of the buck time after time. Why hadn’t I seen him? How could a buck grow invisible in knee-high grass? Maybe he’d been deeper in the swale and had crawled out later when death approached. But why hadn’t we seen him on the second day? The trail to two of our other stands ran close by.

If only I’d gone into the swale on a hunch. If only I’d had a hunch. If only there hadn’t been such thick fog on that day, surely Steve would have seen the deer from his lofty perch. If only the wound had bled so I’d have stayed on the right trail. If only I’d shot truer. If only, if only…

But such musings were no help. I was beside myself with self-loathing. I wanted to throw up. I wanted to cry. Everything I was striving for as a hunter lay in ruin like the buck.

Tim and I returned that afternoon to take the cape and antlers. They were all that we could salvage. Silence returned as I guided the knife, wishing away the green that had begun to color the ruined venison. Forcing myself to speak, I asked Tim to age the deer. He was wildlife manager for the area’s Department of Natural Resources. He estimated 3½ years, give or take a season. That, at least, was a comfort.

I took the antlers as much out of respect for the animal as for myself. I knew the head would be a most worthy reminder.

“Well,” the taxidermist said, “at least you got the horns. You can always buy meat.” He didn’t understand.

Now the head hangs in a place of honor in my home. Beautiful as it is, I get no joy out of looking at it. It remains, however, a lasting monument to that day. I still think of the rotting venison and the slow death the buck may have had. I learned something then, something of deer hunting and of myself.

Many evenings, after the head was first hung, I would sit looking at it and recall that day. I had decided that my gun was to blame for the poor shooting, that the barrel wasn’t tightened down properly on the stock. I knew, too, that the deer had been lost because of poor tracking skills, fog, bad luck, all of the classic reasons. But I was only half right at best.

Finally I came to understand that the tale was told in those first moments when the buck raised his crown and I began to doubt myself. I forgot my skills then, thinking only of the glory or the humiliation that I faced. The strain of the moment, my self doubt and consequent loss of concentration cost me that buck. I was not a competent enough marksman to rise to the occasion, under pressure. Only more practice could be the cure. 

I haven’t faced a buck since then, although I killed a doe cleanly the following season just 100 yards from the spot where I’d seen that buck. Somehow the pressure had faded then, but does don’t carry antlers. I wonder about that day to come when I’ll face a buck once more. And I wonder if the lesson that began on a frosty morning not long ago will serve me then.

Amazon sends chills through its workforce with mixed message memo about AI use

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There's a ridiculously simple test you can use to figure out whether AI mandates at a given company are well-considered, or are being shoved out the door by out-of-touch buffoons: What happens when an engineer documents that AI made a task worse? Are they taken seriously, or are they marginalized and eventually managed out?

Conveniently, this week Amazon published a treatise by Terk Terkowitz, a VP/Distinguished Engineer/Final Boss of Their Independent Contributor Technical Career Track, on the role of the developer in the age of AI. It was originally an internal missive sent to their software engineers and their managers back in September, which is an interesting lag for a document that sounds this confident. It also maps exceedingly well to what other organizations are struggling with.

I think it's a good document, but I can't help but view it with a somewhat cynical eye. I see it as an attempt to make the future of the profession of software engineering feel less bleak. And it's feeling mighty bleak for a lot of folks right now.

What's the context?

This treatise comes at a time of dramatic upheaval. Tech as an industry is no stranger to change (usually at 3AM, heralded by the pager), but something about the current AI-ification of the world strikes many of us as fundamentally dissimilar from previous revolutions. Folks are scared about their career prospects. "Should I give up tech and get into farming?" is a sincere question multiple people have asked me. I don't know! Tech is already a goat rodeo, so you've got a leg up if you do?

This anxiety isn't theoretical. In May, Amazon wisely shut down KiroRank, an employee-built cash incineration leaderboard that ranked people by token usage, after the obvious thing happened: people spun up junk tasks to climb the ranks. This is now called "tokenmaxxing," and the Terkowitz treatise specifically calls it out as being at odds with "frugality." Meta did the same thing with "Claudenomics" and 85K employees that same week. And Amazon reportedly announced another 250 job cuts this week. I can't tell you that these things are related. But I can tell you that every engineer who read the memo assumes that they are.

What's the thing say?

This document contains a lot of great stuff. I have a tremendous level of respect, both personal and professional, for Amazon's Principal Engineering community; I'm led to understand that that respect may not be reciprocated, but that's okay. I am, by their standards, a lapsed engineer who ran away to join the circus as one of its lesser clowns.

Terkowitz's memo emphasizes customer outcomes and engineering judgement, two things I would absolutely not delegate to AI any time soon. (Note: I am writing this in October of 2026, with the full understanding that previous comments on the capabilities where AI falls short tend to age like fine milk.) It documents work that (today) AI tools aren't improving. That's way better than assuming that every objection to AI inherently comes from some self-interested Luddite who's scared of being replaced. It also says something smart about measurement: look at the 90th percentile of AI adoption, at those teams burning the most tokens, and check whether their delivery actually went up commensurately. This is great! Shout it from the rooftops; we should all be doing such things.

But still, I wonder.

The memo says “If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing.” Document it, bring it to your manager, work through the problem. I agree with every word of that! The question is whether anyone's promo doc is gonna survive citing it.

Here's what happens next. The memo concedes that input metrics are "deeply flawed," but then in the next paragraph says to go ahead and use them anyway, carefully. It then warns that non-adopters are falling behind. Those are three different positions within three sentences and they're not at all aligned with one another.

That's not muddled; I think it's cover. A manager can cite "a finding, not a failing" when a strong engineer skips the AI tool, and then cite "falling behind" when it's time to PIP someone. Both are congruent with their stated policy; you get to decide which interpretation you want to go with in a given situation.

How will managers handle justified AI non-use? Are promotion and performance decisions being made in a way that rewards these long term outcomes? Or are they instead taking the easy weasel way out, optimizing for what's cheap to measure, and rewarding tool activity? Because if you give me a metric, I will turn it into a target. So will an overwhelming majority of employees. When that happens, everyone loses — it's how KiroRank happened in the first place.

Everyone's selling tokens — including Amazon

I write this after two days of attending GitHub Universe, where they talked about how agents start roughly 40% of PRs now. And yet, Jay Parikh’s keynote argument was that useful output wasn't "more code," but rather completed outcomes: features shipped, bugs fixed, security issues addressed, loudmouth columnists silenced, etc. The problem here is that their entire business model cuts against that; they're billing in a model that charges customers on a consumption basis, not an output basis. There's nothing, today, that ties "AI spend" to "output that was worth the effort."

Which brings us back to Amazon. The memo says tokenmaxxing is at odds with frugality, and for Amazon’s internal cost line, it is. Meanwhile AWS bills Bedrock and Kiro customers by the token. Inside the building, tokenmaxxing is a problem to be stamped out. Outside it, it’s the revenue plan. ®

O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects creates "quiet yet present" garden studio in London

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A low brick base anchors the timber structure of this pared-back garden studio, which London studio O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects created as a workspace for a creative couple in Highbury.

Nestled in a leafy garden of a Victorian home, the studio provides the filmmaker and artist with a calming, well-lit space with a strong visual connection to the outdoors.

O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects slotted it at the end of the garden to preserve views from the house over the mature garden, avoiding "compromising the sense of depth and continuity of the landscape", the studio said.

"It was important that the building felt distinct from the house while sitting comfortably within its setting, a short walk from the rear garden door," studio co-founder Amalia Skoufoglou told Dezeen.

"The intention was to create a building that felt quiet yet present in the garden, rather than competing with it or the Victorian house," she added.

"The form allows the quality of the space to emerge through its proportions, construction and relationship with daylight," Skoufoglou continued. "Its modest scale and subservient position give the studio the character of a garden building."

O'Sullivan Skoufoglou Architects designed the material palette and low profile of the studio to complement the character of the existing house and garden, ensuring minimal visual impact.

Its low brick plinth nods to the brick boundary walls of the Victorian home and also pays homage to Victorian glasshouses, traditionally supported on low brick walls.

Above the brick plinth is a lightweight timber structure, chosen to "allow the upper part of the building to remain visually light", Skoufoglou said.

Inside, the garden studio comprises a main open workspace and an adjoining bathroom. The focal point is its built-in desk that extends along the main east-facing window wall.

"The main window wall and the built-in desk form the focus of the interior. The desk runs alongside the generous east-facing windows, making daylight and the changing garden views an integral part of the working environment," said Skoufoglou.

"This arrangement brings the working surface into direct relationship with natural light and views of the garden."

Inside, the timber structure is left exposed at roof level, while the brick plinth is also left visible. Between, the walls are lined with painted pine.

Smaller windows are positioned strategically around the studio, framing views of trees while maintaining privacy from neighbouring homes.

A new California museum is a beautiful and complicated ode to the Eameses

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When the Eames Institute of Infinite Curiosity released renderings of its new museum last week, it aimed to show how a museum campus could encapsulate the legacy of the 20th century’s most famous design couple, Charles and Ray Eames. The architectural project—helmed by museum design heavyweight Herzog & de Meuron, along with local firm EHDD and landscape architecture firm Field Operations—is meant to be more a celebration of the Eameses’ philosophies and ideas than an Eames theme park. 

“We try to ask as frequently as possible, ‘What can only happen here?’” says John Cary, the institute’s founding president and CEO.

The institute, a nonprofit charity founded in 2019 to advance the legacy of Ray and Charles, joins an existing constellation of entities dedicated to the couple’s work, including the Eames Foundation (established in 2004) and the Eames Office, which oversees the furniture line. The new museum design, an adaptive reuse of an 88.5-acre site in Novato, California, will transform a warehouse and office originally built in 1964. 

The architectural concept is just one example of the Eames Institute seeking to channel the ideals and vision of the couple, including their “best for the most with the least” axiom, and a focus on mid-century aesthetics and the repurposing of industrial materials.

The release of the renderings has also brought up some uncomfortable recent dialogues about how someone in the institute’s orbit has—or has not—reflected what many consider the key values of the Eames name: anti-elitist, democratic design for everyone, and a fostering of international dialogue and multiculturalism.

[Image: Eames Institute]

In particular, the focus has been on Joe Gebbia, the multibillionaire Airbnb founder and early founding donor of the Eames Institute of Infinite Curiosity. Talking about his donation and relationship with the Eameses in a 2022 interview with Fast Company, Gebbia gushed about what the couple stood for and how it influenced him.

“I became enamored with their values, with their ethos: Create the best design for the most people for the least price. Democratize design for the masses,” Gebbia says. “They really left a gift for us, for the world. And the whole notion of starting the Eames Institute was to share that gift with everyone.”

In recent years, Gebbia has worked for the Trump administration as head of the National Design Studio, where his team has built out a suite of digital services for the government, including the prescription drug platform Trump RX and the America.gov AI chatbot. His political views have taken a far-right turn, particularly on social media, where he’s espoused anti-immigrant views, among others, that have alarmed people who would typically consider themselves aligned with the Eameses’ worldview—and which the institute has pointedly avoided addressing, until now.

(Architecture critic Christopher Hawthorne has been dogged about raising the question about the institute’s response to Gebbia’s statements.) Fast Company reached out to Gebbia for comment, and didn’t receive a response in advance of publication.

When asked directly about Gebbia’s role, Cary offers the first on-the-record statement, saying Gebbia is simply a donor and “an incredible supporter from day one.” Gebbia has never held a board seat and never had a staff position. According to Cary, he himself and Llisa Demetrios, chief curator at the Eames Institute (and the Eameses’ granddaughter), “guide 100% of the decision-making and programming.”

[Image: Eames Institute]

Eames philosophies come to life

Demetrios and Cary see that programming evolving as the museum continues to take shape. “We are learning by doing, we are prototyping, we are iterating,” Demetrios says. “That’s the best way to honor my grandparents.”

The values of the Eames, and the work that those values inspired, is central to the museum layout. References to their work are obvious in the proposed museum campus—plywood seating and a landscape scheme that alludes to the famous Powers of Ten film, for example—but the design is more grounded in the intricacies and possibilities of the site. 

The museum is housed on a mid-century modern corporate campus in Marin County with a striking, crown-shaped roof that sits amid a bucolic Bay Area landscape. These new renderings, featuring a welcoming entry court and fountain, a vast central hall, a maker space, and indoor-outdoor recreation space, give a more concrete view of how the institute plans to host programming and exhibit work, including the collections of Eames furniture and other objects. 

The sprawling site was once a warehouse and office space for publisher McGraw Hill, and then later owned by Birkenstock. The design of the original building was a collaboration between John Savage Bolles, who designed the publisher’s St. Louis headquarters, and Polish American architect Jan Lubicz-Nycz. Its wavy, steel-and-concrete roof is a hyperbolic paraboloid that looks like an egg carton with jagged teeth. 

Cary points to research uncovering that one or both of the architects attended a 1959 University of California, Berkeley, lecture by Félix Candela, famed Mexican architect, structural engineer, and pioneer of thin-shell concrete designs, where they drew inspiration. Simon Demeuse, an architect and partner at Herzog & de Meuron, says the architects must have been “very charismatic” to have sold such a striking design for a place to store books.  

Located adjacent to the 101 Freeway among rolling hills and oak trees, the campus is also roughly 15 miles south of the Eames Ranch, a 26-acre working farm founded by the family that now houses much of the Eameses’ archive. Cary calls the former McGraw Hill warehouse this “mythical building” glanced at via the roadway, a site where the institute could do so much more than it did at its existing property. 

Richard Kennedy, a partner at Field Operations, says their vision for the landscape is to celebrate the uniqueness of Marin County while celebrating the Eameses’ idea of exploration, tinkering, and infinite possibilities.

[Image: Eames Institute]

Lead architects Herzog & de Meuron take a few moves from their own museum-design playbook in repurposing the space. A great, grand hall in the middle, with towering 36-foot spans bisected by a reflective stainless steel bridge, create a massive indoor space for displays and projects. Addition by subtraction helps connect the space to the outdoors, including removing the formerly frosted glass and opening up the truck bays and adding windows, and even moving a section of the wall toward the interior to create an outdoor deck, known as the upper court. 

“A lot of these Eames approaches, the mantra of trying to get the most with the least, they ring very familiar to our shop,” Demeuse says. “We definitely found ourselves to be kindred souls with the Eameses’ approach.”

One of the more consequential changes to the space involved removing the existing lighting, cables, and electrical system (they’d previously been strung up above the warehouse workspace) and embedding them in a series of linear tubes that crisscross the undulating roof, creating a series of shapes that animate the interior ceiling. 

There’s also a series of discrete sections for programming, including maker spaces, a 275-seat theater, and a half-dozen small rooms for displaying work. Demeuse compares it to compartmentalizing the space, creating ways for visitors to “cleanse their palate” between exhibits. He expects the space to evolve with the collection in the coming years before the expected opening in 2030.

[Image: Eames Institute]

The exterior, formally a more staid mid-century corporate landscape of berms, loading docks, and roadways meant to accommodate truck traffic, will be turned into a collective of landscapes, playscapes, and custom outdoor seating meant to evoke infinite pathways and places to gather. Field Operations, riffing off Powers of Ten, will subdivide the surroundings into 10 zones reflecting the diversity of nature in surrounding Marin County, including oak savannas, coastal gardens, and a vast array of native plants that will provide variation as they bloom throughout the year. 

Kennedy says the landscaping is about creating a series of exploratory paths through these different, more exuberant landscapes, filled with a collection of rooms, specimen trees, playground, and nooks and crannies—as well as customized, molded plywood benches and seating—nestled within.

“The building is this thing you come for, but then everything else is kind of open for exploration,” he says. “We learned this with the High Line; people come to these places because of the buzz, but they’ll come back because of how it feels. And the vast majority of their photos are going to be the plants.”

[Image: Eames Institute]

“We Control the Way We Do Our Work”

The proposed museum is a striking piece of architecture—one that promises to be an important physical manifestation of the design legends’ legacy. But it’s hard to shake off the baggage of its most well-known donor.

When I asked Cary whether the political views of a funder matter, especially if the views directly contradict what people understand Ray and Charles to stand for, he said: “We can’t police all the political views of our individual donors any more than we could of our staff. I will say that we will always welcome people who believe in our mission, who believe in the transformative power of design. And, you know, I also think that people’s political views evolve over time, and that’s also not something we could track effectively.” 

He notes that the Eames “were able to work across the aisle,” citing a famous photo of Charles with President Richard Nixon, his work on Nixon’s Federal Design Improvement Program, the couple’s work for the U.S. Information Agency, and Ray’s work with President Ronald Reagan on the Head Start program. 

But the ideas that Gebbia has promoted go far beyond traditional bipartisan compromises. When asked if there is any line a donor could cross, Cary said, “We look for people who are interested in supporting our mission, and we can’t control everything that they do. But we control the way that we do our work, the value we manifest, the ways that we address our mission, the programs that we bring into the world. And if there is any apparent disjointment between any donor, any partner, and what we do as an organization, it simply reinforces our independence.” 

The revealing of the renderings comes at a pivotal moment in the growth of the institute. According to Cary, preliminary cost estimates of the project are near completion; he doesn’t yet have a final estimated price tag, but he does believe it will require a significant fundraising effort to create this campus. “We have an array of donors at this point,” he says, “and we’re going to need infinitely more donors in order to realize this crazy vision.”

Otter.ai is moving beyond transcription—and deeper into your workday

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Eight years ago, Otter.ai’s pitch for its transcription app was pretty straightforward: The Mountain View, California-based startup would turn audio into readable text in real time. And it would do it for free. But in 2026, the company is now competing against a chorus of competitors that all leverage AI to provide real-time transcription. Otter’s answer to that challenge amounts to changing the channel—to go beyond transcribing to extracting insights from all that banter.

“As our CEO Sam [Liang] has shared a number of times, a transcription was never the end goal,” says Otter’s new CMO Alex Gay. “The opportunity for us really is about what can you do when you capture all of that conversation across all the contexts that organizations need.”

As in, building on that to make meetings less of a time-suck by going beyond Otter’s existing ability to summarize what people said (a feature many competitors can do) to suggest what you should say next. 

Gay argues that meetings are often inefficient for predictable reasons: Participants arrive unprepared, the right people aren’t in the room, or agreed-upon follow-up tasks never get done. The result is what Gay calls “this loop of having to have more meetings to just improve the productivity of the meeting.”

Otter hopes to break that cycle with three new features introduced Tuesday, all built around what Liang calls “the whole meeting life cycle” during a recent Zoom call.

All of them reward organization-wide use of Otter, which offers a free Basic tier with usage limits as well as three paid tiers: Pro, $99.96 per user per year; Business, $239.88 per user per year; and Enterprise, with pricing based on such factors as the size and scale of a deployment. Otter says it has more than 40 million users but doesn’t break down how many pay.

First, Otter will automatically compile a “Pre-Meeting Brief” based on transcriptions of prior meetings as well as whatever other apps and tools an office connects to Otter via the platform’s array of integrations.

Second, Otter will offer “Live Assist” help during Zoom, Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet calls based on the context of the conversation. Liang describes it as a “sales-call sidekick” that’s “listening to the customer. It has all the background on the customer. It’s trained on thousands of high-level sales calls in the past.” (While other participants with an active Live Assist agent will see an Otter notetaker in the meeting window, its full capabilities are not disclosed.)

Afterward, Otter switches to “Work Mode” to put together a recap and help handle follow-up tasks, including drafting emails, Liang says.

The CEO’s description for the app that he doesn’t want people to think of as a transcription app: a “conversation knowledge engine.”

Otter also plans to expand language support beyond the current limited selection of English, French, German, Spanish, and simplified Chinese while further improving its understanding of accents. (Gay, who speaks with a Scottish brogue, admitted that Otter’s accuracy can drop “when I get particularly Scottish or start to use colloquialisms.”)

And the company plans to step up its marketing for all of these features with a new $320 million investment from General Catalyst. Gay says expanding Otter’s adoption in organizations beyond individual- or team-sized beachheads is high on its to-do list.

“We think we have to demonstrate the impact that we can have across two or three functional areas, and then show how that could be improved with context access across their organization,” he says.

Privacy concerns

AI notetakers on calls haven’t always been welcome, with some security agents advising against using them at all. Otter itself is defending a class action lawsuit filed last year alleging that its software “deceptively and surreptitiously” captured private conversations.

State privacy laws regarding recording conversations vary widely. California, for example, requires the consent of all parties in a conversation, while Virginia only needs the consent of one party.

Gay acknowledged those concerns but maintained that Otter doesn’t repurpose customers’ conversations for other uses. He says the company’s model training is limited to speaker identification, rather than mining the contents of conversations as some other generative AI tools might. (I should note that I used an AI-transcription app for our conversation, but one that works offline: Google’s Recorder app for Pixel phones.)   

Gay also pointed to Otter’s use of encryption both at rest and in transit, arguing that customers can be confident their data remains their own. Still, the CMO acknowledges that Otter has more work to do to satisfy some security-conscious customers. He says the company already serves regulated industries, including finance, but needs to develop additional capabilities for organizations with zero-trust IT policies. “There’s still new capabilities that we need to build.”

What else is listening?

In a world where a smartwatch, smart glasses, or even AI jewelry could always be listening by default, Otter’s app-based approach could seem a little old-fashioned.

Asked about whether Live Assist could be something that runs on a pair of connected glasses, Liang waved off the idea. “It’s an interesting idea, but we’re not doing it right now,” he says, opining that camera-equipped smart glasses are “just too creepy.”

Gay raised a related question about always-on microphones: Do people begin to censor themselves when they know they’re being recorded? But he suspects that discomfort will fade as the technology becomes more commonplace.

“Eventually, it just becomes the norm and then people become relaxed,” Gay says. The larger question, he suggests, is who ultimately benefits from all those recorded conversations.

“Is it being used to the value of you, as the person that’s being recorded? Or do you feel like it’s just your words being taken to benefit someone else?”

NYC will flip the streets from Halloween to the marathon in just a few hours. Here’s how

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

New York City is gearing up for another big weekend. This year, there are just eight hours between the end of the Halloween parade and the start of the marathon. Organizers of the former expect 80,000 participants in costume and 2 million spectators, while the latter will host 60,000 runners and 2.5 million fans on the sidelines cheering them on. The two routes don’t intersect, but the streets will be unusually busy. And preparing them for the crowds and their aftermath will involve an army of hundreds of sanitation trucks and workers.

“I think the great thing about New York City is that there’s something going on every single day of the year,” Gregory Anderson, the Department of Sanitation commissioner, tells Fast Company. “And so while it’s unusual for us to have Halloween on Saturday followed right by marathon Sunday—and particularly with the streak of good weather we’ve had, we expect to have huge crowds at both events—we’re used to doing multiple things at the same time.”

This summer, for example, the July Fourth holiday weekend overlapped with World Cup watch parties, Knicks’ NBA Finals games, and Taylor Swift and Travis Kelce’s wedding, Anderson adds. “So we’re used to it, we’re ready for it, and we’re just excited to be there alongside New Yorkers as they’re celebrating whatever it is they’re celebrating,” he says.

This weekend, the work entails two roles: public safety and cleanup. In fact, the process has already begun. For the past week Sanitation has been working with the city Department of Transportation to sweep the marathon route, which begins in Staten Island, then crosses into Brooklyn, Queens, Manhattan, the Bronx, and back into Manhattan. This process will continue through Saturday night. Right before each event, workers will park salt spreaders filled with sand to make them heavier as well as collection trucks along the routes to serve as blockades—40 for key intersections around the Halloween parade and 158 along the 26.2-mile marathon. “Our trucks, they’re big, they’re heavy, they don’t move,” Anderson says.

New York Department of Sanitation workers clean up after New York City’s 48th Annual Village Halloween Parade on October 31, 2021 in New York City. [Photo: Getty Images]

But after the events is when the agency shifts into high gear. Anderson says “every event has its own unique circumstances”—confetti from ticker-tape parades to the food and beverage litter around the Halloween parade and the water cups, banana peels, and gel packs from the marathon.

For Halloween, once the Police Department clears the streets of costumed (and probably drunk) revelers, 189 sanitation workers, plus 17 supervisors, will swoop in for cleanup. This year, Sanitation expects to begin the work later than usual.

“When we have the parade on a Monday or a Tuesday, sometimes we are able to get in there earlier, sometimes even before midnight to start cleaning up,” says Anderson, the commissioner. “But this year we’re expecting we may not be able to start cleaning until after midnight, maybe even 1:00, 2:00 in the morning because we do want to wait until NYPD has safely moved folks off the parade route so that we can get in there with our equipment. We don’t want people hanging around and having a good time while our heavy equipment is in there.”

Some will be armed with brooms, and others with what are essentially very big leaf blowers. “And then we have our most effective tool, which is our mechanical brooms,” Anderson says, using the technical term for street sweepers. “And they come through, they sweep up all that litter and debris that’s been pushed out into the middle of the street and leave the street looking cleaner than it was before the event started. We have whatever tools we need to clean that all up and we’re prepared for whatever New Yorkers bring us.”

For the marathon, 480 sanitation workers will be on cleaning duty, plus another 370 staffing the blockades. Anderson says the starting line is where a lot of manpower goes to scooping abandoned garments and detritus that accumulates from runners queuing up for hours to begin.

Empty cups are thrown away as runners compete during the 2022 New York City Marathon in the Brooklyn borough of New York on November 6, 2022. [Photo: Getty Images]

“They want to stay bundled up, but then they discard a lot of that clothing as they start the race,” Anderson says. “So we go through, we try to clean that up, and to the extent we can, we work with our donation partners to make sure what can be reused is.” The other challenging areas are the 25 water stations, which generate the most waste. Sanitation tries to complete its work in those sectors within two to three hours after the last runner passes through.

While this weekend will be a big one for the Department of Sanitation, Anderson says that the team is eager to contribute its part to two events that are a source of so much joy for the city.

“The thing that makes it most successful is our people, and they are just so dedicated to the work and so dedicated to serving their fellow New Yorkers,” Anderson says. “It’s not like people see cleaning up after a parade as a chore or a drudgery. It’s really something that sanitation workers look forward to. They look forward to being a part of that event, even if they’re coming through after the fact. And that goes for every single event that we work, whether it’s a small neighborhood block party or something as big as the New York City Marathon.”

Enterprise AI is waiting for its PageRank moment

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let’s try a little thought experiment in technology-fiction: what would have happened if Larry Page and Sergey Brin’s PageRank, back in 1998, had failed to attract its first investors? Google didn’t invent the search engine: there were plenty of them before, from AltaVista, Excite, Lycos or others who were counting words on pages and selling their results page to the highest bidder, to catalog or portal concepts such as Yahoo! or AOL, that attempted to capture the users’ attention as much as they could to be able to serve them advertising, the more the better. Imagine if these concepts would have gone the distance, and deprived us of the web that resulted from Google’s easy and efficient way of searching … 

What did Larry Page and Sergey Brin realize? They adapted and extended the citation index (or its consequence, the citation impact), a system that they knew worked for academic literature (remember, these guys were graduate students working on their Ph.D., and thus reading academic papers like there was no tomorrow!), and moved it into a new context: the web. They realized there was an underused signal, relationships among pages, and used it to generate a metric, weighting links according to the importance of the pages providing them, that not only allowed them to index the web, but also to define attention as a social construct: a page is important when other pages link to it with a certain term. 

What’s today’s underused signal in corporate AI? 

As I’ve said several times, corporate AI is completely different from individual AI: companies don’t need just answers to questions, what they need is persistent memory and state, feedback learning loops, embedded cybersecurity, logs that can be tracked and not manipulated, etc. 

So far, companies are building these required scaffolding using twentieth-century tools such as Python, and AI agents are proving to be smart enough as to escape from these containments and do all sort of bad things — not because they are intrinsically evil (they cannot be), but because the goals and the guardrails are badly defined, or due to excessive permissions, prompt injection, or weak isolation. These companies are trying to define these properties by repeatedly assembling application by application, rather than supplied coherently by the platform. 

However, companies are repeatedly making all sorts of decisions about suppliers, customers, inventory, pricing, service, etc. Those decisions are constantly generating evidence of what works and what not, provided that the company is smart enough to capture them. What if we could evaluate AI by whether the company learns and improves, beyond the quality of individual outputs? What if AI optimization becomes more important than having access to bigger models? 

The competition moves from model capabilities to system performance 

A company does not just need a very powerful AI model: it needs an architecture that coordinates capabilities toward its goals. Researchers writing on Berkeley’s AI research blog explicitly describe the shift toward compound AI systems, where system design can offer better returns than further model scaling. 

Other experiments, such as FrugalGPT or DSPy are specific precedents for making model selection subordinate to results: FrugalGPT demonstrated that model cascades can match the strongest individual model with up to 98% lower cost on its evaluated tasks, and DSPy showed that optimized pipelines using smaller models can compete with approaches using much larger proprietary models. 

What makes the next step consequential? The ability to learn across executions. If we manage to make each interaction inform subsequent decisions across the relevant population, we can achieve a substantial advantage. And world models add precisely this possibility: they can anticipate consequences before acting. Research about Dreamer successfully demonstrates this mechanism across diverse control tasks. And once you have that, you can use reinforcement learning or another optimization mechanism uses experience to progressively improve decisions. 

Accumulated organizational learning becomes the true competitive advantage 

While broadly available models available through commercial APIs or open-weight deployments can provide shared capabilities, company-specific experience can produce truly differentiated decisions, the ones that can really make the difference. Imagine, for instance, the renewal decisions you can make when you can learn from observed retention outcomes. 

However, bear in mind that simple accumulation does not automatically guarantee an advantage: you have to process the information properly, but not “with an AI”… with “your AI”. Make sure you put the training in the correct layer, the one you can administer and control. 

PageRank unlocked value in something the web was already producing: links. The next breakthrough in corporate AI could unlock value in something companies already produce every day: experience. If that experience becomes accumulated learning that the company controls and retains as models change, the competitive advantage starts to belong to the business itself. The company that stopped waiting for a smarter model might simply be the first to realize that its most valuable intelligence is the intelligence it learns to build — and keep. 

My proposed buying criterion is based on the following question: can the company replace its model provider while retaining its operational history, learned policies, objectives and demonstrated gains? Can your company export and reuse that learning when changing platform providers? Do you own your corporate intelligence, or do you rent the whole thing? When you stop working with “vanilla AI” and you start working with “your own AI”, you control the learning asset, without requiring ownership of every underlying model. 

And that’s when you can become a truly optimizable company. Basically, optimization, not a bigger model, is what you need.

Juventus host Napoli at the Allianz Stadium on Sunday with both sides on 14 points and both short of key figures. Spalletti is missing seven players, while Napoli lose their suspended coach and their midfield organiser Lobotka.

Juventus and Napoli go into Sunday’s Serie A Matchday 10 meeting at the Allianz Stadium level on points. Both benches will look different. Napoli will be without their head coach, with assistant Landucci on the bench. Spalletti is still missing seven players.

Allegri was sent off in Napoli’s 3-3 home draw with Roma. Serie A’s sporting judge then punished him for disrespectful remarks to the match officials. The ban covered the trip to Monza on 28 October and now the visit to Juventus. Napoli first announced an appeal. Allegri dropped it, saying he feared the ban could rise to four matches. The suspension is now final.

Napoli also lost Lobotka at Monza. He was stretchered off just after the 15th minute following a challenge with Mangas. The club’s medical update found damage to the tibiofibular syndesmosis in his left ankle, without instability. He also has a low-grade injury to the anterior talofibular ligament. He has started rehabilitation, with Napoli aiming for a return against Fiorentina in the Coppa Italia on 16 December. Badiashile and Neres are also injured.

Seven absentees leave Spalletti short across the squad

The Juventus list is longer. Yildiz is expected back in early December after a foot operation, Sky Sport Italia reported. Thuram, who had knee surgery, is due back in early 2027. Locatelli is out until 2027 after a lateral meniscus operation. Grabara, with a right-knee ACL injury, is effectively out for the season.

Nico González (right soleus), Çelik (left hamstring) and Conceição (shoulder dislocation) are all expected back after the international break. There is one return. Young forward Ekhator is available again.

Juventus want a response from players, not a new coach

Juventus arrive after an uneven run. Lazio won 3-0 away at Juventus on 18 October. Spalletti’s side then won at Lecce on 25 October, with goals from McKennie and Douglas Luiz. Genoa won the next game through first-half goals from Ehizibue and Baldanzi. Koopmeiners hit the crossbar in stoppage time.

After the Genoa defeat, chief executive Carnevali held a long meeting with the squad. He told them to change course straight away, especially in their mentality. Spalletti then announced the squad would stay together from Friday evening until the Napoli game. The club wants the response to come from the players on the pitch rather than from a change of coach. John Elkann still backs Spalletti, and the coach has no intention of giving up, according to Tuttosport.

South African Airways Can’t Pay Staff On Time, But Says It Doesn’t Need Another Bailout

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

South African Airways is delaying salary payments for some employees for the second consecutive month, while insisting it has a viable strategy and will not ask for another government bailout. When an airline has to choose between paying its operating bills and paying its pilots on time, it’s difficult to take those assurances seriously.

South African Airways Delays Salaries As Cash Pressure Mounts

View From The Wing highlights that South African Airways is again confronting questions about whether its recovery is financially sustainable. The immediate problem is payroll. SAA has acknowledged staggering salaries in September and October, prioritizing critical operating expenses while moving some employees to later payment dates.

Employees have purportedly been paid according to the following schedule:

  • Ground staff and cabin crew: October 27
  • Pilots: October 28
  • Management and specialists: October 30
  • Executives: October 31

SAA says “most” employees were scheduled to receive their salaries on the normal payday of October 27, but it appears that 30-40% of employees were paid late.

SAA Says It Will Not Ask For More Money

SAa’s corporate affairs executive Mphilo Dlamini attributed the pressure to rising fuel costs and acknowledged that the airline had insufficient capital after emerging from business rescue. He said financial institutions were interested in supporting SAA and insisted,“We are not going to ask for more money.”

“After the business rescue process, we did not get enough capital injection that is going to keep us afloat, cushion our costs, make sure that we expand the way we wanted to expand. So, there are those issues that I want to admit made us, you know, to be affected in terms of liquidity.

“The board and the management are seized with making sure that we are actually the support that we got from government through the guarantee. We are talking and engaging a number of our financial institutions, and I must say and confirm to you now that they are willing and interested in supporting us because we have a bankable strategy. We are not going to ask for more money.”

That assurance comes after the government approved a R1 billion (about 60 million USD) guarantee in August to support working capital.

A guarantee does not hand case to SAA, but helps the carrier obtain financing by putting the government behind the borrowing. But it still exposes taxpayers if the airline cannot repay.

The Reported Profit Deserves A Closer Look

As noted by One Mile At A Time, SAA’s annual report for the year ended March 31, 2025 shows a R155 million group net profit. The airline company itself reported R30 million. But the group also reported a R317 million loss before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization. Its income statement included R1.169 billion in net disposal and lease-modification gains, helping turn the final result positive.

Subtract that gains line from the reported R173 million pretax profit and the result is a R996 million pretax loss. The report also contains a disclaimer of opinion from the auditor-general, who could not obtain sufficient evidence to express an audit opinion on the financial statements.

Selling valuable assets like Heathrow slots can provide breathing room, but it cannot indefinitely substitute for generating enough cash from the business to cover its bills.

Another Rescue Is Not A Recovery Strategy

The government guarantee comes with conditions, including a February deadline to find buyers and a requirement to identify assets for sale. SAA has also denied a report that its board considered returning to business rescue. Still, the fundamental question remains: what makes this business sustainable once the next financing arrangement is exhausted, especially if fuel prices keep going up?

Whatever the cause, it’s sad that SAA has repeatedly asked employees to accommodate its cash shortages. Staff members have their own payment deadlines…it’s a vicious cycle.

CONCLUSION

South African Airways continues to operate, and delayed salaries don’t necessarily suggest that a shutdown is imminent. But repeated payroll delays, dependence on government-backed borrowing, and troubling financial disclosures leave ample reason for skepticism. I would welcome a genuinely sustainable SAA, but that does not appear to be the current state of affairs.
