2026 was meant to be the Philippines’ year—yet the country remains stuck in an ‘recurring cycle of underachievement’

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When the Philippines took over from Malaysia as chair of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), expectations ran high that the country could use its one-year term to attract investment and catch up with the more successful neighbors driving the region’s growth story. 

With two months left in 2026, those hopes have been dashed. The country has been Southeast Asia’s worst-hit by high energy prices since the U.S.-Iran war began in February. 

“For decades, the Philippines has been described as a country of enormous promise. It has a young population, a large domestic market, a strategic location, and a highly capable workforce,” Ruben Carlo Asuncion, chief economist of the Union Bank of the Philippines, says. “Yet it has often struggled to convert these advantages into sustained gains in investment, productivity, and competitiveness.”

He notes that the start of 2026 brought expectations for strong growth supported by easing inflation, lower interest rates, resilient household spending, and infrastructure investment. “Instead, the economy faced a series of shocks that slowed momentum and reignited inflation pressures,” he says.

What was meant to be a breakout year, highlighted by the Philippines’ turn as ASEAN chair, has instead become a case study in how the country keeps falling short: its structural advantages are outweighed by corruption, institutional dysfunction, and energy insecurity. 

Some of the Philippines’ woes—particularly energy prices—are out of the country’s control, and may ease in 2027. In fact, the World Bank expects a rebound to 5.2% in 2027, on higher public spending and easing inflation.

Even so, Asuncion sees larger challenges ahead. “The more important question is whether the country can finally break free from its recurring cycle of underachievement.”

Middle-income status a ‘milestone, not a destination’

Peter L. U, dean of economics at the University of Asia and the Pacific in Manila, says the university’s early forecasts put GDP growth above 5% this year, similar to the World Bank’s estimates.

However, high oil prices and cuts in public spending—enacted in the wake of a billion-dollar flood control infrastructure scandal last year—have since dragged this forecast down to a meager 3.7%. 

The one bright spot is the World Bank’s reclassification of the Philippines as an upper-middle-income economy this year. Yet Asuncion calls it a “milestone, not a destination.”

“Many countries reach middle-income status. Far fewer successfully make the transition to becoming high-income economies…the challenge for the Philippines is no longer how to achieve growth, but how to sustain and improve the quality of that growth,” Asuncion says.

The Philippines’ sluggishness is a contrast to Vietnam, the other Southeast Asian country to get upper-middle-income status this year. Vietnam is expected to grow 7.8% in 2026, according to forecasts from the Asian Development Bank. Exports are also booming, reaching about $375 billion in the first eight months of the year. The Philippines, by comparison, just exported $64 billion. 

U puts the milestone in more domestic terms.

“It’s like a child who turned 13, yet is still the same person with the same maturity they had yesterday. That child will need to continue working hard to continue learning more, acquiring more skills and virtues to become an adult,” he says. 

One of the biggest obstacles to climbing the income ladder is that investors still struggle to find projects worth backing. Steep electricity costs—some of the highest in Southeast Asia—as well as bureaucracy and corruption, also raise the cost of doing business. 

Exposing decades-old problems

The biggest drag on the Philippine economy, however, may be entirely out of its control. Continued tensions in the Middle East will keep energy prices elevated for the foreseeable future, driving inflation in the Philippines.

“Oil prices had already begun to come down but spiked again on renewed clashes around a month ago,” U says. Inflation is back at its April peak of 7.2%.

The Philippines is still absorbing sharply higher prices of fuel and fertilizer, along with a peso trading at record lows against the dollar. Those pressures feed into prices across the board, says Michael Ricafort, chief economist at the Rizal Commercial Banking Corporation.

Ricafort cautions that inflation could pick up further through second-round and pass-through effects, including higher transport costs, businesses pushing for higher prices, rising inflation expectations, and wages. The increase in Metro Manila’s daily minimum wage from 695 to 755 Philippine pesos ($10.91 to $11.85) in September could also lead to pay hikes in other parts of the country, he adds.

Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, the country’s central bank, has also been hiking interest rates to rein in inflation—moves that will dampen the consumption spending that has traditionally driven the economy. On Oct. 22, the BSP raised its benchmark rate by 50 basis points to 5.5%. And more hikes could be coming: Nomura now expects the Philippine central bank to raise rates by another 75 basis points to 6.25% by February.

Yet Asuncion argues that these external events exposed the Philippines’ existing weaknesses. “Economies inevitably face external shocks. What differentiates successful economies from struggling ones is the strength of their institutions and their ability to respond effectively,” he says. “The energy shock highlighted the country’s dependence on imported fuel, while the flood control controversy reinforced concerns about governance, accountability, and the efficient use of public resources.”

U makes a similar point: the Gulf conflict and last year’s flood-control scandal mirror challenges long familiar to the Philippines.

“The Middle East conflict and the resultant oil price spikes have been recurrent going back to the 1970s. Similarly, corruption scandals in the Philippines seem to also be recurrent,” U says.

Turning the tides

With just a few months left in the year, attention is back on the Philippines to see whether it can turn things around, starting with the ASEAN Summit in November and into 2027. 

“The ASEAN Chairship has opened important economic opportunities, although many of the benefits will take time to materialise and build on initiatives already underway,” Victor Andres Manhit, president of the Manila-based consultancy group Stratbase, says. He adds that key developments include the signing of the ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement expected during the summit.

While the ASEAN Chairship may have provided a platform and opened doors to raise the Philippines’ profile on the investment stage and position it as a key player in the region, “whether the Philippines walks through them depends largely on governance and execution at home,” Asuncion says.

The Philippines does have some structural advantages, despite the grim short-term outlook. “The country’s geostrategic location, abundant natural resources, large and young population, and investment opportunities remain important strengths,” Manhit says. 

Ricafort also sees opportunities in what has been touted as one of the Philippines’ greatest strengths: a young population of more than 115 million, with an average age below 25. “The Philippines has been in a demographic sweet spot since 2015, with the majority of the population already of working age,” he says.

Yet this window is also narrowing fast. U warns that declining birth rates and slipping educational quality, as well as the rise of AI, could affect sectors that rely on young workers, like the business process outsourcing industry. 

And if there’s one insight from the past year, it’s that institutions are still the the foundation of a well-functioning economy. “The lesson from 2026 is straightforward: there is no shortcut around good governance,” Asuncion says.

“That is ultimately the difference between an economy that is perpetually promising and one that finally fulfills its promise.”

HTX Ends UK Activity as FCA Secures Court Undertakings on Crypto Promotions

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

TL;DR: The FCA says HTX is no longer active in the UK following a settlement over financial promotions. The agreement does not constitute an admission of wrongdoing.

The Financial Conduct Authority says HTX has ceased UK activity after a settlement in litigation over cryptoasset promotions. The October 29 announcement describes restrictions on attracting new UK users and accepting deposits, with existing customers directed to the exchange’s official arrangements for residual assets.

The outcome shows why an exchange’s global availability and its ability to serve a particular jurisdiction need to be assessed separately.

The settlement changes the customer relationship

In its announcement, the FCA said it had secured High Court undertakings that HTX would not knowingly promote cryptoasset services unlawfully to UK consumers. New UK customers cannot register, and deposits are no longer available.

The regulator said HTX would not target, solicit or actively seek UK consumers. It also made clear that the settlement does not constitute an admission of liability or wrongdoing by Huobi Global or another person or entity.

That qualification matters. A negotiated restriction and an admission of the regulator’s allegations are different legal outcomes, even when the commercial result is a withdrawal from a market.

The proceedings have a longer history

The FCA’s published proceedings page records a sequence of court documents, beginning with the October 2025 claim and extending through the October 2026 orders. It provides the procedural background behind the latest announcement.

This is relevant because enforcement against an overseas platform is rarely a single action. Identifying defendants, serving proceedings and obtaining effective restrictions can involve several stages.

For other crypto businesses, the operational implication is that jurisdictional controls need to be reflected in more than the wording of a disclaimer. Marketing channels, onboarding systems and service access all form part of the practical customer relationship.

A platform also needs consistent information across those channels. A registration screen that rejects an applicant while a promotion still invites that same customer to join creates avoidable confusion. The practical task is to align the messages, account permissions and support responses so that the legal restriction has an intelligible effect for the people encountering it.

Permission checks need to match the service

The FCA’s warning page for HTX gives the regulator’s view of the firm’s permission status and the limitations on UK consumer protections. A recognizable exchange brand should not be treated as evidence that every service it offers carries the same safeguards in every country.

Even where a business has some form of registration, the scope matters. A permission for one activity does not necessarily cover another, and a registration entry is not a general guarantee of asset recovery.

For consumers, the most useful check is therefore the exact legal entity, the activity being offered and the relevant regulator’s current record, rather than a broad claim that a group is regulated somewhere.

Residual balances are a separate operational issue

Closing a market to new business and handling existing customer assets are distinct tasks. Customers need accurate information about the arrangements applicable to their own accounts, including any service limitations or deadlines.

The FCA directs UK users to HTX’s published information. That is more reliable than assuming that a general headline creates an identical withdrawal procedure for every account. The announcement itself does not provide a universal deadline or promise of instant processing.

The wider significance is a clearer boundary around the UK business. For the exchange, the next measure of an orderly exit is how consistently it implements the restrictions and communicates with affected users. For the industry, the case illustrates that operating across borders does not make local promotion rules irrelevant.

Content Distribution Matters as Much as Content Creation

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The power of content is immense and unparalleled, for it moves people on a primaeval level. But for that to happen, they have to be able to perceive it and appreciate it with their senses. Life on Earth likely began at a microscopic level, with invisible microbes that lived but perceived nothing. It was only when higher forms of life arrived and evolved into the highly evolved human species that could see, hear and speak and fully perceive the wondrous universe they lived in, that literature, arts, science and mathematics flourished, allowing it to live a life that would have been the envy of the gods of the old legends.​

The invention of the printing press was a seminal event in human history, allowing for the dissemination of the printed word and knowledge to millions of hitherto ignorant and illiterate masses. This led to incremental advancements in science and technology, ultimately enabling human beings to literally master the land, seas, skies and space above, ushering in an unprecedented era of growth, progress, health, wealth and above all, enlightenment.

The advent of newspapers, magazines, telephone, telegraph, television, faxes and ultimately internet and smartphones allowed for content to be pushed forward like nothing before it. The arrival of AI on the scene has taken this to another level.

It is to the credit of content creators like writers, academicians, journalists, artists, filmmakers, playwrights, domain experts, consultants and anybody who steps forward to shine a path for others about something that makes a difference to their lives that the world has reached the level of progress and achievement that it has. It would not be an exageration todeduce that the two pillars of human progress that have ensured humankind’s dominance of planet Earth are content creation and content distribution.

​

Content is all about distribution

The most evocative of content will sit idly and do nothing for a brand if it is not effectively distributed. But distributing the content in today's age of digital clutter is far from easy. Consider the fact that you have to contend with 6 new websites, 116 blog posts and 500 minutes of YouTube video content out there every second

You, therefore, need to seriously evaluate your content distribution options to ensure that you use them optimally. These could be your owned media options, like your brand’s website or your newsletter. It would also incorporate your emails.

Alternatively, you can look at shared media options, which include all your social media channels. This is the flavour of the times, as it is a great way of initiating and maintaining a two-way, real-time communication process with your prospects and customers. Then, of course, there is the paid media option where you can promote your brand with the help of advertisements and paid media placements.​

We, in fact, live in an age where paid ads show up in our digital feeds all the time, often at inopportune times. To counter this, PR mavens came up with the idea of native ads and advertorials that read like regular news. Of course, this requires that great care be taken not to claim or imply that this is anything but paid promotional content.

The effectiveness of one’s distribution campaign can be optimised by fashioning one's content in a manner that perfectly suits the channel of distribution chosen for it. The content pertaining to the launch of an electric two-wheeler, for example, could be repurposed in several ways to suit as appropriate copy for a website, a LinkedIn post, an email newsletter or paid ad promotion.

Optimising your content strategy is, to a great extent, all about matching your content with the optimal distribution channel. You can determine that in several ways. This can be done by monitoring your website traffic, keeping an eye on the rate of your social media engagement, and having a fair idea about the conversion rate in terms of downloads, newsletter sign-ups, or any purchases. You should also know what the cost of every conversion made as a result of a media campaign is.

The best way of matching content perfectly with the relevant medium is to get your marketing segmentation right. The timing of your content distribution plays a role as well. Once you have that in order you can use automation tools to efficiently distribute your content to the right channels in a seamlessly efficient manner.

Understanding the channels of distribution lies at the core of making brilliant content perform brilliantly. Digital distribution began with websites, went on to search and landed on social media outreach To this, you can add influencer outreach, which helps a brand get access to a loyal audience that is heavily invested in what the influencer in question endorses. Getting your content to engage furiously there is what will get your brand the traction that it deserves. It helps to track and analyse your content’s performance across different channels so that you can make any required changes and corrections to your distribution pattern.

To conclude, you can liken high-quality content to a raw, uncut diamond lodged deep in the bowels of the earth. Distribution here would involve mining for the diamond, polishing and cutting it, and finally presenting it to the world in all its dazzling brilliance.
