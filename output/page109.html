Ukraine Charges a Russian General in Absentia Over the Missile Strike That Destroyed the Skovoroda Museum

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ukraine’s prosecutor general reported that a Russian lieutenant general has been charged in absentia for ordering a missile strike that caused significant damage to the Hryhoriy Skovoroda National Literary Memorial Museum in the country’s Kharkiv region in the spring of 2022.

As reported by The Kyiv Independent, “According to the Prosecutor General, the Russian lieutenant general who ordered the attack is originally from the village of Chuhuiv in Kharkiv Oblast and commands Russia’s 6th Guards Air and Air Defense Forces Army.”

The prosecutor general wrote, “The (lieutenant) general knew for certain that he was attacking not a military target but a monument of Ukrainian culture. Despite this, he gave the combat order to destroy it.”

The memorial museum dedicated to Skovoroda, an 18th-century philosopher steeped in the Socratic method, was housed in an estate where he lived the final years of his life and died in 1794. The institution was one of many damaged or destroyed during the siege of Ukraine. As ARTnews reported in a roundup of embattled cultural sites in the country, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said, after the attack in 2022, “Last night, the Russian army deployed one of its missiles to destroy the Hryhorii Skovoroda Museum in Kharkiv Region. A missile—to destroy a museum.”

As The Kyiv Independent reported, “Russia has repeatedly targeted Ukraine’s cultural heritage since the start of the full-scale war. As of September 2026, 2,161 cultural heritage sites and 2,665 cultural institutions in Ukraine had been damaged or destroyed, according to data collected by the Culture Ministry.”

Let this sink in: More than 92 percent of Québec is public land. Come winter, those millions of acres of woods, trails, and open glades get absolutely buried in snow, turning the province into a sprawling winter playground where opportunities for ski touring, Nordic skiing, and multiday snowshoeing abound.

All that wild landscape would be nothing without a trail system to match—which is why it’s a darn good thing that groomed trails and skintracks cover the province, courtesy of Québec’s adventure-minded culture and infrastructure. Cozy rustic huts dot the high country, Nordic centers pepper the lowlands, and there’s an extensive network of designated ski-touring areas across the region.

Behind Québec’s extensive network of winter trails and refuges lies a long tradition of winter travel and exploration.

Backcountry skiing in Base de plein air Air-Eau-Bois. (Photo: ©️ Base de plein air Air-Eau-Bois)

Long before winter travel became a recreational pursuit, Indigenous peoples used snowshoes and knowledge passed down through generations to navigate the territory now known as Québec. Today, these snow-covered landscapes offer countless opportunities for exploration, from Nordic skiing and multi-day snowshoeing to backcountry adventures.

Today, Québec boasts 28 national parks and dozens of Nordic and cross-country centers. It also boasts plenty of grassroots backcountry ski areas, each complete with trail maps and designated runs. There’s no avalanche mitigation at these sites—they’re wild at heart—but the effortless parking, signage, and basic infrastructure make them a perfect stepping stone between sidecountry and true backcountry. Always remember to come equipped with the appropriate training, equipment, and avalanche awareness. And if you’d rather explore with a qualified guide, you can find businesses certified through Aventure Écotourisme Québec’s Quality-Safety certification program, which sets recognized standards for risk management, guide training and service quality.

Village-to-village cross country skiing along Les Routes Blanches. (Photo: Laurentides ©️ Gouv.Qc / S. Diotte)

Looking to do a little bit of everything? Try Vallée Bras-du-Nord. This all-season adventure center maintains both groomed cross-country ski trails and designated backcountry terrain. Its varied slopes and jaw-dropping views guarantee an unforgettable outing, while a collection of yurts and cabins make it a true ski-in, ski-out getaway. You won’t find a backcountry experience like it anywhere else in North America.

Enjoy cozy yurts and cabins after a day of skiing or snowshoeing across the province. (Photo: Région de Québec © Gouv.Qc / I. Roberge)

For a more homegrown vibe, head to Sommets du Fjord, a blues-and-blacks zone just beyond the charming village of Petite-Saguenay. The town sits right on the edge of the legendary Saguenay Fjord National Park—yet another ski touring and snowshoeing epicenter—which makes it an ideal multisport base camp.

Want to turn a great day into a great week? Treat yourself to a fully immersive multiday traverse through Québec’s snow-draped winter landscapes. Robust hut systems in national parks like Mont-Tremblant and Monts-Valins make it easy to plan linkups via Nordic ski or snowshoe, and village-to-village trips in the Laurentians like those along Les Routes Blanches offer chances to discover culture and cuisine alongside the stunning landscapes. Simply choose a trail to one or more of the parks’ remote refuges and use them as stopover points to cook, relax, and connect with your adventure buddies—and recharge by a blazing woodstove before hitting the trail again the next day.

Exploring Parc régional de la Chute-à-Bull. (Photo: Parc régional de la Chute-à-Bull ©️ Tourisme Lanaudière / L. Coutu))

The adventure doesn’t end when the skis or snowshoes come off. Beyond the trails, Québec’s welcoming villages, local flavors, and cozy inns offer the perfect setting to unwind and reconnect. Share a well-earned meal, raise a glass of a local wine or brew with your fellow adventurers, and relive the day’s discoveries. Because sometimes, going further isn’t just about the distance you travel, it’s about everything you experience along the way.

Your next winter adventure starts here. Whether you’re seeking powder-filled backcountry descents, multi-day Nordic ski routes or peaceful snowshoe expeditions through snow-covered forests, Québec invites you to explore further. Filing: Chip startup Powerlattice raises $86M to ease AI’s power crunch

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Powerlattice founders, from left: Gang Ren, Peng Zou, and Sujith Dermal. (Powerlattice / Walden Kirsch Photo)

Powerlattice, a Vancouver, Wash.-based startup developing chips that cut the power demands of AI processors, has raised $86 million, according to an SEC filing on Friday.

The new funding is nearly triple the $31 million the company had raised when it emerged from stealth last November with a $25 million Series A led by Playground Global and Celesta Capital. Board members include former Intel CEO Pat Gelsinger, a general partner at Playground, and Steve Fu of Celesta. The filing also lists Wen Hsieh, founding managing partner of Matter Venture Partners, as a director.

The filing shows Powerlattice is seeking $111 million, with 18 investors participating so far. It does not name the investors or the round. The company declined to comment.

AI chips are drawing so much electricity and throwing off so much heat that data center operators are running into limits on available power and cooling.

“AI is not constrained by capital, it’s constrained by power,” Gelsinger said last year when Powerlattice announced its Series A.

Powerlattice makes what it calls a power delivery chiplet, a small component that sits inside the processor package. Conventional setups route current over long paths to the processor, wasting energy along the way. The company says shortening that path can cut energy use by more than half and boost performance. Powerlattice said last year it was providing engineering samples for GPUs, CPUs and custom accelerators.

CEO Peng Zou, Gang Ren and Sujith Dermal founded the company in 2023. The three are semiconductor veterans whose backgrounds span Qualcomm, Intel and NUVIA, a startup Qualcomm acquired in 2021. Powerlattice also has offices in Chandler, Ariz.; San Jose, Calif.; and Hsinchu, Taiwan.

Earlier this week, Powerlattice announced it had demonstrated what it calls the industry’s first monolithic integrated voltage regulator chiplet, which packs the controller, power transistors and inductors onto a single piece of silicon.

In the test, the chiplet powered a device Powerlattice built to mimic the fast-shifting electrical loads of next-generation AI accelerators. The company says multiple chiplets can be linked to work as one, letting chip designers match the setup to their processors.

“Breaking through the AI power wall requires us to rethink how power reaches the processor,” Zou said in the announcement.
