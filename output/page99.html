How Southeast Asia can realize its commercial promise amid geopolitical rivalry

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Keeping Southeast Asia open for business is a geopolitical task as much as a commercial one. Its relationships across geopolitical divides give companies access to competing powers’ markets, capital, and technology. In 2025, ASEAN attracted a record $243.9 billion in foreign direct investment.

But conflict, U.S.-China strategic competition, and economic nationalism threaten these opportunities. Sustaining them requires protecting the security, supplies, and rules underpinning commerce.

Governments must de-escalate tensions and, with businesses, understand and address the political objectives behind economic restrictions, assess whether diversification reduces vulnerabilities or creates new ones, and strengthen critical infrastructure. They have a shared interest in defending international law—even when doing so may create friction with the country violating it.

Conflict and coercion 

The U.S. war against Iran has disrupted oil and gas supplies to Southeast Asia, which relies heavily on the Middle East. Higher energy prices raise business costs, squeeze household budgets, and increase loan-default risks.

The Strait of Hormuz has also demonstrated how a strategic waterway can become an instrument of coercion, even for a militarily weaker power.

Developments there serve as a stark warning about the risks of coercion and disruption in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore. In August, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore reaffirmed their commitment to keeping those waterways, which carry nearly a quarter of global seaborne trade, free, open, and safe. That commitment does not preclude a littoral government restricting passage, an outside power pressuring it to do so, or a state or non-state actor disrupting shipping.

Economic détente between Washington and Beijing could benefit businesses, but if maintaining it diminishes U.S. commitment to regional security, it could weaken deterrence and undermine the stability commerce depends on. In October 2025, China began its first significant island-building in the South China Sea in almost a decade at Antelope Reef, where it is building a runway; the reef is now China’s largest outpost in the disputed Paracel Islands. In September, China announced the opening of a joint military aviation facility in Laos. China has also expanded its presence in the waters east of Taiwan, where Japan and the Philippines have overlapping exclusive economic zones. A shift in the regional balance of power could increase vulnerability to coercion and affect the rules governing trade and investment.

Strategic rivalry and economic nationalism 

Strategic rivalry has brought gains for Southeast Asian countries able to maneuver for advantage: higher U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods prompted manufacturers to shift production to the region. But U.S. licensing requirements for shipments of certain advanced chips to China can delay or prevent sales by regional distributors. Malaysia, amid U.S. pressure, introduced its own controls, including 30-day advance notification for specified advanced chips moving through or out of the country.

U.S. economic nationalism creates a distinct challenge: Washington’s drive to reduce trade deficits and rebuild American manufacturing extends beyond its rivalry with Beijing. Tariffs have applied across Southeast Asia, including to U.S. allies and partners—even Singapore, with which the United States runs goods and services surpluses. Vietnam, which ran a $178.3 billion goods surplus with the U.S. in 2025, has drawn particular scrutiny.

Legal challenges have not ended this pressure. After the U.S. Supreme Court’s February ruling that the emergency law did not authorize Trump’s “Liberation Day” tariffs, the administration turned to temporary tariff powers and Section 301 investigations into alleged excess manufacturing capacity and failures to adopt or enforce bans on imports made with forced labor. The latter led to July tariffs on all 60 economies investigated, including several in Southeast Asia.

Understanding Washington’s objectives and priorities helps determine what concessions might achieve. The administration has justified tariffs and other restrictions by citing reciprocity, reducing deficits, protecting domestic industry, raising revenue, reducing strategic dependence, and denying capabilities to a rival. Trade concessions may not remove restrictions designed to deny China strategic capabilities. Nor will moving production from China to Vietnam satisfy demands for production in the United States. Unclear or conflicting priorities will complicate responses.

Assess dependencies

Securing multiple suppliers may reduce vulnerabilities, but those suppliers may still rely on the same shipping routes or Taiwanese semiconductor foundries.

The region remains acutely exposed to energy supply disruptions. Businesses cannot yet count on regional arrangements to secure supplies. The revised ASEAN Framework Agreement on Petroleum Security has yet to enter into force; even once it does, assistance during critical oil and gas shortages will remain voluntary and subject to commercial terms. The ASEAN Power Grid could diversify electricity supplies, but a regional system remains elusive nearly three decades after ASEAN leaders called for it.

Nuclear power could reduce fossil-fuel dependence for electricity generation. Vietnam has signed an agreement with Russia for its first plant; an Indonesian company has agreed to buy electricity from two Rosatom floating nuclear units. The Philippines has discussed nuclear cooperation with Russia. Russian reactors, however, could create long-term dependence on Moscow for technology, nuclear fuel, spare parts, and technical support.

Energy insecurity has lent urgency to Philippine-Chinese talks on joint oil and gas development in the South China Sea. Yet any deal’s benefits must be weighed against its implications for Philippine maritime rights and potential dependence on Beijing.

Strengthen systems and uphold rules

Resilience can contribute to deterrence by reducing what hostile actors expect to gain from disruption. Governments must work with owners and operators to strengthen critical infrastructure, including subsea cables, ports and data centers. A World Economic Forum report to which I contributed advocates joint vulnerability assessments, standards setting, communication mechanisms, and response planning.

The rules underpinning commerce also need defending: economic rules provide predictability and recourse, while international law protects freedom of the seas and constrains the use of force. Upholding international law may cause friction, but countries should remain steadfast. Not wanting to choose sides should not be an excuse for failing to defend the rules: this is not about alignment with any country. Keeping Southeast Asia’s commercial options open will require taking a stand to defend rules that support peace and prosperity.

Night on a Spooky Planet

Explanation: What spooky planet is this? Planet Earth on a dark and stormy night, of course. The ghostly scene was photographed at Hverir, a geothermally active area found along northeastern Iceland’s volcanic landscape. Triggered by solar activity, geomagnetic storms have unleashed energetic particles trapped in Earth’s magnetic field. Penetrating the planet’s upper atmosphere at high latitudes, the particles create auroral displays in the starry night sky. Ghostly towers of steam and gas are venting from local fumaroles to dance against the eerie, greenish northern lights. For now, auroral activity on planet Earth remains in a high state as the Sun is only in early phases of decline from the peak of its solar activity cycle. And soon, other ghostly apparitions may dance in your neighborhood. Happy Halloween!

In Reply to Senators’ Amicus Briefs, Austria, Albertina, and Leopold Museum Double Down on Unconstitutionality of HEAR Act

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Republic of Austria, the Albertina Museum, and the Leopold Museum Foundation—the latter two both in Vienna—filed a response Friday to amicus briefs submitted by two US senators, in which they repeat their claim that the updated Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery (HEAR) Act violates Article III of the US Constitution and the Tenth Amendment.

Austria, the Albertina, and the Leopold are defendants in a suit brought by Timothy Reif, a descendant of Fritz Grünbaum, a Jewish cabaret performer and collector who was killed in a death camp in 1941. Reif is seeking the return of several works by Egon Schiele that he alleges were looted by the Nazis when they seized Grünbaum’s collection. The defendants dispute the claim, arguing that the works in question are part of the same cache that was exported from Austria by Grünbaum’s sister-in-law in the 1930s and then sold to a Swiss dealer in the 1950s.

Although the defendants had previously argued in several motions that the HEAR Act is unconstitutional, this new filing responds to briefs submitted last month by the Department of Justice and by Senators Richard Blumenthal (Democrat of Connecticut) and John Cornyn (Republican of Texas). Those briefs presented additional legal precedent supporting the constitutionality of the HEAR Act. Friday’s response addresses each of those cases.

Though the defendants “respect the work of the Senators and the Government, and their attention to trying to remedy at least some of the horrors of the Holocaust,” they believe that in passing the amended version of HEAR, “Congress exceeded its constitutional bounds,” the filing states.

Blumenthal and Cornyn did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

At the heart of the response are the 2025 amendments to the HEAR Act, which President Donald Trump signed into law last year. Originally signed in 2016, the act was set to expire in 2026; the amendments extend it indefinitely. Other key changes were meant to address several high-profile cases that had failed in court on technical grounds, including statutes of limitations that vary depending on the state in which a case is brought.

The defendants argue that the HEAR Act stops short of creating a federal law for art restitution, akin to federal copyright law, and in doing so “delegates the substantive means to restitute Nazi-looted art to the states,” the filing reads. Courts are then to rely on applicable state laws, but by eliminating defenses based on technicalities, the 2025 changes dictate how courts should rule on the cases before them.

As a result, the defendants argue, the HEAR Act violates both the Tenth Amendment, which states that any power not explicitly granted to the federal government belongs to the states, and Article III of the Constitution, specifically the separation-of-powers doctrine, which gives courts the authority to decide the cases before them and how to rule on them.

“By forbidding the federal courts from any longer considering these equitable defenses, Congress is intruding on the constitutional right and obligation of Article III courts to ‘decide’ such cases and controversies for themselves,” the defendants’ response reads.

One defense eliminated by the 2025 amendments is laches, under which an otherwise valid claim can be dismissed if the claimant unreasonably delayed bringing it. The change bars courts from conducting “any factfinding as to questions of whether ‘inexcusable delay’ by a plaintiff or the plaintiff’s predecessors caused the loss of critical evidence, resulting in ‘unfair prejudice’ to the current owner of allegedly stolen art,” the response states.

The filing adds, “By outlawing the laches defense in particular, which prevents unprovable allegations of Nazi theft from becoming de facto conclusive presumptions of Nazi theft, the HEAR Act unquestionably does compel particular factfinding in favor of plaintiffs.”

In a phone interview, William L. Charron, an attorney for the defendants, said the purpose of Friday’s filing was to respond directly to the additional arguments for the HEAR Act’s constitutionality raised by the Justice Department and Senators Blumenthal and Cornyn.

“The government and the Senators intervened and involved themselves in this case because they wanted to be heard as to their reasons why they believe the HEAR Act is constitutional, and so this was an opportunity for my clients to respond and explain why they respectfully disagree with those positions and respectfully believe the HEAR Act is unconstitutional,” he told ARTnews.

Charron said he believes Reif v. Republic of Austria can be decided without addressing the constitutionality of the HEAR Act. But if the courts decide they must rule on that question, he said, it is the kind of case that could eventually make its way to the Supreme Court.

“I believe these are important and serious issues,” Charron said of the arguments raised in the filing. “We have given the Court a number of arguments and grounds to dismiss the case without having to rule upon these constitutional questions. But if the Court decides that it does need to address the constitutionality of the HEAR Act, I certainly hope that it seriously considers the arguments that have been raised in this filing.”

WOVEN BAMBOO pairs with CROCHETED TEXTILES IN CHAIR SCULPTURE

 

Designed by Peiyang (Camille) Li, She Blooms in the Dark is a glowing seating sculpture that examines emotional vulnerability and protective spatial thresholds. The project pairs hand-crocheted textile craft with traditional bent-wood furniture techniques, mounting an array of individual blossoms across the open-lattice framework of an oversized, woven bamboo armchair.


SHE BLOOMS IN THE DARK: Day Time View | all images ©Kexin Liu

 

 

UV-REACTIVE YARN BLOOMS STORE DAYLIGHT TO EMIT AMBIENT GLOW

 

The sculptural seat by designer Peiyang (Camille) Li functions simultaneously as a functional chair and a semi-enclosed personal retreat, utilizing a curved, basket-like silhouette to delineate an intimate interior microclimate for solitary reflection. Each floral element is crocheted by hand using phosphorescent, UV-reactive yarn engineered to absorb daylight and emit a soft, low-intensity glow in dark environments. By forgoing high-output electric fixtures in favor of photoluminescent fibers, the textile piece activates after dark, transforming the structural bamboo envelope into an illuminated shelter where soft woven media counterbalance the rigid natural framework.

With spooky season behind us, we’re into fall’s final stretch. Here at Backpacker, we made the most of it: A month ago, our editors got together to backpack a stretch of Wisconsin’s Ice Age Trail, then spent October exploring the forests of the Pacific Northwest, strolling our local trails in Vermont, and even notching a few early-season ski days in Colorado. Here’s the gear that helped us do it

Therm-a-Rest Z-Lite Sol (Photo: Adam Roy)

Therm-a-Rest Z-Lite Sol

$60 at REI

I know: How brave of me to pick what must be the most commonly-used sleeping pad in existence. But some things are classics for a reason, and this folding closed-cell pad is one of them. Consider: It’s unpoppable, a big deal when you regularly go backpacking in the desert like I do; it doesn’t need to be inflated or deflated, which eliminates one task from both your nighttime and morning routines; and it’s light, with a regular running just 14 ounces.

Is it comfortable? Opinions on that vary. I’ll admit that it may not be the supportive option available for side sleepers (or, with its R-value of 2.0, cold sleepers) but I’ve found its egg-crate-style design to be comfier than flat closed-cell models like Walmart’s roll-up Ozark Trail. Anyway, if you’re not tired enough to fall asleep the minute your head hits the ground, you could always just try hiking farther. —Adam Roy, Editor-in-Chief

Ruffwear Palisades Sleep Poncho (Photo: Zoe Gates)

Ruffwear Palisades Sleep Poncho

$100 at Ruffwear

This poncho is the answer to a dilemma I’ve faced since starting to backpack with my dog. I worry about keeping her warm on chilly nights, but a dog-size sleeping bag never seemed like the best solution, and she’s too big to crawl in with me. The Palisades Sleep Poncho from Ruffwear is the first wearable sleeping bag I’ve seen available; it offers more full-coverage warmth than a dog jacket, stays put better than a blanket, all while being less confining than a sleeping bag. Featuring a ripstop shell and packable synthetic insulation, the poncho slips over the dog’s head and drapes over their back so they have the freedom to move around, curl up, roll over or sprawl without compromising coverage.

A cinchable neck baffle keeps in warmth, and soft, adjustable leg loops eliminate slippage (it’s also easy to bundle up the rear of the poncho with a button for potty breaks). On an October night camping in Washington’s Mt. Baker Wilderness, temps dipped below freezing. My dog, who is usually restless in the tent, slept soundly all night curled beneath the Palisades Poncho. Her water dish was frozen solid when we woke up, but she was cozy and content all night long. Paired with a pup-sized foam pad, this poncho makes for a doggy sleep system I can feel confident in. Bonus: It’s extremely cute. —Zoe Gates, Senior Editor

Thrudark Charge Trousers (Photo: Will Brendza)

Thrudark Charge Trousers

$430 at Thrudark

I should have mentioned the price of these hiking pants in the video, but I forgot to. So I’m going to lead with it here: $430 for a single pair of pants is a little outrageous no matter how you spin it. That’s about the same cost as weatherproof snowpants. But the Charge Trousers from Thrudark are exceptionally well made, thoughtfully designed, and built with premium materials (some of which I’ve only ever seen in packs and tents). There are three main things I like about these pants. First, the layout is exceptional. There are six pockets (all on the front), but they don’t look like cargo pants. The ankle cuffs have snap-button adjusters to tighten or loosen them depending on preference and what you’re doing. The hips have Velcro adjusters built in, so you can dial in a perfect fit to your waist, and despite that feature, Thrudark still included belt loops. (Some brands don’t do loops if they have the adjusters, and that drives me nuts.)

My second favorite thing is the materials that these pants are made with. Thrudark used a high-tensile cotton and a Dyneema-Cordura blend that is both highly durable and lightweight. The knees are reinforced with that blend, so you can kneel in a bed of nails and would probably be just fine (at least the pants would be). The rear panel has a four-way stretch material over the butt, which offers a lot of freedom of movement. Third and finally, they’re just comfortable. I can’t say that about every brand’s hiking pants. Where do they fall short? The price, obviously. There’s also the question of how well the cotton elements would hold up if exposed to too much rain (or ungodly perspiration from the wearer). Maybe I’d ask Thrudark for one more pocket on the back, but then again, who on Earth needs seven pockets? —Will Brendza, Associate Editor

Ornot Alpha Wool Pullover (Photo: Benjamin Tepler)

Ornot Alpha Wool Pullover

$198 at Ornot

Polartec, a well-known fabric company that supplies much of the outdoor industry with its fleece, just released two new versions of its popular Alpha fabric that you’ll be seeing in big-name and cottage brands this winter. One of them is Alpha Wool, a fabric that uses a wool base weave with tufts of Alpha polyester insulation on the outside.
