Ancient sled dogs had a surprising physical advantage that helped them pull heavy loads

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Pull a heavy sled and the load tries to tip you over backward. A dog that carries its weight closer to the ground resists that pull better, and Arctic sled dogs appear to have been built that way.

For their size, their lower leg bones run about 6 percent shorter than the same bones in wolves, village dogs and spitz breeds.

The same short legs turn up in dogs from Chukotka, in far eastern Russia, that lived as much as 2,000 years ago.

That matters because sled gear rarely survives in the ground. For most of the history of dog sledding, the dogs are all that remains.

Mitya Kletzerman, an archaeologist at the University of Haifa in Israel, led the study.

His co-authors are affiliated with the Severtsov Institute of Ecology and Evolution in Moscow, Tel Aviv University and the University of Algarve in Portugal.

A body built for pulling

A dog standing still rests about 60 percent of its weight on its front legs and 40 percent on its back legs.

Once it leans into the sled lines, the backward drag of the sled and the forward push of its paws together try to rotate the dog nose-up. The back legs end up bearing more of its weight.

Kletzerman’s team worked out which body shape handles that best.

The team’s calculations suggest that halving the height of a dog’s center of mass could double the pulling force it can generate before tipping backward.

Shifting that center of mass forward by a tenth of the body’s length does the same.

In a short, all-out pull, when the back legs do all the work, a lower center of mass still doubles the force, but the forward shift adds only about 17 percent. So a heavy hauler should carry its weight low.

Sled dogs have shorter legs

The team then measured five long bones in the legs of 80 modern animals.

There were 22 working sled dogs from Greenland, Chukotka and Kamchatka, plus 32 village and stray dogs, 13 spitz-type dogs and 13 wolves.

Sled dogs were the tallest dogs in the sample, standing an estimated 22.6 inches (57.3 cm) at the shoulder, and only the wolves were larger.

So the team scaled every measurement to each animal’s overall size before comparing.

After that adjustment, three bones stood out. The radius and ulna in the forearm, and the tibia in the lower hind leg, were about 6 percent shorter in sled dogs than in the other three groups.

Sex made no difference, and the cold climates the sled dogs came from didn’t explain the pattern either.

Arctic dogs appear to have formed a distinct lineage by about 9,500 years ago.

The oldest evidence of sledding, sled parts beside dog remains on Zhokhov Island in the Russian Arctic, is about 9,000 years old.

Ancient sled dogs fit the pattern

The 82 ancient bones came from three sites on the Chukchi Peninsula, across the Bering Strait from Alaska: Paipelghak, Kaniskak and Ekven.

The sites span the first centuries BC to the 16th century AD and are associated with ancient Arctic communities ancestral to modern Indigenous populations of the region.

The team scored each ancient bone on how likely it was to have come from a sled dog.

All 15 upper arm bones scored as sled dog, along with 20 of the 25 forearm bones and 38 of the 42 shin bones. Altogether, 89 percent of the ancient bones matched the sled dog pattern.

Three dogs that had been buried on purpose scored as sled dogs too.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kletzerman described one of them, found at Paipelghak in 2009, beneath the wooden floor of a passage leading out of a house.

Permafrost had kept its dense brown fur. It was an old male, around ten years of age, and a walrus penis bone and a cut piece of reindeer antler had been placed near its tail.

“We identified possible traces of a harness in the fur of this dog,” he said.

Dog teams let people hunt together and trade ideas between communities, Kletzerman said, and the technology also changed the dogs it depended on.

Work may have shaped the dogs

Nothing in the bones shows how the trait arose. Kletzerman told Earth.com that his team has no good evidence that prehistoric Arctic communities deliberately bred dogs for particular traits.

A less deliberate process may be the better explanation.

People didn’t need to select for cold tolerance, he said. Dogs lived and slept in the snow, and those without the right coats were less likely to survive and breed.

“Once people began relying on dogs for traction, animals physically better suited to this work were more likely to endure it and leave descendants,” he said.

He added that this process of selection was probably not consciously directed by humans, but driven by the working conditions they had created for the dogs.

Kletzerman also emphasized that the ways people managed working dogs likely differed between communities.

“Such practices probably varied between communities and over time, combining accumulated practical experience with culturally specific understandings of animals and the world,” he told Earth.com.

Other routes are possible. Arctic sled dogs share ancestry, so the proportions could have been inherited and then kept because they worked.

Sled dogs also start work before their skeletons finish growing, and hard loading at that age can slow the lengthening of bones. So can a diet rich in vitamin A, which Arctic dogs may have been fed.

Bones reveal clues

Short legs alone don’t make a sled dog. The authors wrote that similar proportions can arise in dog lineages shaped by something other than hauling.

This test thus only works where sledding is already a plausible explanation.

Most of the ancient bones were loose rather than part of a skeleton, so each score is a probability rather than a verdict. And none of the three sites has yet produced clear evidence of sleds alongside the dogs.

How far back the short-legged build goes, and how far across the Arctic it reaches, are open questions.

Kletzerman’s team has an early impression that it was more pronounced in some ancient dogs than in modern sled dogs.

Their next study looks inside the bones, at how shortening may have changed their strength.

If that fuller profile holds up, a single leg bone could tell archaeologists whether a dog pulled sleds, at sites where the sleds themselves rotted away long ago.

The full study was published in the journal iScience.

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →
Sen. Dan Sullivan (R-Alaska). (US Senate)

 

Today, in United States v. Alaska, federal district Judge Sharon Gleason ruled against the Trump administration's lawsuit seeking to block certification of Alaska Senate election results on the grounds that doing so might be necessary to prevent "voter confusion" caused by the presence of two candidates with very similar names on the ballot. As I have explained previously, Dan J. Sullivan can potentially be confused with Dan S. Sullivan (the incumbent GOP senator). Evidence gathered by the New York Times suggests Democrats may have deliberately recruited Dan J. to run, in hopes of siphoning off votes from Dan S. on this topic.

Today's ruling dismisses the Trump lawsuit on the grounds that the United States lacked standing to bring the suit. Most notably, it failed the standing requirement of "redressability." The remedy requested by the administration - blocking certification until the federal government could further "investigate" the issue, would not actually redress the harm of voter confusion, and certainly could not do so while maintaining legally required secrecy of the ballots. In addition, Judge Gleason emphasizes that the federal government's proposed remedy would likely intrude on the powers of the state government,  create more voter "confusion" than it forestalls:

The Supreme Court "has repeatedly emphasized that lower federal courts should ordinarily not alter the election rules on the eve of an election…" "Such late-breaking, court-ordered rule changes can 'result in voter confusion and consequent incentive to remain away from the polls,' and thus undermine the '[c]onfidence in the integrity of our electoral processes . . . essential to the functioning of our participatory democracy.'"

The United States maintains that it is "not seeking to alter the time, place, or manner of Alaska's election" [which the Constitution generally leaves under state control]. And yet it seeks a court order preventing the State from certifying the election if certain circumstances occur; a court order requiring the State to segregate and preserve certain ballots; and a court order permitting the United States to conduct an investigation into the intent of those voters who cast ballots for Daniel J. Sullivan. These requests certainly involve the time, place, and manner of Alaska's election, which are the responsibilities of the State, not the executive branch of the federal government. Clearly, if this Court were to order that certain ballots will be segregated and subject to a future and as yet unspecified investigation by law enforcement, it would likely result in voter "confusion and consequent incentive to remain away from the polls."Such late-breaking changes to the election rules are strongly disfavored.

In addition to these procedural problems with the Trump lawsuit, it also deserves to be rejected on the substantive merits. I covered some of the reasons why here and here. See also the excellent amicus brief filed by prominent election law scholar Rick Hasen, which goes into these issues in greater detail. Quite simply, there is no federal law giving the administration the power to block certification of election results based on "voter confusion."

As explained in one of my earlier posts about this case, Democrats deserve blame for seeking to deceive voters in this way, even though it is highly unlikely it would cause enough confusion to change the outcome of the election (various aspects of the Alaska ballot and election system reduce the already low odds of that happening). But for reasons summarized in the same post, this kind of thing is far from the worst example of exploitation of voter ignorance. Lies and deceptions about public policy issues are far more significant.

Today's decision may be appealed. But I think it highly unlikely that the appellate court (or the US Supreme Court) will reverse it.
