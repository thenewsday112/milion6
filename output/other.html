Ausify Campaign Returns as Australian Music’s Streaming Share Hits New Low

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ausify, the national campaign to lift Australian music’s visibility at home, launched its second year Monday (Nov. 2) against a worsening backdrop: local music now accounts for just 8.5% of all audio streamed in Australia.

That figure comes from APRA AMCOS’s latest Year in Review, released in October, which recorded a 37.5% decline in local content’s streaming share over five years, down from 13.6% in FY21. The rights body’s CEO, Dean Ormston, has warned the share could fall to about 5% within five years without intervention. On user-generated content platforms, Australian music’s share sits lower still, at 3.6%.

A separate analysis published by the Australia Institute last year, using IFPI data, found Australian artists’ share of the 10,000 most-streamed acts in Australia fell from 12% to 8% between 2021 and 2024, with the number of Australian acts on that list dropping from 932 to 773.

The campaign frames the problem as discovery rather than demand. A nationally representative survey of 1,018 Australians, conducted by Pureprofile for Music Australia in October, found 84% say a defining life moment brings an Australian song to mind, and two-thirds could name the song. But only 27% say they have streamed an Australian artist in the past 12 months. Earlier research in Music Australia’s Listening In series found 71% feel pride hearing local music, 62% feel a connection to it and rate it as good as international music, and 66% want more of it.

“These positive attitudes are not translating into listening, sharing, attendance and advocacy,” the campaign said in its launch material, pointing to availability, discoverability and default listener behaviors “often influenced by streaming algorithms and dwarfed by artists from larger English-speaking markets.”

Where last year’s campaign asked listeners to “Ausify” their algorithms, this year’s pitch is visibility: Ausify is calling for “Public Displays of Ausify,” from social posts and tour merch to singing along in public. The month-long run launched with seven Australian artists performing a karaoke version of “Love Is in the Air,” and the artists backing it include Ben Lee, Fanny Lumsden, Astrid Jorgensen, aleksiah, Ayesha Madon, Becca Hatch, Budjerah, Conor Jacob Smith of Midnight Til Morning, Georgia Maq, ISHAN, Missy Higgins, Nooky, Ruel and Vv Pete.

Lee, an ambassador for the campaign, said the goal has shifted. “The Ausify campaign this year isn’t about convincing people that Aussie music is incredible — everyone already knows that,” he said. “This year it’s about being loud and proud about the music we listen to and love.” He added: “I’m calling on everyone to join me in showing their love for Australian music in whatever way they like — tell a friend, get a tattoo, annoy people on the bus by blasting your favourite local track.”

Ausify is run by Music Australia, the contemporary music body within Creative Australia. It launched in 2025 during Ausmusic Month with Thelma Plum, Keli Holiday, G Flip and Ben Lee among its first backers.

The campaign runs alongside APRA AMCOS’s policy push, which includes advocating for a federal live music tax incentive and minimum Australian music requirements across platforms. Australian music continues to perform strongly offshore: APRA’s international revenue topped $100 million for the first time in FY26, with total group international revenue reaching $107.3 million.

Your Compose Screenshot Tests Never Run at fontScale 1.3 — and That's Where Your Layout Breaks

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Spent a while debugging a Compose screenshot test suite that was green on every PR for months, then watched the actual app clip a button label the first time someone bumped their phone's font size to "Larger."

The test suite "passed" the entire time. That's the part worth writing up.

The gap: your snapshots only exist at fontScale 1.0

Most Compose screenshot setups — Paparazzi, Roborazzi, Compose UI's own snapshot tooling — render each @Preview or test composable through one fixed device config. Unless someone deliberately parameterizes it, that config uses fontScale = 1.0f, because that's the default on both the emulator image and DeviceConfig's own defaults.

That's a real gap, not a hypothetical one: Android's accessibility settings let users scale system font size up to 1.3x ("Larger"), and some OEM skins + the newer non-linear font scaling in Android 14+ push effective scale even higher. None of that ever touches a snapshot test that only renders at 1.0.

Here's a layout that looks completely fine in every screenshot:

@Composable fun OrderRow(item: String, price: String) { Row( modifier = Modifier .fillMaxWidth() .padding(horizontal = 16.dp), verticalAlignment = Alignment.CenterVertically ) { Text( text = item, modifier = Modifier.weight(1f), maxLines = 1, overflow = TextOverflow.Ellipsis ) Spacer(Modifier.width(8.dp)) Text( text = price, modifier = Modifier.width(72.dp), // fixed width, "it always fits" maxLines = 1, overflow = TextOverflow.Ellipsis ) } }

At fontScale = 1.0f a price like $1,249.00 fits inside 72dp with room to spare. At fontScale = 1.3f it doesn't, and TextOverflow.Ellipsis quietly truncates it to $1,24… — a price, silently wrong, with no crash, no lint warning, and a fully green test suite, because nothing ever rendered that composable at anything other than the default scale.

Making the gap visible: parameterize the device config, not just the composable

The fix isn't a new testing library, it's actually using the config knob that's already there. Paparazzi's DeviceConfig has a fontScale field — most setups just never touch it:

class OrderRowScreenshotTest { @get:Rule val paparazzi = Paparazzi( deviceConfig = DeviceConfig.PIXEL_5 ) @Test fun orderRow_defaultScale() { paparazzi.snapshot { OrderRow(item = "Wireless Keyboard", price = "$1,249.00") } } @Test fun orderRow_largeFontScale() { val scaledConfig = paparazzi.deviceConfig.copy(fontScale = 1.3f) paparazzi.unsafeUpdateConfig(deviceConfig = scaledConfig) paparazzi.snapshot { OrderRow(item = "Wireless Keyboard", price = "$1,249.00") } } }

Run that second test once and the snapshot shows the clipped price immediately — no manual device testing, no accessibility scanner, just a second rendering pass at a scale real users actually use.

If you're not on Paparazzi, the same idea works with a plain Compose UI test by overriding LocalDensity directly, which is the mechanism fontScale ends up driving anyway:

@Test fun orderRow_largeFontScale_composeTest() { composeTestRule.setContent { val scaledDensity = Density( density = LocalDensity.current.density, fontScale = 1.3f ) CompositionLocalProvider(LocalDensity provides scaledDensity) { OrderRow(item = "Wireless Keyboard", price = "$1,249.00") } } // assert on layout bounds / semantics instead of a snapshot if you're not on Paparazzi/Roborazzi }

What actually to do with this

Don't try to snapshot every screen at every scale — that's a combinatorial mess and most of your screens don't have the fixed-width-next-to-dynamic-text shape that breaks. Instead:

  • Audit for the specific pattern: any Row/Box pairing a weight()-based text with a fixed-width sibling (price columns, trailing badges, icon+label chips) is the shape that breaks first.
  • Add one fontScale = 1.3f variant per screen that has that pattern, not per composable. You're testing "does this layout survive," not re-testing visual design at every scale.
  • If you're on Paparazzi, deviceConfig.copy(fontScale = ...) plus unsafeUpdateConfig is enough — no new dependency.
  • Treat a passing snapshot suite as proof your layout works at the scales you actually tested, not proof it works for users who turn on accessibility text scaling. Those are different claims, and only one of them is usually true.

The fuller version of this — plus three other classes of screenshot-test blind spots (RTL layout direction, dark theme state capture, and dynamic color token drift) — is in the Modern Android Screenshot Testing Playbook if you want the rest written up together.

Testing gRPC Services: A Client GUI vs a Contract-Driven Flow

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Quick answer: A GUI gRPC client is the right tool for exploring a service and sending a request by hand, but it is not a regression strategy: the saved calls are manual, tied to a running server, and do not assert behavior against the proto contract. A contract-driven flow compiles the protobuf definitions, generates typed stubs, and tests the service against fixed request and response messages including error statuses, so a changed field type or a renamed rpc fails the build rather than a manual smoke test. The GUI stays valuable for debugging and ad hoc calls, while the contract suite carries the repeatable coverage. If your only gRPC testing is a folder of requests clicked by hand before a release, a breaking change to the proto can ship because nothing in CI actually validates the service against the current contract.

gRPC and protobuf bring strong typing to the wire, but that type safety only helps if the tests exercise it. Developers often start with a desktop client because the workflow is pleasant: load the proto, pick an rpc, fill the JSON representation of a message, and see the response. That proves the service answers a particular call and is genuinely useful for local debugging. What it does not do is run automatically, cover the error statuses, or verify that a change to the proto still matches every consumer. Because protobuf evolves, fields are deprecated and rpc signatures change, and the contract is the artifact that records those rules. Anchoring the automated tests to the proto means the generated types and the service are checked together rather than relying on a person to remember which saved calls to click.

Manual client versus contract-driven tests

The two approaches answer different questions about the same service.

Dimension GUI gRPC client Contract-driven flow Primary use Exploration, debugging Automated regression Type safety From loaded proto Compiled stubs Error statuses Rarely exercised Explicitly asserted Proto change Manual recheck Compile/test failure CI readiness Low High

The proto change row is the key safety mechanism: a field type change that breaks a caller surfaces as a compile error in the generated stubs before any test logic runs, which is far earlier than a manual client would notice.

Generate stubs and test against fixed messages

Compile the proto into typed stubs in the build, then call the service with fixed request messages and assert the exact response and status codes, including the failure cases.

Running the service in-process or against a disposable test instance keeps the tests deterministic and fast, so they do not depend on a shared environment whose state can change between runs.

Cover status codes and streaming behavior

gRPC express failures through status codes rather than HTTP-style error bodies, so the contract suite should assert the specific codes, such as NotFound or InvalidArgument, and test streaming rpcs by driving the stream through multiple messages and asserting completion.

Because the proto is a versioned contract, keep it in the repository and generate stubs from it on every build, rather than relying on pre-generated code that can drift from the source. The contract should document backward-compatibility rules, such as reserving removed field numbers, so the tests and the evolution policy reinforce each other.

One more practical detail is test data. Build request messages from small, named fixtures rather than large ad hoc objects, so a change to a default value is made in one place and the intent of each case stays readable. When a test needs a nearly valid message with one field deliberately wrong, start from the valid fixture and mutate that field, which both documents the exact rule under test and keeps the message aligned with the proto as unrelated fields evolve.

The practical rule is to keep the GUI client for exploration and local debugging while putting the automated coverage in a contract-driven suite built from the proto. Compiled stubs catch signature and type changes at compile time, and explicit assertions cover status codes and streaming that manual clicks never reach. A folder of saved calls is a useful debugging aid and a weak safety net; with the proto as the source of truth in CI, a breaking change fails the build instead of reaching a consumer.

Frequently asked questions

Is a gRPC GUI client enough for testing?

It is enough for exploration and ad hoc debugging, not regression. It does not run in CI or cover error statuses and proto changes automatically.

How do contract tests catch a proto change?

The stubs are generated from the current proto at build time, so a changed field type or renamed rpc breaks compilation before the test logic even runs.

How are gRPC errors asserted?

Through status codes rather than error bodies. The test checks the exact code, such as NotFound or InvalidArgument, returned by the call.

What about streaming rpcs?

Drive the stream through a sequence of messages, assert each one, and verify the stream ends with EOF, covering behavior a single manual request cannot.

Should generated stubs be committed?

Prefer generating them from the versioned proto on every build so they cannot drift from the contract, and reserve removed field numbers to keep evolution backward compatible.

MCP Server Observability: Self-Hosted Logs vs a Registry Black Box

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Quick answer: When an MCP server runs through a hosted registry, the operator's view of what the server actually did is limited to whatever logs and metrics the registry chooses to expose, which makes diagnosing a slow tool or a bad response a black-box exercise. A self-hosted server keeps the request, tool invocation, latency, and error logs in your own infrastructure, where they can be correlated with the rest of the system and retained under your own policy. The trade-off is that self-hosting means you run and monitor the process yourself, while the registry gives you easier distribution at the cost of observability and control. If you depend on a registry for a production MCP server and have no first-party logs, the first serious incident becomes a debugging session based on screenshots and vendor status pages rather than the exact request that failed.

Observability is often treated as a later concern when teams first adopt MCP, because the happy path works and the registry makes deployment feel frictionless. In production, though, the questions are inevitable: which tool was called, with what arguments, how long did the upstream take, and was the error generated by the MCP layer or the service behind it? A hosted registry intermediates the transport and may surface aggregate usage or limited logs, but it does not give you the raw, queryable trace of every invocation, and it cannot be joined against your application's request IDs. A self-hosted server emits those logs directly into the sinks you already operate, so an MCP failure is diagnosed with the same tools and correlation model as any other service.

What each deployment lets you see

The observability gap is best understood signal by signal rather than as a generic "less control" claim.

Signal Hosted registry Self-hosted server Raw invocation logs Limited or aggregated Full, queryable Latency per tool Platform metrics only Custom histograms Error stack traces Rarely exposed Captured directly Correlation IDs Not joinable Linked to app traces Retention policy Vendor-defined Your own policy

The correlation row is the practical difference during an incident. When the MCP server logs the same request identifier as the calling application, a single trace follows the action end to end; without it, you reconstruct the timeline from separate, unlinkable systems.

Emit structured logs from the server

A self-hosted server should log each tool call as a structured event with the tool name, a correlation id, timing, and the outcome, while avoiding secret values in the payload. This makes the logs safe to ship and easy to query.

Scrubbing arguments before logging is a step that is easy to skip and expensive to regret, because tool inputs can contain tokens or personal data. Logging the structure and the outcome while redacting sensitive fields keeps the observability useful without turning the log store into a credential leak.

Add metrics and tie them to alerts

Beyond logs, expose per-tool latency and error counters so a degraded tool shows up as a metric before users report it, and wire those into the same alerting system as the rest of the platform. Even if you use a registry for discovery or distribution, you can run a self-hosted instance for production traffic and keep the registry entry pointing at it, so you retain the distribution benefit without surrendering the logs.

The contract for the server should document which events and metrics it emits, so the observability surface is a defined interface rather than an ad hoc set of print statements, and a new operator knows what to expect from the logs.

The practical rule is to assume a production MCP server will fail and decide in advance where the evidence of that failure lives. A hosted registry is convenient for distribution but treats detailed invocation data as a platform-controlled, often aggregated view; self-hosting puts raw logs, per-tool latency, and correlation in your own sinks at the cost of running the process. Keep secrets out of the logs, add metrics with alerts, and if you use a registry, point it at a self-hosted instance rather than letting the registry be the only record of what your server did.

Frequently asked questions

What is wrong with relying on a registry's logs?

They are typically aggregated or limited and cannot be joined to your application's correlation IDs, so you lack the raw per-invocation trace needed to diagnose a specific failed tool call.

Does self-hosting mean giving up registry distribution?

No. You can keep a registry entry for discovery while running a self-hosted instance that serves production traffic, retaining the distribution benefit and the first-party logs.

What should an MCP invocation log include?

The tool name, request id, duration, and outcome, plus redacted arguments. Avoid raw secrets and personal data in tool inputs, and capture full error detail in your own sink.

Why use metrics if you already have logs?

Metrics reveal latency and error trends and drive alerts before users notice degradation, while logs answer the detailed why for a specific invocation.

Who controls log retention in a self-hosted setup?

You do. The retention and storage policy follows your own infrastructure and compliance rules rather than a vendor's platform defaults.

10 Amazing Thrillers That Deserve More Credit Than They Get

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Brownie Batter Bark

📅 Nov 02, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ingredients

  • 1-2/3 cups almond flour
  • 1/3 cup baking cocoa
  • 3 tablespoons packed brown sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup coconut oil, melted and slightly cooled
  • 1-1/2 teaspoons vanilla extract
  • 1-1/2 cups miniature semisweet chocolate chips, divided
Text Ingredients

Directions

  1. Line a 15x10x1-in. baking sheet with parchment paper.
  2. In a large bowl, combine almond flour, cocoa powder, brown sugar and salt. Stir in melted coconut oil and vanilla until smooth. Spread onto the prepared baking sheet in an even layer. Freeze 20 minutes.
  3. Meanwhile, in a small microwave-safe bowl, microwave 1 cup chocolate chips in 30-second increments until melted, stirring in between. Spread over the frozen bark. Sprinkle with the remaining 1/2 cup chocolate chips.
  4. Freeze until firm, about 1 hour. Break into bark pieces.

Nutrition Facts

1 serving: 588 calories, 45g fat (25g saturated fat), 0 cholesterol, 203mg sodium, 44g carbohydrate (33g sugars, 8g fiber), 9g protein.
