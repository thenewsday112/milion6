Landline phones will switch off across Britland in 100 days, no extensions – Openreach

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Openreach has issued a stark reminder to customers that the legacy phone network is being officially switched off on January 31, and says the deadline will not be postponed.

The infrastructure arm of BT Group, Britain’s biggest telecoms biz, circulated the warning this week in a letter to comms providers that use its network to deliver their services.

It informs them that any customers still on legacy public switched telephone network (PSTN) connections are at risk of disruption when these are retired at the end of January, 2027.

The switch-off could affect businesses and consumers alike. Beyond the plain old telephone, systems that rely on PSTN connectivity include point-of-sale terminals, fire and security alarms, and personal care alarms for elderly and vulnerable people.

The move is part of a wider government scheme to get the UK onto all-digital high-speed networks running on fiber-optic infrastructure, and the legacy phone service – with its dependency on copper cabling – was standing in the way.

Many households have now ditched landline handsets and instead use only mobile phones, yet for those who still want a landline, the changeover means plugging the phone into the back of a broadband router to access a Digital Voice service.

Openreach has been planning this move for the best part of a decade. It has already pushed back the deadline at least once, after the government asked telecoms providers not to force vulnerable customers who use phone-network systems such as TeleCare to switch until fixes are in place.

Nevertheless, public awareness seems low that analog phone services will soon be disconnected. "Openreach and all providers need to coordinate a much more cohesive campaign that reaches customers directly," said telecoms analyst Paolo Pescatore at PP Foresight.

"TV, billboards and newspaper advertising should reinforce a consistent message, supported by targeted letters, calls and door-to-door support for older and vulnerable people who need extra help," he told The Register.

"Customers need to understand what is changing, what action to take, and who will help them. Providers must take responsibility for ensuring people remain safely connected, especially those who rely on their landline or a care alarm."

Caroline Abrahams, charity director at Age UK, agreed.

"With only three months to go, the digital landline switchover is entering a critical final phase. Most people have moved over without major problems, but nobody who relies on their landline or TeleCare should be left behind," she told us.

"Anyone with concerns should contact their provider, but the responsibility cannot rest with customers alone. Providers must not give up on people who have not responded. They need to keep trying to reach them and offer practical, individual support, recognizing that some may be unwell, living with dementia, worried about scams or without anyone nearby to help."

Openreach has a webpage with information regarding the switch-off. This advises consumers to call their service provider as soon as possible to ensure service is not interrupted.

The company previously told The Register that customers without a fiber connection will likely be switched to SOTAP for Analog (Single Order Transitional Access Product), where equipment is installed in the local telephone exchange to allow them to continue to use their old phone line as before.

Openreach has warned that commercial customers delaying migration risk significant disruption and unnecessary costs - leaving it until the last minute could make it harder to secure the requisite engineering support before the deadline hits.

"Businesses and organizations that delay action are making things harder for themselves," said James Lilley, Openreach Director of All-IP.

"The straightforward migrations have largely been completed. What's left are the complex connections that underpin critical operations, requiring careful planning, supplier coordination, and thorough testing. We are urging businesses to act now, audit their remaining analog services, and work with their communications provider on a migration plan today."

Earlier this year, the firm tried to drive home the message by announcing it was stepping up its charges to service providers and companies still operating legacy business lines, who will by now be paying double the rental cost compared to last year. ®

Quick Takes With: Kate Berry

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Kate Berry is a creative powerhouse. A floral designer in her past life, she can whip up a gorgeous centerpiece with utter ease. During her tenure as editor-at-large for Martha Stewart Living, she mastered both the glue gun and the piping bag. Later, as the chief creative officer at Domino, she learned exactly what goes into making an artful home. Now the U.S. editor-in-chief of Cabana Magazine, Kate can add one more skill to her resume: wallpaper designer.

The multi-talent recently launched her “Garden in the Sky” collection of wallpapers for Graham & Brown. It’s inspired by her lush 16th-floor terrace garden in the heart of Midtown Manhattan. “The launch has been very satisfying for me. Three wallpaper designs with Graham & Brown, including a climbing branches pattern and an espalier motif, both pulling directly from my terrace garden. It’s been really rewarding to translate something so personal —my own outdoor space— into something people can bring into their homes,” she says.

Below, the plant lover shares one formative memory involving an apricot tree, one tragedy involving a fig tree, one lesson learned from Martha Stewart, and other gardening stories.

Above: One end of Kate’s terrace has comfy seating. The other end is dominated by vegetable beds. And all around are potted plants and trees. Photograph via her @nygardeninthesky Instagram account.

Your first garden memory:

We moved a lot when I was growing up, but my mom always planted Vietnamese herbs wherever we lived. I would watch her tend to those little garden beds as if she were a farmer working her fields back in Vietnam. One house, in particular, has stayed with me. We had peach, plum, cherry, and apricot trees, and I was especially drawn to the apricot tree with its beautiful umbrella-like canopy. As a suburban kid with long summers (and no screens), I’d spend hours lying beneath its branches, eating apricots from the tree. They remain the most delicious apricots I’ve ever tasted. Those memories are so deeply embedded in my mind that I can still recall every detail: the warm golden skins dusted with coral-red freckles, their perfect round heart shapes, and their incredible fragrance. It’s one of my happiest childhood memories.

Instagram account that inspires you:

My gardener and designer friends @francespalmer @jduncancampbell. [Read Frances Palmer’s Quick Takes here.]

Describe in three words your garden aesthetic:

Untamed, bountiful and lush—which is really the same instinct I brought to designing “Garden in the Sky,” my wallpaper collection with Graham & Brown. I wanted that same lived-in, slightly wild feeling, just translated onto a wall. The collection is named after my terrace garden, which has always felt like my own little slice of nature above the city.

Plant that makes you swoon:

Oh so many but for fragrance, I’d have to say Jasminum sambac.

Plant that makes you want to run the other way:

I mean, I don’t hate any plants but my least favorites would be peace lilies and pothos. They make me think of bad office plants.

Favorite go-to plant:

Begonia.

Hardest gardening lesson you’ve learned:

I didn’t wrap my fig trees properly and they froze to death this past winter. I feel terrible about it.

Gardening or design trend that needs to go:

To each their own. Are there any bad trends?

Old wives’ tale gardening trick that actually works:

Above: A summer morning harvest from Kate’s garden. Photograph via @nygardeninthesky.

Martha Stewart has a bunch of blueberry bushes that produce these giant and tasty blueberries. I’m convinced it’s because of the coffee grounds she feeds them.

Tool you can’t live without:

Swiss Army pruning knife.

Go-to gardening outfit:

My pajamas or whatever I wake up in.

Favorite nursery, plant shop, or seed company:

Surry Gardens in Maine and Logee’s in CT.

Favorite way to bring the outdoors in:

Above: Photograph courtesy of Graham & Brown.

I have plants and trees and style them in clusters at different heights within my apartment to mimic the outdoors. I will add a touch of the garden to meals we have indoors, too—a sprig of lemon verbena or mint in a glass water bottle, or herbs as a centerpiece. And if you’re into wallpaper, that’s the ultimate way to bring the outdoors in through the walls. Its actually what led me to design my wallpaper collection.

On your wishlist:

A greenhouse.

Not-to-be-missed public garden/park/botanical garden:

Lotusland in Montecito.

The REAL reason you garden:

Above: Kate with her daughter. Photograph via @nygardeninthesky.

I love witnessing the transformation from flower to fruit. It’s incredibly satisfying to watch this process and then be able to pick that fruit. I just love it.

Thanks so much, Kate! (You can follow her on Instagram @nygardeninthesky and @kateberryberry.)

Americans forced to change clocks again as Senate stalls Trump-backed daylight saving time bill

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Daylight saving time has dodged the wrath of President Donald Trump — for now.

As Americans again participate in the twice-yearly ritual of fiddling with their clocks — this time rolling back an hour on Sunday morning — Congress has fallen short of tackling the issue in time.

Earlier this year, the House passed legislation that would allow states to voluntarily observe daylight saving time year-round. But progress on the legislation has ground to a halt in the Senate, even though it’s a popular issue with voters and Trump.

BATTLE OVER DAYLIGHT SAVING TIME ERUPTS AS PUSH TO STOP CHANGING CLOCKS HITS SENATE WALL

But it’s not for lack of trying. Trump has routinely pressured the upper chamber to pass the bill and re-upped that call earlier this week in the midst of traveling across the country to rally in key states and safe red bastions to urge voters to keep Republicans in power.

"The United States Senate is letting the daylight saving bill die when a vast Majority of the Country want it," Trump said on Truth Social. "Most people are demanding light in the evening, but ALL want not to change their clocks anymore. TAKE A VOTE!"

Trump wants the Senate to pass, or at least take a vote on, the Sunshine Protection Act, but it’s facing heavy headwinds from a broad swathe of lawmakers in the upper chamber on both sides of the aisle.

TRUMP FACES REPUBLICAN REBELLION OVER DAYLIGHT SAVING TIME AS SENATE BILL NEARS DEAD END

His pressure campaign got to the point that he shared the cellphone number of one of his top allies, Sen. Tom Cotton, R-Ark., one of the more vocal opponents of the bill, urging supporters to call the lawmaker and demand he work on passing the bill.

"No one likes changing the clock, me included, but Congress has enacted permanent Daylight Saving Time multiple times before and repealed it each time once Americans experienced absurdly late sunrises in the winter," Cotton said in response. "Arkansans don’t want their kids going to school in the pitch dark, and I’ll always put Arkansas first."

The House of Representatives passed the Sunshine Protection Act along bipartisan lines in July. Majorities of Democrats and Republicans voted in favor of the bill, which passed 307 to 117. Just 22 Republicans and 95 Democrats opposed it.

But it’s one of several pieces of legislation that House lawmakers complain has fallen by the wayside in the upper chamber. House Majority Leader Steve Scalise, R-La., pressed the Senate to take up that bill in comments to Fox News Digital in early October.

TIME ENOUGH AT LAST: WHAT'S NEXT AFTER THE HOUSE PASSES BILL TO DO AWAY WITH DAYLIGHT SAVING TIME?

Scalise told Fox News Digital, "There's no reason Americans should lose an hour of sunlight at the end of the day when more daylight means more time with our families, more activity for our businesses and safer communities."

"President Trump has been pushing to make daylight saving time permanent, and House Republicans delivered by overwhelmingly passing the Sunshine Protection Act. It's a commonsense change and the Senate should take it up," Scalise said.

And when the Senate returns after the election, lawmakers will have a densely packed schedule crammed with leftover legislation. With floor time being the top commodity in the upper chamber, squeezing the Sunshine Protection Act onto the floor may be a challenge, even without the resistance it already faces.

"The president feels strongly on it like he does a lot of issues," Senate Majority Leader John Thune, R-S.D., told reporters earlier this month. "It’s something we’re having conversations about, but there’s no clear path forward or consensus on it yet."

In two days, Americans voting in the midterm elections will decide control of the U.S. House and Senate for the next two years.

Republicans are defending their slim congressional majorities, but President Donald Trump predicts the GOP will hold the House and Senate.

"If you elect a Republican House and a Republican Senate on Nov. 3, we will continue to put America first every single day," the president pledged on the campaign trail last week. And he claimed if the Democrats take control, "this country will go to hell."

Republicans are fighting political history, as the party in power in Washington has traditionally lost seats during midterm election years. The GOP also faces a challenging political climate fueled by persistent inflation tied in part to what polls show is an unpopular war with Iran and Trump's increasingly negative approval ratings.

Also up for grabs in this year's midterm elections — governorships in 36 of the 50 states (as well as the territories of Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands and the U.S. Virgin Islands). Plus, all but four states are holding legislative elections. And voters will also weigh in on 145 statewide ballot measures in states from coast to coast.

Here's a closer look at what's up for grabs on Election Day 2026.

Republicans currently control the chamber 53-47, with the final Fox News Power Rankings ahead of Election Day rating the race for Senate control a toss-up.

Both parties are three seats short of a majority, according to the forecast. Democrats are favored in 48 seats and Republicans are ahead in 47. The GOP needs to hold 50 seats to retain control of the chamber, since Republican Vice President JD Vance is the tie-breaking vote in the Senate.

WHO'S UP AND DOWN IN THE FINAL FOX NEWS MIDTERM ELECTION POLLS

If Democrats ride a blue wave to victory by winning most or all of the competitive races, they could potentially come away from the midterms with up to 54 seats. But if Republicans have a good night and defy expectations, the GOP could hold the majority with up to 52 seats, according to the forecast.

Republicans are playing defense in Alaska, Iowa, Maine, South Carolina and Texas, which are all rated toss-ups in the Power Rankings. They're also defending seats in competitive races in Kansas, which is rated Lean Republican, and Ohio, which is rated Lean Democrat.

Democrats are on defense in Michigan, which is rated a toss-up, and in Minnesota and New Hampshire, which are listed as Lean Democrat.

The GOP is hanging on to a razor-thin majority in the House, and the Power Rankings point to the Democrats retaking control of the chamber.

A good night for the Democrats could see them ending up with as many as 240 seats in the 435-seat chamber. But if Republicans have a good night, they could end up with 212 seats. That’s six short of a majority, but enough to make life difficult for the next Democratic House speaker, who would have to balance the demands of the moderate and far-left wings of the party.

Voters in Missouri weigh in on repealing a right to abortion and banning gender transition medical care for children. California, Colorado and Arizona hold ballot measures on policy around energy, transportation and development.

What to Consider When Choosing an Electric Fireplace

Various factors go into choosing the best electric fireplace for a given space and situation. You might simply be looking for aesthetics and something that feels homey, or you might prefer the look of a fireplace and the warmth of an electric heater. 

How an electric fireplace will be used and where it will go will help narrow the selection to the style best suited to an individual user’s needs. For instance, will it go in the living room under a wall-mounted TV, or will it add ambience in the dining room? The buyer’s skill level for installation or assembly is also important, along with any extra features and how realistic it needs to look.

The following considerations will help guide your decision on what makes the best electric fireplace for your particular home.

Type

Easy installation is a big selling point when it comes to electric fireplaces. Most simply plug into a wall outlet. The caveat, however, is that electric fireplaces that produce heat require a dedicated outlet. Due to power strip safety precautions, electric fireplaces can’t plug into an extension cord or surge protector. But many of the best electric fireplace models require very little else in the way of installation.

  • A wall-mounted electric fireplace resembles a flat-screen TV and can hang via brackets (usually included). These models have a sleek, minimalist aesthetic most suitable for modern interiors. Like recessed models, they’re often made of durable fiberglass or metal. 
  • Recessed fireplaces/inserts fit into a wall cavity, where the surface of a fireplace insert is flush with the wall. Unlike wall-mounted models, this type of fireplace comes in various modern and vintage styles and is best suited for DIY fireplaces.
  • Mantel fireplaces often mimic the look of a traditional fireplace and are constructed from wood, stone, or a combination of the two. These classic freestanding models are available in many styles besides traditional, including modern, rustic, and farmhouse. Some mantel fireplaces offer shelf storage and come in corner configurations as well.
  • Stove fireplaces offer the antique look of a wood-burning stove. These exclusively metal models often emit more heat than other fireplace types. They fit in well with rustic and farmhouse decor.
  • TV stand fireplaces are similar to entertainment consoles, with a television placed on top. Many models feature shelving that frames the fireplace. Available in multiple styles, they’re often made of wood or particleboard (a cheaper material consisting of wood and resin).

Size and Placement

You’ll likely know where you’d like to place an electric fireplace, and it’s important to note that not every model fits every room. Sleek wall-mounted ones, for example, look better in bedrooms and dining rooms. 

The planned location and type of fireplace will help determine the right size. It’s vital to get the exact measurements of the space where the electric fireplace will sit or hang, along with the size of the room. For instance, converting a conventional fireplace into an electric fireplace requires the precise measurements of the existing fireplace in order to find the best electric fireplace insert for the job.

The size of the room also makes a difference if the fireplace is meant to heat the room. In this case, it’s essential to ensure that the electric fireplace isn’t too small or too large for the room. Something big enough to heat a 1,200-square-foot room for a space that’s only 450 square feet might be overkill.

Heating Options

A handful of today’s electric fireplaces are purely for looks; they produce a visual fire effect but no heat. Most models, though, generate warmth in much the same way a space heater does. Some come with thermostats to maintain a steady temperature in the room, while others feature basic heat-level settings. 

All the best electric fireplace heater options offer the illusion of flickering flames, but heat options come in three basic types:

  • Fan-type electric: Many of the electric fireplaces on the market today are fan-type models that heat a room by blowing air over a series of hot coils and out one or more vents located along the bottom or sides of the unit. Fan-type fireplaces offer supplemental heat for rooms up to 450 square feet in size.
  • Infrared: Rather than heat the air, infrared fireplaces heat the objects in the room. Like the rays of the sun, infrared heat is heated light, warming whatever it shines upon. Infrared fireplaces can heat rooms up to 1,000 square feet, but the user must be in the direct line of its rays to feel the warmth.
  • Convection: Electric convection fireplaces allow air to circulate naturally. Cool air enters through a vent near the bottom, flows upward over the heated ceramic plates, and then flows out through an upper vent and into the room. Some convection fireplaces come with a fan to help direct the flow of air. These can heat rooms up to 600 square feet. Electric convection fireplaces without fans heat a smaller area near the fireplace itself and are best suited to rooms without drafts.

Keep in mind that electric fireplaces shouldn’t be the room’s only heat source. They cannot replace central heating or an electric wall heater, for example. 

Realism

Advances in LED light technology in combination with small rolling mirrors create the illusion of genuine flames in modern electric fireplaces. Some models include molded fake logs and embers that glow to enhance the effect. The most realistic electric fireplaces and realistic-looking features tend to be more expensive, but not always. 

However, not everyone prioritizes realism in an electric fireplace. Some units offer color selection for the flames, such as gold to match holiday decor, green for a spring look, or even purple or pink flames to match the room’s decor or set a certain mood.

Safety

Electric fireplaces don’t burn wood, create smoke, or have actual flames, all of which make them safer than the real thing. Moreover, the glass on most electric fireplaces doesn’t even get hot to the touch, making them safe for children and pets. Unlike their conventional counterparts, electric fireplaces can sit safely on carpet or against a wall. They also pose no risk of carbon monoxide exposure. 

In addition, electric fireplaces go through tests and inspections during production and come with safety features like automatic shutoffs set to a predetermined time or heat level. Additional safety features may include a Canadian Standards Association (CSA) seal. This certification ensures that the fireplace meets specific safety standards and regulations, giving users peace of mind knowing that the product has undergone rigorous testing and evaluation. 

A few tips to avoid electric fireplace mistakes that could affect safey: Follow the manufacturer’s installation instructions, don’t use extension cords or power strips, don’t place objects on the intake fans or heaters, keep them dry, and keep small children and pets away.

Additional Features

While electric fireplaces are pretty straightforward, some include a few fun bells and whistles, such as:

  • Remote and touch-screen controls
  • Programmable timers that turn the fireplace off after the user falls asleep, or turn it on before they wake up in the morning
  • Night-light features that emit a soft glow
  • Bluetooth speakers that can play crackling fire sounds or seasonal tunes

Installation

The difficulty of the installation depends on the type of electric fireplace, but it should be relatively easy for a person with the right skill set. Cutting into the wall and installing a recessed or see-through fireplace requires the user to have some framing skills. A wall-hanging unit will require hand tools, like a drill and a lever, and enough know-how to anchor the wall bracket securely. 

Some mantels require assembly while others don’t, and the same goes for TV stands. No matter what type of fireplace it is, make sure to follow the installation instructions carefully and ask for help if something is unclear.

The Advantages of Owning an Electric Fireplace

Many people love the idea of a fireplace in the living room, dining room, or bedroom. They provide a warm place to cuddle up with a good book or the latest movie. Not everyone is keen on the mess, work, and upkeep that come along with all that charm, though. Cleaning up ashes, stocking firewood, and maintaining the chimney are chores. Electric fireplaces relieve those burdens. 

Some other benefits of electric fireplaces include:

  • Easy installation
  • No chimney required
  • A choice of styles, including mantel, insert, TV stand, wall-mounted, and electric fireplace stove
  • No fireplace tools required
  • Compatible with any room
  • Modern features such as colored LED lights 
  • Some come with crackling sounds and other features that provide a more realistic experience

FAQs

For more information about the best electric fireplaces, check out these answers to some of the most common questions. If additional concerns arise, contact the manufacturer.

Q. Do electric fireplaces give off heat?

Yes, some give off supplemental heat. They are not intended to be a room’s only heat source, though.

Q. Do electric fireplaces increase electric bills?

Yes. An average electric fireplace uses about 1,500 watts of power. When used strictly for ambience, the cost can range from $0.003 to $0.03 per hour. When you add heat, the cost can range from $0.09 per hour for medium heat and $0.18 an hour for maximum heat.

Q. Can I plug an electric fireplace into a regular outlet?

Yes, you can plug one into a dedicated wall outlet. Do not plug one into an extension cord or power strip.

Q. Can you leave an electric fireplace on all night?

Yes, you can. 

Q. Can electric fireplaces catch fire?

Electric fireplaces don’t produce real fire. There are no flames, only LED lights. They don’t produce toxic byproducts either. If you follow the instructions, an electric fireplace should not cause a fire. However, ignoring instructions, such as using an extension cord, can cause fires.

Why driverless trucks won’t be taking over highways just yet

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Driverless trucks with artificial intelligence on board could dramatically change the landscape of freight shipping. A few companies have begun testing early models on some limited routes in Texas and California. Simulations of automated fleets show the potential benefits of the technology: The vehicles could improve safety, enhance fuel economy, and lessen transit times.

Truck makers and operators still have to solve some major issues, however, including how automated trucks will operate in variable weather and road conditions, and whether drivers should still be inside big rigs.

I am an engineer who studies human factors and transportation safety, and in my view, it is likely that automated trucks and human drivers will be working together for quite some time.

Benefits of automation

Fully automated trucks, which can drive on an interstate highway in good weather without human intervention, use a combination of AI, machine learning, computer vision, pattern recognition, and adaptive decision-making. Torc, one company developing these vehicles, uses AI to combine data from multiple sensors and generate models that predict truck performance.

Data taken from modern big rigs that are equipped with certain technology such as automatic emergency braking and lane-keeping assistance systems indicate that fully automated, driverless trucks could reduce the number and severity of crashes. They could also save fuel and therefore cost by optimizing acceleration and braking, and by running multiple vehicles in a close-knit convoy or platoon. Driverless trucks could reduce parking needs as well as emissions from idling because they could run almost 24 hours a day and would not need to idle when they do park, which is common for regular rigs at truck stops.

Finally, automated trucks could help ease the ongoing driver shortage. Ironically, automation could make driving less stressful and less physically demanding, improving job satisfaction among drivers.

Weather and work zones are hard to navigate

The main reason automated trucks are not common today is that they have difficulty operating in variable weather, under changing roadway conditions, and alongside unpredictable drivers.

Automated trucks require sensors, radars, and cameras that gather information about the environment around them to make decisions. However, snow and rain remain a problem for all automated vehicles, including cars, because they prevent the technology from “seeing” the roadway. Driverless taxi company Waymo, for example, operates driverless cars only in areas that get no snow.

Furthermore, any stretch of road where there may not be a predictable, clear surface and prevalent lines is difficult to handle. Imagine a construction zone with orange cones, narrow lanes, and missing lane lines. Driverless car companies still try to avoid these areas. And, oddly, some research shows that automobile drivers tend to crash into driverless cars at a higher rate than they do cars piloted by people.

Given those challenges, companies are testing automated trucks on roads that lack complications.

For example, autonomous truck maker Aurora is operating large freight trucks on I-45, a relatively flat and straight highway between Dallas and Houston. Rival Kodiak’s robotrucks are delivering sand to industrial sites in Texas over roads few people use. Few if any automated trucks are being tested in chaotic urban environments, given the pedestrians, bicyclists, traffic lights, and elements of city traffic.

Driverless trucks are operating on some roads in Texas.

Some companies that were developing automated trucks have scaled back ambitions, changed direction, or exited the market.

In 2023, Embark, TuSimple, and Locomotion closed down. Waymo began to focus solely on cars. Other companies narrowed their focus to trucks that would operate only along a few dedicated routes between two large cities or manufacturing sites.

Drivers and trucks that work together

In my view, for years to come, automated trucks with AI-based controls will still require a driver.

The best technology will support a symbiosis between human and machine, where the person and the truck each do what they do best. A human would likely drive the truck across the “first mile”—the uncertain and crowded warehouse grounds and local streets to a highway. The driver would then stop using the steering wheel, gas pedal, and brake and let automation take over. The human would take back control during the “last mile” to the truck’s final destination.

Research my colleagues and I conducted shows that human-machine interfaces that communicate between drivers and autonomous vehicles will be key. For example, a screen or head-up display inside the cab would tell the driver how the technology is doing. Verbal messages or a vibrating seat could signal that the automation cannot handle a developing situation, and the driver would take over.

Developers will have to be aware of possible biases. So-called automation bias could lead to a driver who trusts and relies on the truck’s decision-making so much that they defer to it. Algorithmic bias could cause truck sensors to recognize certain groups of people on the road more than others, such as only detecting pedestrians of a certain size or shape.

The long-term vision is to have no driver, in part to lower costs. But driverless trucks do not seem to me to be practical on a large scale in the near future. It is more realistic to envision the driver and the automated truck as a team. Vehicle manufacturers, technology developers, regulators, and drivers will need to work together to realize the benefits.

Shannon Roberts is an associate professor of industrial engineering at UMass Amherst
