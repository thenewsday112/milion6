Skiing Vermont's Pre-pre-Season Isn't for the Faint of Heart

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

To everything there is a season, and the first flakes in the forecast mean it is time for pre-pre season skiing.

Pre-pre is an autumnal ritual that was established long before I stumbled into town, including all who see skis and knees as a renewable resource. As soon as the slightest hint of winter-ish precipitation seems likely, a ragtag band assembles. Preceded by panicked dives into musty gear bins and resurfaced memories of buckles that barely limped to the end of the season, the gang assembled at a relaxed 7:30 a.m. at the Stowe base. 

Unlike previous years, it seemed that the forecast failed us. Despite my dear friend and weather fiend Aaron Rice’s assurances, the snow didn’t arrive. The father of a two-week-old can be forgiven for meteorological misses.

The soft morning light revealed not coveted snowliage, but rather past-peak yellows and stick season knocking at the door. As the low cloud slowly lifted, white appeared high above us. SOMETHING. Something frozen, something worth walking up the hill for. Our collection of rock skis runs the gamut from a couple of pairs of mid-2010s pow skis with frame bindings to a mismatched pair of seventy-something underfoot. Three of us, including myself, chose to throw skins on while the rest chose to throw skis on a pack and march up the grass. We took the usual uphill route, Nosedive, making our way nicely through the freshly-mowed grass. Windy and slightly damp, the skinning is rather easy and truly quite comparable to the mid-winter experience, a bit of a shocking revelation. There is no snow here. A few bits of graupel dot the ground, if you seek them out. Nonetheless, we are here and up we go. 

Aaron Rice

It all feels so familiar, the first steep punch, the sharp right-hand turn into the flats, the stream that drains across the trail that we jumped over in May. Six months later we are back in the same place with the same people, friends hanging out, chatting, bickering, and catching up. This year, the ritual feels a bit different; two in the group have had kids since last year, so the banter is tamer and more focused on human development than on late-twentysomething debauchery. We keep getting older, but the allure of early-season turns keeps us dumb. 

About forty minutes into our day we hit the frost. The coefficient of friction underfoot drops considerably. Surely this grass will be fast. Up the Nosedive headwall we plod; the sight of many mid-season backward Bambi slides. Luckily, we make it through unscathed. The last couple of pitches pass quickly; it’s chilly now, and if you look at the rime-covered trees, it feels like  ski season. 

As experienced optimists, the optimal route down the mountain is clearly mapped in our minds. Avoiding rocks is the number one goal, followed by ensuring there is enough pitch to make some sliding happen. Luckily, the Queen Green Mountains provide ample terrain. 

With just a bit of faffing around, we point them downhill. A deep breath or two, a little smile, and all of a sudden the wind is in my face. Going in a straight line of frozen grass feels like skiing. It is skiing. Turning is a different story as the skis disengage from the grass. I’ve learned from Aaron that a little hop at the start of the turn is crucial. Quickly, Jeff takes a digger but luckily avoids catastrophe. The speeds are slow, but the ground is far scarier than snow.

Aaron Rice

After a few hundred feet, the frost runs out. Our joy doesn‘t, and with the anticipation of fallen leaves on the lower slopes we pick our way down through the rocky bits. We know it’s silly, but we deeply don’t care. Actually, we do care; it makes us love it more. Finally, on the top of North Slope, the levers have stacked up on skiers’ right. Now we are cooking, we can even make some semblance of turns. Dennis gets dared to take a side hit; he does, and of course backslaps. When we hit the road at the bottom, he also somehow manages to go head over heels and double eject, mud staining his entire back. 

Moments later, we arrive in the parking lot to the chagrin of leaf-peeping tourists. They are mostly confused and a little bit horrified. Soon I am horrified by Dennis’s request for a second lap. All the others scatter to their kids and families, but I acquiesce. Skins back on, we march upwards under the gondola, putting on a show for the flatlanders. It feels right to go up again since what’s better than one lap? Two. 

When we finally reach the top, instead of ripping skins, I click out of my skis. “Enough is enough,” I think to myself as I download the gondola. We’ve had our fun for now. It’s only mid-October, and the forecast calls for sunshine for the foreseeable future, so our next chance will have to wait. It will snow; we will all gather again, but it won’t be the first time for the first frost.

Later, Jeff calls me to go mountain biking. As we ride, it begins snowing. How about that?!

'Mother of all editors' Emacs is 50

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

No lesser authority than the MIT Technology Review says that the Emacs editor has turned 50 years old. But then, it is biased — Emacs was invented in MIT's AI lab.

Simson Garfinkel's article is simply called EMACS turns 50, but the sub-heading explains why it matters more:

"How the MIT AI Lab's text editor gave birth to the free software movement."

There are multiple timelines and accounts of the origins of Emacs, and it is not a single monolithic application. The name is an abbreviation for "Editing MACroS", and the original editor for which this set of macros was developed was TECO – "Text Editor and COrrector," written by Dan Murphy in 1962. Murphy died earlier this year at the age of 83.

The inimitable Jamie Zawinski has an encyclopedic Emacs Timeline – as he mentions at the start, his interest being that he long ago maintained a fork of GNU Emacs, originally called Lucid Emacs. It's still around, under its later name XEmacs. There are a lot of forks and re-implementations of Emacs out there, and we are not going to go into all of them, or this article would become a book – one only interesting to programmer-archeologists (to use a term coined by the late great Vernor Vinge).

As for who originally wrote those Editing Macros, though, another history has quotations from several of the people involved: On the Origin of Emacs in 1976. (Note – some of the quotes still contain the | symbols that marked quoted text.)

This credits Lisp boffin Guy L. Steele Jr with the original work in 1975, but in it, Steele says:

"RMS is responsible for the names 'E' and 'EMACS'. RMS still deserves 99% or 99.9% or 99.99% or 99.999% of the credit for taking a package of TECO macros and turning it into the most powerful editor on the planet."

RMS is of course Richard Stallman, and the page also quotes some emails from RMS on October 23 and 31, 1976. Mind you, just a few years after the event, in a 1981 talk now preserved on the GNU website, Stallman said, "I began the development of EMACS in 1974 with an improvement to TECO". Like all the best FOSS, even in its very early stages, it was a collaborative effort – but around late October 1976 is when RMS took over the bulk of the development.

Of the many forks of Emacs over its half a century of development, some are still just about hanging on in there. When we looked up the link for XEmacs, we were surprised to see that there was a new release: XEmacs 21.5.37 "lettuce" appeared just last month. And even the forks have forks: in this case, SXEmacs, last updated in 2020.

Most of the changes in XEmacs and SXEmacs eventually made their way back upstream to GNU Emacs, which is still in active development, although as The Register reported in 2008, RMS is no longer the primary maintainer. GNU Emacs 31.1 appeared in August.

Cards on the metaphorical table: this vulture is not an Emacs user. We have tried. A major influence was one of the Reg FOSS desk's favorite writers, Neal Stephenson, who in 1999 published an essay called In the Beginning… Was the Command Line. (It was later republished as a rather short book, but the text is still online.) In it, he says:

"In the GNU/Linux world there are two major text editing programs: the minimalist vi (known in some implementations as elvis) and the maximalist emacs. I use emacs, which might be thought of as a thermonuclear word processor. It was created by Richard Stallman; enough said. It is written in Lisp, which is the only computer language that is beautiful. It is colossal, and yet it only edits straight ASCII text files, which is to say, no fonts, no boldface, no underlining. In other words, the engineer-hours that, in the case of Microsoft Word, were devoted to features like mail merge, and the ability to embed feature-length motion pictures in corporate memoranda, were, in the case of emacs, focused with maniacal intensity on the deceptively simple-seeming problem of editing text. If you are a professional writer–i.e., if someone else is getting paid to worry about how your words are formatted and printed–emacs outshines all other editing software in approximately the same way that the noonday sun does the stars."

Emacs is 50-plus-year-old software, and it shows. This is a huge and complicated program: decades ago, the joke was that EMACS stood for "Eight Megs And Constantly Swapping". It is not an easy program to learn. Despite lavish documentation and a built-in tutorial, this is software so old that its terminology predates microprocessors and the personal computer.

Its exhaustive manual is 804 pages long, and to pick some trivial examples, its terms for parts of the screen use “frame” to mean what’s now called a window, and “window” to mean what’s now called a pane. In the section on Keys, it discusses the Control and Meta keys. That’s a slight snag: you don’t have a Meta key, and neither do the authors of Emacs. It was a key on the now almost legendarily rare Space Cadet Keyboard, of which the Deskthority keyboard wiki says:

“It is known as the holy grail of keyboards.”

You can buy a modern replica, the Keymacs keyboard. They are hand-made in the Czech Republic, and depending on exactly what model and specifications you want, they cost from €2,998 for the Lispsaver up to €6,878 for the A624N Space Cadet.

In other words, you can have a keyboard with a real Meta key if you want, but it is going to cost you.

One of the problems with these historical terms, unfamiliar to anyone who wasn’t already programming in the 1970s, is that meanings drift. In the context of Emacs, "Meta" means Alt, or Opt on Macs. However, KDE also uses the Meta key – but what the KDE documentation calls "Meta" is the Windows key – or Command on Macs. Like most Linux folks, the GNOME documentation calls it the Super key.

Names are important. They matter. Who's right, the Emacs folks or the KDE folks? Well, neither: nobody has Meta keys any more. We have Ctrl, Alt, and Cmd/Super/Windows.

There have been valiant efforts to modernize Emacs and bring it into line with modern UI and terminology. This vulture tried to learn Aquamacs, which modernized Emacs with a native macOS UI, but sadly it's no longer maintained.

Another, which we enjoyed trying, is ErgoEmacs, which changes the key bindings and rearranges the UI into something much more like the IBM Common User Access arrangement, as used in Windows and most Linux desktops. However, it never really caught on and you need to know your way around Emacs to install it and configure it for your regional keyboard layout.

So complex is it to configure Emacs that there are multiple pre-configured distributions of Emacs, such as Doom Emacs, or the minimalist Emacs Solo. We wish someone did a "CUA-Macs" config, with ErgoEmacs integrated and able to pick up your existing keyboard config.

Come to that, the Gershwin Desktop project that we mentioned along with GhostBSD last year is in active development, and it's based around GNUstep. Maybe its developers could pick up Aquamacs, and adapt it to GNUstep.

There is a long history of such Emacs facelifts. One instance is Borland’s Sprint word-processor, one of the selling points of which was that it could emulate the user interfaces of most of the leading MS-DOS word-processors of its day (just before they all converged on IBM CUA). Before Borland acquired it, Sprint was called The Final Word, developed by musical equipment makers Mark of the Unicorn. FinalWord was based on an Emacs clone called MINCE.

The Great Rival

Another well-known Emacs distribution is Spacemacs, which adapts Emacs to have a more Vi-like user interface.

Vi is the other seminal Unix text editor, as the Stephenson quote above mentions. As this article on Origins and the Evolution of Vi & Vim notes, it too dates back to 1976. Indeed, that’s the reason we chose the year in this story: Tilde, a text editor that doesn’t work like it’s 1976. One feature of Tilde we enjoy is that it supports use of the mouse even on the Linux console – which is the headline new feature of GNU Emacs 31.1.

Vi is very much alive and well today. GNU Emacs is based on the original source code, but Vi mainly survives as re-implementations. The recreation Stephenson mentions, Elvis, lives on today in the form of nvi.

However, by far the best known is Vim, maintained by Bram Moolenaar until his death in 2023. In the grand FOSS forking tradition, it too has forks, including Neovim, as used in Omarchy, and more recently, the AI-free Vim Classic – a new project led by Drew DeVault, who just launched the similarly LLM-free AlpineBSD. ®

Think back to when you were a kid and watching football on the TV. A player picks the ball up on the edge of the box, dribbles past one or two defenders and then is faced with the goal. At that point, did you ever think “I hope he recycles possession here.”

At the risk of sounding more dinosaur than a velociraptor appearing on Mick McCarthy and Tony Pulis’ podcast, one of the worst things about modern football is the dying breed of the midfield baller who just fancies having a go.

It used to be a staple of the Premier League. The midfield renegade who had ridiculous technique and was given the freedom to have a go from long range. These days, a failed long-range effort will have you scorned by the manager for failing to spot the winger who had more space out wide.

But there is one player in the Premier League who is pleasingly old school in his approach, Ipswich’s Julio Enciso.

The Paraguayan returned to Portman Road in the summer having spent the 2023-24 season on loan and Ipswich fans were rightly a little sceptical.

In his first stint at the club, Enciso was nothing special. He had come through the Brighton academy, but perhaps lacked the confidence or experience to be at his best. He played 13 games for the Suffolk club, scoring just two goals. Even if one of them was a screamer.

After Ipswich, he looked set to disappear into the BlueCo spreadsheet as he joined Strasbourg but in the summer, Ipswich, now managed by Enciso’s Strasbourg boss Gary O’Neil, brought the 22-year-old to the club permanently for a fee of £26m. Since then, he has been Ipswich’s best player.

For anyone of an Ipswich persuasion, he is stupidly good fun to watch. He possesses an excellent dribbling technique that means the ball is never more than an inch away from his leading foot, even while running at speeds as high as 34.4km/h and that close control makes him an absolute nightmare for defenders.

Enciso is seventh in the Premier League for most dribbles completed this season but what makes him particularly fun is that he will almost certainly get a shot away at the end of it. He is second behind only Erling Haaland for shots attempted this season having had 22 of them. Jeremy Doku, who tops the dribble metric, has had 10 shots this season in comparison.

And for a 22-year-old non-native, Enciso has shown a surprising desire to get amongst it from a physical standpoint too. He has won the possession in the final third the most of any Ipswich player this season and is more than happy to use his body instead of going down easily.

Every skill of Enciso was showcased in his excellent equaliser against Hull on Saturday.

The move starts with him being clattered in the back by Oli McBurnie and rather than go down, he springs forward and somehow manages to organise his feet quickly enough to skin Tim Iroegbunam.

And then it is panic stations for Nobel Mendy who is one-on-one with Enciso on the edge of the box. Enciso is right-footed so Mendy is smart to try and shift him onto his left side and Enciso even tricks him into thinking he is going right.

He feints right but cuts left and Mendy, whose momentum is taking him to Enciso’s right, has now lost a yard and given the Paraguayan the necessary space for a shot.

Enciso takes another touch to ensure he would be clear of Mendy’s diving leg and with his weaker foot picks out the top corner past Konstantinos Tzolakis.

It is a sublime goal that deservedly brought Ipswich level and rescued a point from a game that had threatened to get away from them.

Credit must also go to O’Neil for identifying Enciso as the answer to a problem that hampered Ipswich last time round in the Premier League.

In their first season back, Ipswich lacked goals from anyone other than Liam Delap. Sammie Szmodics was second top scorer on just four, Enciso is already halfway to that tally.

The blueprint of how to utilise Enciso was created by his national team. He has been described as the best Paraguayan talent of the last 30 years and the team is set up to flow through him. O’Neil recognised that and built his Strasbourg side in the same way, allowing Enciso to score nine goals in the second half of last season.

Having a number 10 who is given the freedom to shoot does require a specific tactical setup. Florentino Luis, Exequiel Palacios and Sasa Lukic are all players who are happy to sit and cover any space Enciso may leave behind while striker Emersonn has so far been more effective in bringing others into the game compared to Delap, whose instruction was to score. But it is so far proving effective.

If Ipswich are to stay up, and their start to the season suggests that is well within the realm of possibility, Enciso will be crucial to that. But more than that, the Portman Road faithful can simply enjoy a player who is focused on producing the most fun parts of football.

Royal Caribbean Out-Earned Hilton’s Whole 2025 In A Quarter

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Royal Caribbean earned $1.7 billion in one quarter, more than Hilton made in all of 2025. Cruising now out-earns hotels and car rentals by a wide margin.

Royal Caribbean Just Posted Another Record

Royal Caribbean earned $1.7 billion in Q3 2026, or $6.45 per share, up from $5.74 a year ago. Revenue climbed 9% to $5.6 billion, and the company raised its full-year adjusted earnings guidance to between $17.85 and $17.95 per share. RCCL Management said bookings are up in the mid-teens since the last call, and 2027 sailings are up in the high teens, booked at record rates.

Net yields (revenue per passenger cruise day) rose only 0.9%, so most of the growth came from more capacity rather than higher spend per guest. That capacity is spread across three brands many travelers don’t realize share a parent. Royal Caribbean International is the big-ship family product, Celebrity Cruises is the premium line, and Silversea is the luxury line. A traveler who would never book Royal might happily book Celebrity, and plenty of Silversea guests have no idea Royal Caribbean owns it. Each serves a distinct market, and all three feed RCCL’s results.

How Cruising Stacks Up Against Everyone Else

Comparisons are fun, but until I looked at the numbers, I wasn’t aware of just how impressively cruise stacks up against even asset-light industries like modern hotel chains. The four big cruise companies with public data available earned about $8.6 billion in net income on roughly $60.8 billion in revenue. That includes Carnival at $2.8 billion, Royal Caribbean at $4.3 billion, Viking at $1.1 billion and Norwegian at $423 million (a GAAP figure; its adjusted net income was Norwegian at $423 million.05 billion). These four operate on a net margin of about 14%. MSC Cruises, the other giant, belongs to the privately held MSC Group and does not publish comparable results.

Hotels, comparatively, are less profitable for the most part. Marriott earned $2.6 billion, Hilton earned $1.5 billion, and Hyatt lost $52 million. IHG earned $759 million on $5.2 billion of revenue. The four combined for about $4.8 billion, or a little over half of what the four cruise companies made.

Car rental is worse. Avis Budget lost $995 million, including a $518 million impairment on its electric vehicle fleet, and Hertz lost about $747 million, according to the wider-than-expected losses. Together that is a $1.7 billion loss on about $20 billion of revenue. Enterprise Holdings (Enterprise, National, and Alamo) is a private company and does not report results publicly.

Airlines are the only group that earns more than cruising in nominal dollars, and only because of their sheer scale. IATA put 2025 global airline net profit at $45 billion on $1.065 trillion of revenue, a 4.2% margin, and cut its 2026 forecast to $23 billion at 2.0%. Simply, four cruise companies earned about 19% of what every airline in the world made combined, on less than 6% of the revenue.

Why The Margins Look So Different

Hotel companies like Marriott and Hilton are asset-light, so their revenue includes massive amounts of reimbursed costs and franchise fees, and most of the physical buildings are owned by other entities. A fair comparison would use margin on fees, and I would not suggest these numbers are apples-to-apples. Cruise lines own the ships, the fuel bill and the debt, with Royal Caribbean carrying about $22.4 billion of it, so their profit has to cover a much heavier balance sheet even after accelerated (and substantial) depreciation.

Passenger captivity on a ship is unrivaled throughout the travel industry. Travelers pay for the room, the meals, the drinks, the excursions and the Wi-Fi in one transaction, all on a property the cruise line controls. Royal Caribbean says close to half of this year’s guests are repeat customers and about 40% belong to its loyalty program. A hotel chain would love to know exactly how much each guest spends in its properties but they don’t; cruise lines know it down to the cent.

Royal Caribbean’s $1.7 billion, Carnival’s $1.9 billion and Norwegian’s $491 million add up to about $4.1 billion in net income in a single quarter. That roughly matches what Marriott, Hilton and Hyatt earned together in all of 2025. Hilton’s own third quarter net income was $536 million, about a third of Royal Caribbean’s. That’s absolutely incredible, especially when

Two caveats belong here. My sample includes only the hotel companies and cruise lines that publish numbers I could confirm, so MSC and plenty of private hotel owners are missing. The fiscal years also do not line up perfectly, because Carnival’s year ends in November. But it’s also worth noting that Hilton has about 18x the keys that Royal does, owns less of the product itself (and carries fewer costs), and still falls woefully behind Royal.

Why This Explains The Sandals Deal

When I wrote about Royal Caribbean’s $3 billion Sandals deal in September, part of the claim was that Royal knows the customer base well (it does) and that it knows the market well (it does) but understanding the power of capturing the total spend for a client trip is the magic. With this much cash coming off the ships, buying 50% of Sandals, expected to close in early 2027, gives Royal Caribbean a stake in the land-based all-inclusive business. The same logic is why I argued that every big hotel brand wants your all-inclusive money. The cruise industry is not trying to beat hotels at hotels; rather it’s buying the segment of the hotel business that already works like a cruise.

Conclusion

My agency sells both cruises and hotel rooms, and the difference in how the two operators treat the traveler’s wallet is all the difference. Cruise companies have figured out how to capture the whole vacation budget on a floating property they control, and the earnings show it. I expect the gap with hotels to widen next year as 2027 bookings convert at record rates, and I expect more hotel companies to copy the model through all-inclusive resorts and bundled pricing. But I was still shocked that Hilton, which owns very few hotels, and is mostly a marketing arm for a far larger footprint, is dwarfed by a cruise line in a single quarter.

Ahead of Tuesday’s midterms, Democrats are biting their nails and enduring sleepless nights, wondering whether they will be forced to endure yet another election that has so often characterized the Trump era: close races that seem inevitably to go to the Republicans. All the fundamentals and polls indicate a possible blowout and a big one, but after watching Americans shockingly elect Donald Trump the first time, live through his Big Lie and witness his historic comeback only two years ago, no one can feel confident that this era of chaos, corruption and cruelty might finally be beginning its descent into the dustbin of history. We thought we were here before, and it didn’t end well.

There are reasons to be hopeful, though, even if you assume that the polls are underestimating Republican enthusiasm for their leaders once again. While there have been misses in the polling over this past decade, it hasn’t manifested in midterms as it has in the races with Trump on the ballot. In those years, the polling was pretty much on the money. Democrats have done very well in all the off-year and special elections this cycle, and although that’s no guarantee, it has to be considered a good sign. 

The big difference this time is that Trump’s approval rating is hovering close to 30%, a lethal number, and he has presided over a high cost of living and a war of choice with Iran, both things he promised would never happen if he were president. The one election he lost in 2020 was the result of his mismanagement of the only crisis he faced, the Covid-19 pandemic, and this second term has solidified the impression among many voters who were inclined to give him the benefit of the doubt anyway, that he simply doesn’t know what he is doing.

One sign that should give Democrats some real comfort in these last couple of days is Trump’s performance on the campaign trail. To say the 80-year-old has lost a step is an understatement. Sure, he barnstormed through all the battleground states and even attempted to shore up enthusiasm in places that should not need his presence to get out the vote. But his patented “weave” is unraveling at every stop. 

Trump has taken to doubling down on the weirdness, going on about men’s bodies to a truly bizarre degree, being under the impression that because his trained rally goers will ritualistically cheer anything he says, this is a winning message. His recent comments warning people to stay away from Mar-a-Lago, his private Florida club, because they would be unable to afford the hamburgers could not be more in keeping with his apparent pursuit of Marie Antoinette’s legacy.

But most embarrassing for the president is that he is no longer the singular star of the Trump show. As POLITICO reported, after years of ragging on Democrats for bringing celebrities on the campaign trail and bragging that he didn’t need any to help him fill up stadiums, the president has invited some to join him at campaign rallies — although one could scarcely call them A-listers. The cast leans heavily on older athletes, from retired NFL players to race car drivers, although Trump did manage to talk at least one genuine country music star, Jason Aldean, to join him in a rally held just a few miles from his home. Rapper Nicki Minaj has become a staple on the trail, and the president’s old friend Mike Tyson is often with him. Trump simply can’t carry the show by himself anymore. 

This is the most expensive campaign in history, with Elon Musk alone spending more than $400 million in money he dug out of his couch cushions, but the total spending on both sides has reached upwards of $16 billion, the vast majority of it to benefit the Republicans. The fact is that it doesn’t appear to have done them much good. In fact, their spending is almost all coming from large donors like Musk, while their small donor base has shrunk considerably. That’s usually a sign of a lack of enthusiasm, and it doesn’t bode well for Tuesday’s results.

The election’s stakes are profound. America crossed the Rubicon in 2024 when it invited Trump back into power after he tried to overturn the election, was convicted of fraud, and was revealed to have possessed and leaked classified information after he was out of office. The media and the nation’s elites interpreted that victory as a sign that Trump was some sort of magical superhero, and that it was in all their interests to join with him and reap the rewards. The result has been nothing short of catastrophic, revealing gaping holes in our constitutional order, weaknesses in our democratic system and a void in our society where moral authority used to be. America has never been perfect, and it always falls short of its professed ideals, but this political era has stripped away the belief that we have any at all. 

The corruption on display, coupled with a lack of concern for the lives of ordinary people, is breathtaking. And while it remains shocking that tens of millions of Americans still support this administration, it seems that a majority may have finally had the veil lifted from their eyes, allowing them to see the incompetence, grift and megalomania for what it is.

It’s hard to see how a nation founded on the ideas espoused in the Declaration of Independence can survive without even the pretense of ethics or universal values. At the moment, the American creed is simply “to the victor go the spoils,” by which Trump is not just imperiously referring to the foreign countries he seeks to colonize. He’s talking about America itself.

There can be no more questions about what the Republican Party is capable of, and as weak as he appears on the campaign trail, Trump is clearly frustrated and angry. Reporting suggests that he plans to double down on all of it the minute the election is over. If the American people somehow fail to forcefully repudiate Trump and the oligarchs who use and manipulate him, it’s unlikely we’ll get another chance.
