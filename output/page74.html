Putrid blood shoots from these lizards’ eyes

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Horned lizards are spiky cuties. Three to six inches long, these lizards are coated with sharp spines all across their hard, sand-colored backs. But in the dry deserts and prairies they call home, they are still vulnerable. As they scuttle through the protective brush in arid landscapes from Mexico to southern Canada, they have to reckon with hungry coyotes, snakes that will gobble them whole, and even peckish roadrunners. That’s why multiple species of horned lizards have developed a weird but effective deterrent: blood.

And not just any blood: These lizards shoot foul-smelling blood out of their eyes, sending many would-be predators scattering. But experts say the lizards are now facing other threats to their survival that even a fountain of eye blood can’t deter.

The unusual diet that gives horned lizards foul blood

Dean Williams, a professor of biology at Texas Christian University, studies horned lizard populations in central Texas. He says that blood squirting is really a backup defense for the lizards. “Their first line of defense is going to be crypsis,” aka blending in with their background, says Williams. 

Williams and his colleagues tag the lizards they study with radio trackers that pinpoint their location to an area roughly the size of an office desk. Even then, he says, it can take “15 minutes of rummaging” to find the lizard in their scrubland habitat. The lizards don’t move much, he added, blending in with nearby rocks. 

Horned lizards are master hiders and can easily blend into their arid desert homes. Image: Dean Williams

But when hiding doesn’t work, the lizards deploy their unusual, foul-smelling blood, which Williams says is a product of an equally foul delicacy. “Their diet consists mainly of these large Pogonomyrmex or harvester ants,” says Williams. Harvester ants defend themselves using neurotoxic venom that causes intense, long-lasting, stinging pain in mammals. 

Unfortunately for the harvester ants, this toxin doesn’t even slightly deter horned lizards, who eat the ants in large numbers. A 2024 study identified that a compound in the lizards’ blood spiked after scientists fed them harvester ants. The researchers think this compound, possibly adapted from ant venom, makes the lizards’ blood taste foul. 

How horned lizards turn their blood into a weapon

However, that gross metabolized ant blood coursing through the lizards’ veins wouldn’t be much help if it only annoyed a predator after it had already bitten off the lizard’s head. That’s where the lizards’ unusual squirting mechanism comes in handy. 

Williams explains that the lizards can build up pressure in blood vessels around their sinuses. Eventually, the vessels burst, shooting the toxic blood out in jets. Mammals exposed to the stream of blood react with notable disgust. There seems to be little consequence for the lizards, even though in one experiment, a lizard lost 6.8 percent of its body mass after squirting blood when exposed to a dog. 

The foul-smelling blood doesn’t protect the lizards from every predator, though. Roadrunners and other birds of prey still chow down on them, says Williams. To guard against birds, the lizards use the sharp horns on the back of their heads to stab at predators that try to seize them in their beaks.

Horned lizards only shoot gross blood from their eyes when hiding doesn’t work. Video: Blood Squirting Lizard, National Geographic

The lizards’ foul blood also does little to deter snakes. Instead, the lizards have evolved specialized strategies to evade these predators. Rattlesnakes are lazy hunters that wait in the shadows for prey to approach. Horned lizards have learned to run away from these snakes. 

But Williams says they use a different strategy if hunted by a coachwhip or racer snake. These fearsome snakes can easily chase down the lizards, who instead quickly flatten their bodies against the dirt, showing the snake their spiked backs, which makes them harder to swallow. 

Why horned lizards are in trouble in Texas

But despite these bespoke, often ingenious defense mechanisms, horned lizards in the Lone Star State are under serious threat. They’ve disappeared from their habitat across eastern Texas. Williams says that industrialization is devastating lizard populations. “They seem to be very sensitive to those habitat changes,” he says. 

In small towns, the lizards can live alongside humans when their natural brush habitat is preserved. But in towns where humans cleared the brush, “the lizards just disappeared,” Williams says. 

Horned lizards are ectotherms, meaning they must change their behavior to keep their body temperature under control. In the blistering heat of a Texas summer, the lizards simply can’t keep their bodies within safe limits once the cool shade of vegetation has disappeared. 

Baby horned lizards can fit in the palm of your hand. Image: Dean Williams

Harvester ants provide horned lizards with one of their strongest defenses. But another ant species has become the second major challenge to the lizards’ survival. Fire ants are an invasive species in the United States. They swarm the nests of ground-dwelling lizards, eating vulnerable eggs, or even attacking and killing young lizards. 

That’s where conservation efforts, like the one Williams works on with zoos across Texas, play an important role. The program releases nearly a thousand young lizards each year. 

These hatchlings, which can easily fit in the palm of a hand, are packaged in individual Tupperware containers and then released in the desert scrub. Williams says that his graduate student, Kira Gangbin, has been working on a targeted poison to cut down fire ant numbers and help keep these hatchlings alive. These efforts will hopefully keep horned lizard populations thriving for many more years. Just remember: If you come across a horned lizard on a hike through the Texas desert, give it a wide berth, or risk a foul, bloody surprise.

The best new science-fiction novels of November 2026

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Characters in The Falling, out this month, face a similar danger to those in the film Deep Impact, shown here Paramount/Everett/Shutte​rstock

I am currently deeply engrossed in the November read for the New Scientist Book Club, a piece of classic science fiction (well, from 2012) from Chris Beckett, Dark Eden. But I’m also in the mood for some fresh writing, and the new sci-fi on offer this month is an exciting and eclectic range. I’m keen to check out Benjamin Percy’s audio drama Exoplanet, produced by none other than Andy Weir, as well as books from some old favourites: Michael Moorcock, Carole Hailey, John Scalzi and Andrew Hunter Murray. And for all those still in the mood for scares after Halloween, there’s some space horror from SA Barnes in the deliciously named Cold Eternity.

The best new science-fiction books of October 2026

Exoplanet by Benjamin Percy

This is something a little different – produced by Andy Weir, author of The Martian and Project Hail Mary, and written by Benjamin Percy, this sci-fi thriller is a full-cast audio drama from Audible, and it sounds really exciting. It follows the fallout when a quantum anomaly tears a starship apart, leaving its crew in different eras on the same deadly exoplanet – one in a machine-ruled future, for example, another in a past peopled by hostile aliens. I’m a recent (only this year) audiobook adopter, and this sounds right up my street.

Matt Damon in the film adaptation of Andy Weir’s novel The Martian Aidan Monaghan/THA/Shutterstoc​k

Everything Happens So Much by Andrew Hunter Murray

I really enjoyed Andrew Hunter Murray’s speculative novel The Last Day, in which the world stops turning, leaving half the planet in scorching sunlight and the other half permanently dark. This latest book sounds equally interesting. A young woman, Esme, escapes her dull job by taking tiny potions known as The Elsewheres, which allow her to visit alternate versions of herself. But then an “incident” turns her into the most important person in this, or possibly any, universe. How will she handle it?

Advertisement

Monsters of Ohio by John Scalzi

Sci-fi legend John Scalzi’s latest speculative adventure has a Lovecraftian tinge, as the locals in Richland, Ohio, take offence to the actions of a predatory private-equity firm – and develop some unexpected tentacles. The local police chief, and former resident Mike, get entangled in what’s going on and in events that are likely to change humanity forever.

The Falling by Carole Hailey

I always appreciate a good “asteroid hitting Earth” story (my recent-ish reads include Larry Niven and Jerry Pournelle’s Lucifer’s Hammer, a classic from 1977, and Not With A Bang by Temi Oh, a twist on the tale). Carole Hailey’s previous book, The Silence Project, was great, so I’m looking forward to her take on this story. It follows Cassie, who grew up with a father obsessed with an impending apocalypse. Now she’s middle-aged and discovers that his teachings might have some use after all when an asteroid is found in the solar system, heading for northern Europe.

Michael Moorcock, who has a new book out this month Ulf Andersen/Getty Images

The Wounds of Albion by Michael Moorcock

There aren’t many names in science fiction and fantasy bigger than Michael Moorcock, and this is the final part of his “fictional autobiography”. It sees, if I understand the blurb correctly, the author tell the story of both his real life (from the impact of his science fiction to the music of the sixties) and of a fantasy world only he can see (the example given: “the Carmelite Monks must protect their latest multiverse-spanning invention”). Intriguingly weird, as you’d expect from this wonderful writer – I’m keen to see how, as we’re promised, “Moorcock meets ‘Moorcock’ and together they produce a remedy for the world’s pain!”.

Cold Eternity by SA Barnes

If I were SA Barnes, I would be pretty pleased with the moniker laid on me by my publisher: she is, apparently, “the queen of space horror”. In this latest, Halley Zwick (who is on the run) boards the spaceship Elysian Fields. This was sold to the super-rich as a place to enter cryo-sleep until their immortality could be assured, but it has been silent and dark for decades… although is it really?

On the Calculation of Volume V by Solvej Balle, translated by Jennifer Russell and Sophia Hersi Smith

My colleague Gerardo loved previous instalments in this series, which follows a protagonist living the same day on repeat. Tara has now spent 12 years reliving 18 November, while her partner Thomas, to whom she has returned, repeats his own identical day. Tara sets off with her friends for a community where other time-stranded people have come together and begins to see a life beyond Thomas on the horizon.

Bridge Planet Nine by Jared Throne

This is a graphic novel in which four thieves travel to an abandoned colony planet (Bridge Planet Nine) with plans to rob one of the uncrewed freighter ships that land there. But it turns out this apparently deserted outpost isn’t as empty as they thought…

Alfa by Lilja Sigurdardóttir, translated by Larissa Kyzer

This speculative crime novel was shortlisted for the Icelandic Crime Book of the Year award. It’s set in Reykjavík in 2052, where an artificial intelligence manages society and ensures it is almost crime-free. Sabína discovers that her brother, a dissident who lives outside the controlling AI’s reach, has been murdered, and she begins investigating his final days. I’ve enjoyed Sigurdardóttir’s straight crime novels, so will definitely be exploring this one.

Eternity’s Tomb by Dan Moren

Ex-con Z is asked to help locate the tomb of Ikanto, which is hurtling through space towards a black hole. It’s a strange job offer – especially when it’s made by a floating brain in a jar. But Z is desperate and up for the challenge. This is an irreverent sort of caper, according to its publisher, and comparable to Guardians of the Galaxy – no bad thing in my eyes.

Deadlock by Chloe Gong

Humans have moved to virtual reality to escape a world that is falling apart, in the second dystopian novel in The Strangeloom Trilogy. We follow those working against megacorporation NileCorp to bring it down. Sounds like some cyberpunky, futuristic fun.
