How to Thaw the Frozen Housing Market

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Today’s housing market is painful for just about everyone involved, from builders to buyers to sellers. Government failures over the last seven years froze the industry and made homeownership less affordable, but the problem could be solved if Congress would act. 

To understand the solution, though, we first must know how we got here.

Beginning in 2020, Congress increased annual spending by trillions of dollars, and the Federal Reserve simply created the money to cover the massive deficits. The Fed also pushed interest rates to near zero and bought mortgage-backed securities (MBS).

The low interest rates made it cheaper for the government to borrow, but also for everyone else to borrow, including would-be homebuyers. Lower mortgage rates allowed people to take on much larger mortgages without making monthly mortgage payments prohibitively expensive. The resulting bidding wars sent home prices to stratospheric levels.

Fed purchases of MBS were fuel on the fire. They incentivized banks to make more loans at lower interest rates, stimulating mortgage lending. That’s precisely why the Fed bought MBS for the first time in the wake of the mortgage meltdown in 2009—it was attempting to rescue the industry. This time, the Fed bought MBS from early 2020 through early 2022.

The result was even larger mortgages from even lower interest rates as home prices went to the moon. Additionally, the runaway spending, borrowing, and printing of money by the government devalued the dollar and sent inflation to 40-year highs. The weaker dollar also meant it took more of those dollars to buy the same home, further inflating prices, but it also caused the Fed to change course.

>>> Government Broke the Housing Market—Only This Will Fix It

What followed was the sharpest rise in interest rates in four decades. Suddenly, mortgage rates were three or four times their previous levels. Ordinarily, higher interest rates would put downward pressure on home prices so that monthly mortgage payments stay roughly the same, but that didn’t happen.

If you bought a home in 2020 with a 2 percent interest rate mortgage, you effectively can’t sell your home today, not without downsizing. If you sell today, your old mortgage gets paid off and your next mortgage will have an interest rate closer to 8 percent. You can’t borrow anywhere near as much on the next home, or your monthly mortgage payment will explode.

These golden handcuffs have kept many existing homes off the market and even those that are for sale have unrealistically high prices. Sellers want those high prices so they’ll have more money to put down on their next home and therefore need to borrow less. That makes the higher interest rate much less painful and keeps the monthly mortgage payment manageable.

But that’s left a massive mismatch between buyers and sellers. Would-be buyers can’t afford today’s high prices with today’s high interest rates. Homeownership affordability is near an all-time low because it takes 44% of the median household pre-tax income to afford a median-price home. The after-tax figure is well over 50 percent.

Consequently, pending sales of existing homes have fallen to the lowest level on record, a full one-third below the level from a quarter century ago.

>>> U.S. Government-Backed Homebuying Market Drives Up House Prices for Ordinary Americans  

The situation is painful for new homes too. Residential construction is trending down, and homebuilder sentiment has fallen to the lowest level in 14 years. Builders are being squeezed by costs 60% higher than before the pandemic, and buyers who can’t afford today’s prices. About two-thirds of builders are using sales incentives to clear inventory, with more than 40 percent cutting prices.

For transactions to increase and the housing market to clear, home prices ultimately need to come down relative to incomes, which will take time because sellers need to adjust their expectations. But lower interest rates would also give buyers some relief and reduce the lock-in effect on current homeowners looking to sell. 

That’s where Congress needs to act. The legislature has no direct control over interest rates, but they do control spending, which is at obscene levels. In the last fiscal year, which just ended on September 30, the federal government spent $7.4 trillion and had to borrow almost $2 trillion of it.

Everyone, including the government, competes for loans and that additional $2 trillion of competition drives up the price to borrow money, which is the interest rate. Reducing government spending would reduce the deficit and immediately put downward pressure on interest rates, including for mortgages.

Granted, other recent factors have contributed to higher interest rates—like the Iran war increasing oil prices, which increase inflation expectations, which increase interest rates. Ending that conflict would help stabilize energy markets and eventually reduce interest rates too. But it’s the spending that’s squarely within Congress’s control and which would have a faster impact.

But given the abundance of ineptitude and lack of fortitude within the halls of Congress, don’t expect those spending cuts anytime soon.

Your Robot’s Safety Functions Already Work. What If the Input Lies?

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →


This article is brought to you by VicOne.

A mobile robot slows for someone in a hallway. A collaborative arm eases its movement as a worker approaches. A humanoid pauses to let a person pass. Each response depends on information about the robot or its surroundings: a distance reading, a position estimate, or a stop signal.

But what happens when one of those inputs is wrong, and the robot still responds exactly as designed? The mobile robot reads the hallway as clear while someone is still there. The arm estimates that the worker is farther away. The humanoid does not receive a stop signal as a person crosses its path.

In each case, a fault could cause that mismatch. So could deliberate manipulation.

At VicOne LAB R7, we examine how manipulated inputs can change robot behavior — and what that means for the systems meant to keep people safe.

How an untrusted input can change a robot’s behavior

A robot does more than measure its surroundings. It may interpret what it sees and hears to decide what to do next. Information placed in its environment can therefore influence its actions.

Published research shows how this can happen. In a study of vision-language-action (VLA) models, a patch in a camera’s view reduced task success in simulated robot tests. FreezeVLA found that an adversarial image could cause tested models to ignore later instructions. Although the studies used different methods, both demonstrate how visual input can interfere with a robot’s intended task.

In a robot-dog test using Gemma 4 E4B, text on a poster was treated as an instruction and changed the robot’s movement.

VicOne LAB R7 also tested how untrusted inputs could change robot behavior. In a robot-dog test using Gemma 4 E4B, text on a poster was treated as an instruction and changed the robot’s movement. In a separate hospital-service-robot simulation using Nemotron on NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin, crafted audio that is inaudible to people changed the robot’s simulated behavior. The assigned tasks did not change; the inputs did.

In a VicOne LAB R7 test, text on a poster changed a robot dog’s movement, illustrating how a crafted input can turn an unlikely outcome into a repeatable attack.VicOne

Safety-related decisions have inputs too

An independent protective system may still stop a dangerous action. But that system also depends on information: readings, estimates, and messages that tell it when to intervene. What happens if one of those inputs is manipulated?

At a robotics bug bounty event, VicOne LAB R7 researchers injected a ROS 2/DDS message into a robot that organizers expected to remain still under a safe-control setting. The robot moved.

A humanoid’s balance controller offers another example. If it relies on a vulnerable gyroscope, sound at the sensor’s resonant frequency could distort its reported rotation. The controller might correct for a tilt that never occurred. Researchers demonstrated the underlying acoustic attack against drones with vulnerable gyroscopes. The research did not demonstrate the same attack chain causing a humanoid to fall.

Other protections could interrupt either chain. The question for safety and security teams is whether those protections detect the danger independently or depend on the same manipulated information.

Figure 1. VicOne maps how accidental faults and deliberate cyber manipulation can lead to similar robot safety outcomes, from loss of control to physical harm.VicOne

Deliberate attacks challenge safety assumptions

A safety assessment may treat a combination of accidental faults as unlikely. Deliberate manipulation changes that assumption. An attacker can choose when to introduce a false input and repeat the same trigger. That does not make harm inevitable, but it may change how cyber risks should be assessed.

Redundant sensors also need an adversarial test. If one action can affect both inputs, their agreement may offer less reassurance than expected. In autonomous driving research, a crafted physical object misled a system combining camera and LiDAR perception. The study does not establish a weakness in a particular robot, but it shows why teams should test whether one manipulation can affect several checks at once.

Cyber threats add intent to the safety equation, so risk assessments based on accidental faults must be revisited to account for attacks that can be timed and repeated.

Security testing strengthens robot safety evidence

Safety teams set limits for speed, protective distances, and permitted operating areas. The evidence behind those limits should also show what happens when the information used to enforce them is deliberately manipulated.

That evidence can also support assessments against applicable requirements. China’s GB/T 45502-2025 addresses information security for service robots. IEC TS 63074 examines security threats that could affect safety-related control systems. The EU Machinery Regulation, which applies from January 20, 2027, includes requirements to protect safety-relevant systems and data against corruption. Each has its own scope, and teams still need evidence for their robot’s design and operating conditions.

Figure 2. Cybersecurity strengthens robot safety evidence throughout the lifecycle. VicOne helps teams test safety limits against manipulation before deployment and monitor the conditions behind safe behavior in operation.VicOne

Before deployment, safety and security engineers can compare normal and adversarial scenarios against the same safety limits. During operation, they can monitor for changes to software, models, sensor signals, or behavior that may call earlier results into question. Any response to suspicious behaviors should follow policies approved by the safety team.

VicOne supports that work across the robot lifecycle. Through the Robotic Hacking Community, VicOne LAB R7 works with researchers to investigate how cyber threats can change robot behavior. VicOne’s Radeis helps teams validate potential effects before deployment, while Rthena provides visibility into risks and behavior during operation.

Five questions safety and security teams should answer together

The central test is whether a robot’s safeguards still protect people when the information they rely on is manipulated. Safety and security teams can begin by tracing the inputs behind each protective decision, challenging them, and revisiting the evidence as the robot changes. They can start with these five questions:

1. What does each protective function read? Map the readings, estimates, messages, and confirmations. Identify which safeguards operate independently of the robot’s AI decisions.

2. How does that information arrive? Check how inputs are produced, transmitted, authenticated where appropriate, and handled when they are missing or implausible.

3. What happens if an input is manipulated? Test false distances, drifting position estimates, and other relevant scenarios against the robot’s safety limits.

4. Can one action mislead several inputs? Check whether sensor fusion or seemingly independent safeguards share a point of failure.

5. When should the evidence be revisited? Reassess after changes to software, models, sensors, or operating conditions. Agree on bounded responses to suspicious behavior with the safety team in advance.

The goal is not only to show that a protective function works. Teams also need evidence that it remains protective when the information behind its decision is wrong or deliberately manipulated — and that another safeguard can catch the resulting hazard when needed. As robots change, that evidence needs to change with them.

Creators who show their sources build trust, survey finds

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

About one in five U.S. adults regularly gets their news from news creators on social media platforms, but it can be difficult for audiences to suss out which personalities to trust amid an avalanche of misinformation, hot takes, and polarizing content.

New research spearheaded by a team of journalists and political scientists shows that giving audiences a peek into the reporting process can help bolster their trust in the news.

Proof News, a nonprofit newsroom focused on tech accountability, in collaboration with researchers at the French university Sciences Po, shared social news videos with 2,000 U.S. adults to measure their perceptions of trustworthiness. Participants were randomly assigned one of Proof’s yet-to-be-published short-form news videos.

Some participants viewed videos with labels describing the sources, others saw videos with labels describing the newsroom’s ethical standards, and some saw videos without a label. The Sources labels appeared on cards embedded at the end of the video, like a credits reel, or were placed into the video description, the text accompanying the video.

[img wide]When viewing a short-form news video, survey respondents saw a label noting sources, newsroom standards, or no label at all.[/img]

Participants then rated the videos on various trust metrics. We found that labeling videos did increase trust — particularly a label that described the video sources in the description and, to a slightly lesser extent, a label describing newsroom standards in the credits.

This effect was particularly prominent among survey respondents who identified as Democrats or reported having high trust in science. Those groups, when shown the sourcing label in the description of a general news video, reported higher assessments of transparency and trust in the journalist.

There were fewer statistically significant increases in trust metrics with labels for text articles, but Sally Lehrman, founder of The Trust Project, said the findings were still intriguing.

“I’m excited to see the results,” Lehrman said. “Methods and references really do resonate…People are asking, where did the journalist get this information, and how can I trust it.”

[img wide][/img]

Lack of standards

As audiences increasingly turn to social media for news, there is a growing public discussion about how trustworthy creators are, especially when it comes to disclosing their funding and relationships. In January, The Wall Street Journal wrote about corporate and foreign interests using influencers to push agendas. In August, The Washington Post reported that the Pentagon secretly hired conservative military veterans with large online audiences to amplify Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s messaging, and a New York Times investigation revealed online influencers received funding from political campaigns without disclosing the connection in their videos.

Some creators have begun trying to distinguish themselves from this type of behavior through their own disclosure efforts.

Journalist and creator Dave Jorgenson, who has nearly 1 million followers across TikTok, YouTube, and Instagram, has started publishing a transparency report on his website, Local News International, revealing his sources of funding alongside his editorial standards.

Ultimately, he thinks disclosures of political funding should no longer be voluntary but mandatory. “Platforms should be regulating themselves,” Jorgenson said in an interview. “You can’t really force that unless there are laws.”

Proof used Jorgenson’s ethics and transparency statement as a model for the newsroom standards label in video testing and found that it increased viewers’ trust in the news outlet and the likelihood that they would share a general news story.

New York City is already considering disclosure requirements in the wake of revelations in the Columbia Journalism Review that exposed the inner workings of New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s connections with influencers. The author of the article, Anya Schiffrin, a senior lecturer at Columbia University’s School of International and Public Affairs, testified at a September city council meeting that creator relationships — both paid and unpaid — should be disclosed to viewers.

“It’s important for democracy that we have standards,” Schiffrin said.

Proof for a new model

Proof News, which launched in 2024, pioneered an Ingredients label that takes cues from the scientific method and names the journalists’ hypothesis, sources, limitations, and the story’s findings.

To test whether the label worked to increase viewer trust for short-form vertical videos, we stripped down the Ingredients label to a bullet list of sources followed by an asterisk denoting the reporting limitations. The survey showed the label had a consistent positive effect when placed in the video description, the text box that is often located under the video.

The team also tested how labels performed on online text news articles. The survey showed 1,600 U.S. participants original, unpublished Proof News stories without a label, with a Proof Ingredients label, a label modeled after the Trust Project that described the reporting process, and a label describing newsroom standards.

[img wide]Journalists designed three different labels: one about the newsroom’s standards, one about the reporting process, and one inspired by the scientific method, stating a hypothesis, sources, limitations, and findings.[/img]

There were fewer statistically significant increases in trust metrics with the print labels. The most significant findings were an increase in perception of transparency when the reporting process label was used on a polarizing story and an investigative story.

One possible explanation for the differing results between labeling video and text articles is the “ergonomics” of the medium, said Damien Bol, a professor at the Center for Political Research at Sciences Po who helped lead the survey design and data analysis.

“When reading a text, for example, people can quickly skim through it or skip parts of it, whereas a video forces them to follow the story second by second, even when watching at double speed,” Bol said. Different formats, Bol said, shape the “experience of consuming a news story.”

Influencing the influential

Earlier this month, Julia Angwin, an author of this article, hosted a private convening of creators at Harvard Kennedy School’s Shorenstein Center on Media, Politics and Public Policy to discuss creating shared standards that could be conveyed through a label or other public statement.

The group of 30 leading content creators from news, science communication, education, history, and other evidence-based fields discussed how they could distinguish their work by signaling to viewers their commitment to standards that could include disclosing funding sources and the use of artificial intelligence. Creator-led efforts to bring this to fruition are ongoing.

San Diego-based journalist Lisa Remillard, who has more than 4 million followers on her TikTok channel “The News Girl,” said that making videos with verified facts can feel like a small club. But if she can’t independently confirm something, she’s “not putting that out there.”

Remillard’s audience appears to appreciate transparency. Earlier this year, she published a video describing her editorial standards — and was richly rewarded for it. “I think clearly defining those ethics and standards resonated with my audience and gave them a reason to want to financially support my work,” Remillard said.

Jessica Yellin, the founder of the political podcast, YouTube channel, and social media brand “News Not Noise,” has also called for news creators to voluntarily sign a Creator Constitution, agreeing to journalistic standards such as citing sources, fixing errors, and disclosing financial relationships.

She said labeling sources is about the “signal.” Even if audiences don’t take the time to digest every word, Yellin observed, “It’s the act of showing it that matters.”

U.S. home prices continued to lose ground to inflation in August, even as nominal price growth accelerated slightly, according to the latest S&P Cotality Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price NSA Index.

The National Index posted a 2.1% annual gain in August, up from 1.9% in July. Consumer prices, meanwhile, increased 3.4% year over year, meaning inflation outpaced home price growth by roughly 1.3 percentage points. It marked the 15th consecutive month that U.S. home values declined in real terms.

The 10-City Composite recorded a 3.6% annual increase, up from 3.4% the previous month, while the 20-City Composite increased 2.7%, compared with 2.5% in July.

“U.S. home prices declined in real terms for the 15th consecutive month in August 2026, though the gap narrowed as nominal home price growth strengthened,” says Rebecca Kaufman, associate director of commodities at S&P Dow Jones Indices.

Regional differences remained pronounced. Chicago led the 20 markets for the sixth consecutive month, posting a 6.7% year-over-year gain, followed by New York at 6.2% and Cleveland at 4.3%.

At the opposite end, Seattle recorded the largest annual decline for the third consecutive month, falling 2%. Las Vegas prices declined 1.3%, while Denver prices fell 0.8%. The gap between Chicago and Seattle approached 9 percentage points, highlighting the continued geographic divergence in home-price performance.

On a month-over-month basis, prices softened before seasonal adjustment. The U.S. National Index declined 0.2%, while both the 10-City and 20-City composites fell 0.4%.

After seasonal adjustment, however, each index increased. The National Index rose 0.3%, the 10-City Composite increased 0.4%, and the 20-City Composite gained 0.3%.

“The divergence suggests that the typical post-summer slowdown is obscuring some underlying strength in home prices,” Kaufman says.

Despite that strength, affordability remains a significant hurdle for prospective buyers. Kaufman pointed to 3.4% inflation, Brent crude near $100 per barrel, and mortgage rates in the mid-to-high 6% range as factors keeping household costs elevated alongside persistently high home prices.
