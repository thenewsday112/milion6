Justices to debate when an established rule is “previously unavailable” in habeas case

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

The Supreme Court will hear oral argument on Nov. 4 in Guerrero v. Johnson, a case about the limited circumstances in which a state prisoner can return to federal court for a second round of post-conviction review.

But let’s back up a bit: If inmates want to challenge the grounds for their detention, they must typically bring a habeas claim. The Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996, seeking to curb the number of such petitions, made it significantly more difficult to bring successive applications. Specifically, under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2) of AEDPA, a new claim in a second federal habeas petition is barred unless it falls under one of two exceptions. The first is when such a claim “relies on a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable.” The second exception covers claims based on “the facts underlying the [initial] claim,” but only if those facts would show “by clear and convincing evidence that, but for constitutional error, no reasonable factfinder would have found the applicant guilty of the underlying offense.”

Texas inmate Dexter Johnson brought his claim under the first exception, contending that he cannot be put to death based on the court’s 2002 ruling in Atkins v. Virginia, which held that executing people with intellectual disabilities violates the Eighth Amendment’s ban on cruel and unusual punishment. Johnson did not present the Atkins claim in his initial petitions but, after qualifying as intellectually disabled under revised diagnostic standards, raised this in a subsequent petition. The question before the justices is whether Johnson’s Atkins claim was “previously unavailable” to him.

Some background

In 2007, Johnson was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas for his role in the double murder of 23-year-old Maria Aparece and 17-year-old Huy Ngo. At trial, a defense expert testified that Johnson’s IQ was between 74 and 88 and he “did not have obvious deficits” with regard to “personal independence and social responsibility when compared to other people of similar age and sociocultural background.” Under the diagnostic standards in place at that time, the expert concluded that Johnson was not intellectually disabled. (Johnson did not raise an Atkins claim in his initial habeas petitions but invoked Atkins in those petitions to argue – unsuccessfully – that its protections should extend to people with mental illness.)

In May 2013, while Johnson’s federal petition was pending, the American Psychiatric Association published the fifth edition of its diagnostic manual, known as the DSM-5, which Texas’ highest criminal court adopted in 2018. The new edition de-emphasized IQ cutoffs and recognized that someone whose score is above 70 may still be intellectually disabled. In April 2019, an expert gave Johnson an IQ test on which he scored 70, and a second expert agreed that Johnson met the DSM-5’s criteria for intellectual disability.

Johnson filed a successive habeas application in the district court, in which he claimed that he could not be executed under Atkins. After an evidentiary hearing, the trial court determined that Johnson’s Atkins claim was previously unavailable and consequently stayed his execution. The state appealed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 5th Circuit, which in July 2025 affirmed the district court’s decision. The full 5th Circuit then declined to rehear the case by a vote of 9-7. In February, the state asked the Supreme Court to weigh in, which the Supreme Court agreed in June to do.

The parties’ arguments

In its brief, the Office of the Texas Attorney General maintains that “Johnson’s Atkins claim does not rely on a change in the law.” Instead, it emphasizes, Atkins was decided well before Johnson filed his initial habeas application, and “before Johnson even committed the capital murder for which he was sentenced to death.” Nor, according to the Texas AG’s office, would permitting Johnson to bring such a claim serve the purpose of enabling successive petitions, which is to allow for new evidence as to whether a person is “guilty of the underlying offense.”

The state further presses the point that no “rule” was previously unavailable here. “An object—or a rule—can be available even when it is not needed or useful. A carpenter, for example, likely has a hammer ‘available,’ even when working on a task for which a hammer would not be necessary or useful,” the state writes. The publication of the DSM-5 may have allowed Johnson to discover a new factual predicate, according to the state, but it does not change the availability of any rule. Instead, the 5th Circuit’s approach “erases the line Congress drew between new law and new facts and effectively writes § 2244(b)(2)(B)’s limitations out of the statute.”

Johnson’s attorneys counter that something is “available” only if it is “capable of being used by a person, for a purpose”; prior to the publication of the DSM-5, no such usage was possible. “No one would say the hammer was available to the carpenter if it were locked in a toolbox he couldn’t open,” Johnson’s team writes. “Mr. Johnson did not change; his intellectual and adaptive characteristics are the same as always. What changed was the framework that made Atkins capable of protecting him. Atkins was not available to him before; now it is.” Nor, contrary to the AG’s claim otherwise, Johnson’s lawyers argue, would this open the “floodgates” to many other such challenges. Because very few rules are retroactive, such issues rarely arise.

Perhaps unsurprisingly given its stance on capital punishment, the Trump administration filed a “friend of the court” brief supporting Texas. Echoing several of the state’s arguments, U.S. Solicitor General D. John Sauer asserts that “[s]tandards promulgated by a board of psychiatrists cannot transform a decision of this Court that predates a prior habeas application into a ‘new’ and ‘previously unavailable’ rule of constitutional law.” Louisiana and 21 other states, also supporting Texas, call the 5th Circuit’s rule “a recipe for eternal Atkins claims and zero finality,” pointing out that a professional association also moved the age by which intellectual disability must appear from 18 to 22 in 2021 – raising the possibility that such age-based claims could also be brought and questioning the possibility that the habeas floodgates would remain closed were the court to agree with Johnson.

On Johnson’s side, the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers compares Johnson’s claim to second petitions based on new science about “microscopic hair comparison analysis, arson science, and shaken baby syndrome.” According to the NACDL, “claims of constitutional error grounded in faulty science in a second or successive petition” must be allowed to go forward because “flawed analytical methods may not be debunked until” it is too late.

One final thing worth mentioning: Last term, after hearing argument in Hamm v. Smith, a dispute over how courts should weigh multiple IQ scores in Atkins cases, the justices dismissed the case as improvidently granted (known as a “DIG”) – that is, they decided that it was a mistake to have agreed to hear the case in the first place and did not resolve it. At the end of his brief, Johnson asks the court to do the same in his case on the ground that the state has continually changed its arguments. Whatever the merits of Johnson’s position on that front, it seems unlikely the court will DIG two Atkins cases in successive terms.

The decision in Guerrero v. Johnson is expected by late June or early July.

10 of the Most Iconic Patrick Stewart Roles Beyond Captain Picard

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

We may earn a commission from links on this page.

Sir Patrick Stewart, an actor equally at home onstage at the Royal Shakespeare Company and on the bridge of the Starship Enterprise, has died at the age of 86. Unexpectedly for most of us, in part because he kept his final illness private, but more so because it once seemed as though nothing could fell the man who looms so large in our cultural consciousness.

The thoughtful, erudite performer who had a troubled childhood in working-class Yorkshire; the committed socialist who was eventually knighted by Queen Elizabeth; the advocate who put his money and his considerable voice toward the fights for LGBTQ+ acceptance and animal welfare and against domestic violence, his presence and performances meant the world (the galaxy, even) to myself and so many others. But those who knew him personally can offer better tributes; perhaps it's best left to Shakespeare's Player King, performed by Stewart himself opposite David Warner's Hamlet back in 1965:

Where joy most revels, grief doth most lament;Grief joys, joy grieves, on slender accident.

Much of Stewart's finest work was on the stage; other performances remain rather shockingly hard to come by (whither I, Claudius?). Nonetheless, he left a true legacy behind him, well beyond Captain Picard and Professor X. Here are 10 of his most iconic other roles.

Jeffrey (1995)

A goofy, feel-good gay rom-com with the HIV/AIDS crisis still very much in the foreground, Jeffrey was a near-impossibility in the mid-1990s: a movie that neither villainized its queers nor treated them as pitiable martyrs. Steven Weber and Michael T. Weiss are the leads, joined by a cast that includes Christine Baranski, Victor Garber, Nathan Lane, Olympia Dukakis, and Sigourney Weaver but, for all those names, Stewart is the undeniable standout as Sterling, the irrepressible gay best friend: a swishing, mincing, sassy, high-camp character who steals every scene without ever becoming a caricature. He's the leader of the "Pink Panthers," a squad of queers who defend locals from gay bashers, and that is the key to the character's power: He's as gay as they come, but not ashamed and not even a little weak. This movie, and the presence of Captain Picard himself in it, might have saved my life back in 1995. Stream Jeffrey on Prime Video, Tubi, and The Roku Channel.

A sprawling, epic, and appropriately lusty take on the King Arthur stories from John Boorman, Excalibur is a mixed bag full of stunning visuals and iconic moments that never quite coalesce into something entirely coherent. Standing out among the film's many characters, Stewart is entirely memorable as Leodegrance, the boisterous, boorish, but ultimately faithful father of Guenevere who is among the first to pledge his loyalty to Arthur. Patrick Stewart's role as Professor Xavier in the X-Men is almost as pop-culture iconic as that of Captain Picard, spanning 26 years and nine movies, including the forthcoming Avengers: Doomsday. As a cinematic actor, Stewart's greatest strength was bringing humanity to larger-than-life, even operatic roles, whether they be Arthurian knights, Dickensian villains, or superheroes. Vietnamese-American Photographer and Oberlin Professor Pipo Nguyen-duy Found Dead at 64

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Pipo Nguyen-duy, a professor at Ohio’s Oberlin College and Conservatory and a recipient of the Guggenheim Fellowship in photography, was found dead at 64 in an off-campus home on Wednesday.

ABC News reports that Nguyen-duy’s son, Daniel Jones (formerly known as Asia Nguyen-duy), has been charged in connection with a double homicide for the murder of his father and stepmother, Tran Thi Phong. Nguyen-duy and Phong were found fatally shot by local police.

“The male victim was discovered on the first floor of the residence, and the female victim was discovered on the second floor. Both victims suffered from multiple gunshot wounds,” police said in a statement on Thursday. The victims’ bodies were discovered on Wednesday.

Jones appeared in court on Thursday afternoon in handcuffs and ankle shackles. He was ordered to be held in jail, and a bail was set at $3 million. Jones was arrested on Wednesday without incident after an Oberlin resident called police to report a “wet disheveled man” at her front door. About 90 minutes before the bodies were found, police officers were called to an Oberlin College campus parking lot to support firefighters extinguishing a fire in a vehicle that was registered to Jones.

In court, prosecutor Zachary Simonoff said that Jones asked the resident to record a video of him to post online. “He told her that he did not want the police involved. In the video, he identifies himself as Asia Nguyen-duy, says he’s escaping a cult. He also says, ‘Everything I did was on my own accord and I take full responsibility,’” said Simonoff in court. “Officers went there and found two people dead apparently from gunshot wounds with blood throughout the house.” In court, Simonoff said that Jones told officers that he “killed his family” when they first spoke to him and gave the officers his father’s address.

Simonoff added that police have contacted Jones’s mother in Oregon. She told them that her son is psychotic and needs medication. A motive for the shooting remains under investigation.

The weapon allegedly used in the shooting has not been found, according to the prosecutor.

Pipo Nguyen-duy had been a professor of studio art and photography at Oberlin College since 1998. “Tragedies such as this one intrude upon our lives in jarring ways. I am simply overwhelmed with sadness, and I am deeply concerned about the impact of the devastating loss of Pipo to our community,” said Oberlin College President Carmen Twillie Ambar in a statement on Wednesday addressed to students and colleagues. “Pipo was beloved. During his years at Oberlin, he touched all of us through his passion for photography. Our community will be hurting for a very long time, and when the shock of this news subsides, we will all need to process our grief. This process will sometimes be together and sometimes alone, but we will need to take the time to do so.”

Nguyen-duy had immigrated from Vietnam in 1975 as a political refugee and went on to become an internationally exhibited visual artist. He took up photography in his late 20s and pursued it academically, receiving his MA and MFA from the University of New Mexico at Albuquerque. “In his wide-ranging body of work, Nguyen-duy considered immigration and emigration, intimacy, abandonment and decay, cultural assimilation, and the cost of war,” said a tribute post from the Guggenheim Foundation on Instagram. “And he touched people all over the world with his probing, compassionate work.”

In 2023, Nguyen-duy participated in the 15th Sharjah Biennial, exhibiting photographs of schoolchildren playing in what looks like the Vietnam countryside, with some napping on felled trees while others have assault rifles tucked under their arms.

When a winter storm dumps a thick white blanket of the white stuff on your property, a snow blower or snow thrower can be your best friend. But which one do you need? Many often use the two terms interchangeably to describe snow removing power tools, but snow blowers and snow throwers are actually two distinct types of machines. While they serve the same purpose, they differ in size, power, snow removal ability, and price. Understanding the difference between the two is key to choosing a snow thrower vs snow blower to handle your winter snow clearing duties. Here’s what you need to know about. 

Are snow throwers and snow blowers the same machine? 

Snow throwers and snow blowers clear snow in the same way: Both have a spinning auger that lifts the snow and launches it through a chute as you guide the tool along a driveway or walkway. But while snow throwers do this in a single step, or stage, a snow blower uses multiple stages to move the snow. There’s also a size difference: Snow throwers are smaller, more nimble machines that can handle moderate snowfall on smaller driveways, while snow blowers are larger and capable of clearing deeper snow from bigger driveways. 

What is a snow thrower?

Photo: rabbitti via Adobe Stock

Snow throwers are ideal for removing small amounts of snow that are not extremely deep.They are single-stage machines that scoop up snow with an auger and launch snow out of a chute in a single action. Depending on the size of the snow thrower, they can clear a path that’s 18 to 24 inches wider and between 6 and 12 inches deep. Many can hurl it a distance of 15 to 20 feet, while some can send snow as far as 40 feet.

What is a snow blower?

Snow blowers are more powerful than snow throwers, making them a good choice for those who need to clear larger driveways or live in areas with harsher winters that receive higher snowfall totals. Like snow throwers, snow blowers have a rotating auger that scoops up snow, but they use two or three stages to clear snow. A three-stage model is even more powerful. How to Choose the Right Snow Removal Machine

Photo: Nikita Sobolkov via Adobe Stock

Choosing between a snow thrower and snow blower comes down to several factors, including snowfall totals, driveway type and size, ease of use, and price. 

Snowfall in your area

The average snowfall totals of the winter storms passing through your area are a big factor in determining whether to go with a snow thrower or snow blower. If snowstorms routinely drop snowfall of more than a foot of snow that’s wetter and more compacted, you’ll need a snow blower’s power and capacity to clear it. If your snowstorms are typically under a foot and consist of fluffy to moderately heavy snow, you can take on winter with a snow blower.

Keep in mind that the depth a snow blower or snow thrower can handle can vary from model to model, so make sure to check the manufacturer’s ratings. Some lighter-duty snow throwers max out at 6 inches, while there are compact snow blowers that might only be able to handle up to a foot of snow. 

Type of driveway

The type of driveway you’re clearing determines the type of snow removal machine you need. If you’re clearing a gravel driveway, don’t use a snow thrower. Since snow throwers have paddles that scrape across the ground, they can pick up and throw gravel, damage the auger, and send the gravel into your yard.

Snowblowers, in comparison, have skid shoes that prevent the auger housing from coming into contact with the ground, making them capable of clearing gravel driveways. But unlike snow throwers, which clear snow down to the pavement, snow blowers leave a thin layer of snow–about 1/8 of an inch–on smooth pavement. 

Size of area to be cleared

The size of your driveway largely determines whether a single-stage snow thrower or multi-stage snow blower is the best choice. Snow throwers can cut a swath through snow that’s 18 to 24 inches wide, which makes them best suited for the typical two-car driveway.  Snow blowers have wider clearing widths of up to 32 inches, so they can cover significantly more ground than a snow thrower with each pass. They are a better choice for larger driveways. 

Throwing distance is also an important factor when deciding between a snow blower and snow thrower. Most snow throwers can launch snow a distance of 15 to 25 feet away, though there are some single stage models that can throw snow up to 45 feet. A snow blower’s multi-stage design allows them to launch snow further, typically up to 50 feet, which is useful when clearing wider driveways or large patios. 

With their ability to remove a wider swath of snow with each pass and launch it further, snow blowers are best suited for handling long winding driveways or larger driveways that hold three or more vehicles. While snow throwers have a narrower clearing width, their small size makes them easier to turn and maneuver, allowing you to clear 4 to 8 inches of snow over a smaller one- to two-car driveways. 

However, these are recommendations. You can certainly use a snow thrower to tackle a larger driveway. Just plan on spending more time out in the cold to get the job done. Likewise, you can use a larger snow blower on a small driveway, but it may end up taking you longer. These larger machines require more room to turn and are more difficult to maneuver around smaller driveways. 

Ease of use

Photo: Tatiana Kuklina via Adobe Stock

Snow throwers are smaller and lighter, which generally makes them easier to maneuver, especially in tight areas. The controls on a snow thrower are also relatively simple, so there’s less of a learning curve when operating them. 

Snow blowers are much heavier but are usually self-propelled and have power steering, so there’s less physical effort to operate them than a snow thrower. However, they are very difficult to move when the engine is off, which makes it more difficult to get in and out of storage. 

A snow blower usually has more controls on the handlebars than a snow thrower, so expect a longer learning curve when learning how to use a snow blower. 

Power

Both snow throwers and snow blowers come in battery-powered and gas-powered versions. Cordless electric snow blowers are easier to maintain and start than gas models, operate with less vibration, and don’t produce exhaust. While gas-powered machines were once prevalent, today’s cordless electric models are comparable in clearing width, throwing distance, and snow capacity. That said, gas-powered machines still dominate when it comes to runtime. While it takes minutes to refill a gas tank, it can take hours to recharge a battery. 

Price

Snow throwers generally cost less than snow blowers. The transmission, large engines, and self-propelled system on these machines adds to their expense. Single-stage snow throwers are simpler machines, so they cost less. Snow throwers generally cost $500 to $1,000, while snow blowers can range between $800 and $2,000, with battery-powered snow blowers typically costing a couple hundred dollars more.

A Writer’s Night at America’s Most Haunted Hotel

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Book tours have taught me to expect the unexpected. There was the couple in Texas who kindly housed me but warned about their vicious late-night, door-opening, biting cat, and the time a prominent politician’s son asked me to edit his “fictional novel,” a thinly-veiled story about his father’s real-life exploits. But nothing prepared me for what happened in Eureka Springs, Arkansas.

I’d recently met the fabulous writer Stephanie Storey while she was touring her beautiful debut novel Oil and Marble. Stephanie grew up in Arkansas, and I’m pretty sure she told the director of the literary festival, Books in Bloom, about me. I received an invitation to be a featured author at the festival in the spring of 2018. I’d never been to Arkansas and was excited to experience both the town and this celebration of literature. My communication from the director stated I’d be staying at the Crescent, a historic hotel founded in 1866. The town was as adorable and welcoming as advertised, a Victorian village with rumored healing springs, and everyone I met exuded warmth and hospitality. The thriving literary community radiated kindness and engagement.

My room was comfy and oddly shaped, the kind of space that had clearly remained much as it was in the 1800s. The quirks of the layout only added to its charm. It wasn’t until I checked in that day that I noticed a sign proclaiming the hotel to be “America’s Most Haunted Hotel.” I’m not easily spooked, but I do have a healthy curiosity and respect for possibilities.

That evening, after going out for dinner with several other authors from the festival, I took the stairs rather than the elevator to get some exercise as I made my way back to my room. When I reached my floor, I noticed a throng of people blocking the hallway. As I got closer, I realized they were listening with rapt attention to a hotel employee leading them on the nightly ghost tour. And then it hit me: they were standing directly outside my room. The tour guide was calling my room the most haunted room in the hotel.

I looked around, half-expecting someone to jump out and shout, Gotcha! You shoulda seen your face! But no. I eased closer to listen, catching fragments of the guide’s narrative: something about a secret pregnancy, a woman who had mysteriously fallen—or perhaps jumped—from the window of the very room where I’d be sleeping that night. The group murmured, a young girl chewed her nail, a man snapped a photo of my door, and then they moved on down the hall, leaving me standing there. I said aloud to no one, Are you kidding me?

That night, I settled into bed, read for a while, and then turned off the lamp. I wasn’t particularly concerned about the ghost tour’s commentary. After all, the guide was doing his job, creating atmosphere and mystery to justify the tour fee. My room was cozy and funky, and I liked the bed. I nestled into the sheets, closed my eyes, and drifted off.

Then, almost immediately, I sensed a cat hopping onto the bed. Now, I know that sounds wacky. And I can’t exactly articulate the sensation. Still, if you’ve ever had a cat, you know that particular feeling—the gentle padding of paws, the slight shifting of the comforter, barely perceptible but unmistakable. This felt so real, so present, that I jumped up and turned on the lamp, sending my reading glasses flying off the nightstand. There was no cat on my bed. I looked underneath, checked the bathroom, searched the corners of the room, all the while calling Here, kitty kitty, my suspension of disbelief tested. Absolutely nothing.

I got back into bed, turned off the light, and lay there, mind racing, acutely aware of every shadow and sound. I thought of my youngest child, a seventh grader, back home in South Carolina. He had a big math test the next day. I turned the lamp back on and grabbed my phone to text him a good luck, good night text. Not eager to get back out of bed to retrieve my glasses, I squinted and pecked out my message.

“Night, buddy. Proud of you. You got this! Love you so much.”

I hit send, turned off the lamp, then, oddly enough, slept soundly. The next morning, I awoke early and absentmindedly picked up my glasses, only to realize, with great embarrassment, that my late-night text had not been to my son Will, but to my longtime OB-GYN, Dr. Robert. Mortified, I typed a quick explanation. I kept it simple: “Oops. Wrong recipient. Meant for my son.” He replied with a crying-laughing emoji. Thank goodness my gynecologist had a sense of humor.

I opened my laptop and prepped for my session at the festival later that morning. Sipping my coffee, curiosity got the better of me. On a whim, I googled “ghost cat of Crescent Hotel, Eureka Springs,” not really expecting to find anything.

Holy moly.

There was, in fact, a ghost cat at the Crescent Hotel—and it had a name: Morris. He was a real orange tabby who had lived at the hotel for twenty-one years, supposedly serving as its unofficial general manager. He had an office with his title on the door and a custom cat-door cut into the lobby so he could move freely through the hotel. When Morris died in 1994, over three hundred people attended his funeral. He was buried on the hotel’s East Lawn with a headstone.

Are you kidding me!

According to what I found, Morris apparently loved his job too much to leave. Guests and staff continued to report his presence—a cat brushing past ankles, faint meows in empty hallways, and, you guessed it: a gentle weight on the bed.

I hadn’t experienced the ghost of a tragic woman in the most haunted room, but I may have experienced Morris.

As I left the hotel that morning to start my day at the festival, I stepped outside and visited Morris’s grave. Standing there, I thought about what it means to be a writer—to remain open to possibilities, to imagine. Morris’s late-night visit had broadened my lens. And as unexpected situations tend to do, the experience made me consider a new what-if. A cat so devoted to his purpose that he transcended death itself to continue his work? There was a story in that. 

There always is, if you’re willing to see it.

We’re in the Era of the $200 Cup of Coffee

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →
Sey | Sey

A version of this post originally appeared in Eater Today, which spotlights the freshest news and stories from across the food world every day. Subscribe now.

These days, Bushwick is perhaps best known for its graffiti art, ecuavolley, and queer bars; the Brooklyn neighborhood is famous for its family-owned taquerias, American-Italian pastry shops, and the hallowed grounds of the Girls episode where Shoshanna accidentally does meth and runs around pantsless at a warehouse rave. Situate yourself at the heart of its largest park, Maria Hernandez, and you’ll see a kaleidoscopic blend of ravers, Latino families, and the baklava guy.

Starting this week, it’s also home to a $200 cup of coffee. 

Bushwick’s Sey Coffee recently announced that it would be touring a rare Panamanian geisha from Finca Sophia, a coffee farm in Panama’s Chiriquí highlands. Sey scooped up the geisha at auction for $44,275, which comes to $6,325 per kilogram, or roughly $200 per cup. The geisha is billed as extraordinarily aromatic, floral, and juicy; Doug Cohen, Sey’s director of brand strategy, advises enjoying it with a clean palate, completely black, and taking your time with it. “As the coffee cools, different aspects of its character become apparent, and that evolution is part of the experience,” he says. 

Even among fourth-wave coffee shops where prices continue to climb higher, Sey’s $200 cup is an outlier. “Coffee as a premium product is relatively new,” says Cohen. “For so long, coffee has been treated as a commodity, and its value has been constrained by that perception.” He says that both before and right after COVID, folks were becoming more curious about coffee, and educating themselves on platforms like Youtube, Discord, and online forums about how to appreciate it and “understand just how high the ceiling of quality can be.” The arrival of the $200 cup of coffee from a cafe in a neighborhood at the epicenter of conversations about both culinary innovation and gentrification feels ripe for closer examination.

So, how did this coffee end up at this price? “We have been purchasing coffee from Finca Sophia for several years,” Cohen says. “This release really grew out of that existing relationship.” When Finca’s general manager told Cohen that the farm’s yields were down by approximately 95 percent, Cohen says a bigger question was raised: “What does it mean to be a good partner when the people you have built relationships with are facing a difficult year?” Buying up the geisha beans felt like a way to both support Sey’s producer, and further develop what Cohen calls “elevated coffee experiences.” 

In the 2010s, the artisanal coffeehouse wave washed over the country, and especially Brooklyn, with hipster gusto. Sey Coffee was an integral part of that boom. Initially called Lofted Coffee, it operated out of its founders’ Bushwick loft until it rebranded to Sey in 2017, and expanded operations to its present Bushwick location on Grattan Street. That space has become something of a local icon since; Sprudge called its aesthetic “shoebox crossed with an airplane hangar”; the minimal-industrial, cream-hued interiors hit a very Axel Vervoordt-with-a-cyber-sigil-tattoo note (positive). The shop’s cachet has drawn coffee lovers, artists, yuppies, and artist-yuppies; customers have snacked on the gluten-free pastries from L’imprimerie (and now, post-L’imprimerie closure, pastries from Smør), and often post up with their dogs thanks to the semi-indoor-outdoor layout. 

Sey is hoping to elevate the coffee shop model to something more premium, with a stated focus on ethics and sustainability. Thus far, it’s earned acclaim for those efforts. Food & Wine named it the best coffee shop in New York and received positive media attention for opting out of tipping, choosing instead to pay its staff up to more than 80 percent above the NYC hourly minimum wage; full-time baristas can make up to $31 per hour in addition to receiving benefits and a share of the cafe’s revenue. 

Increasingly, coffee is getting the same treatment as high-end dining and tasting experiences. There’s the omakase-style menu at NYC’s Driftaway Coffee, or San Francisco’s Komakase coffee tasting room, both of which steer coffee away from quick consumption and toward a world of degustation that appears to have more in common with pricier wine and spirit tastings. The expansion of “espresso house” chain Watch House stateside is also an indicator: The London-based specialty roaster expanded to a fourth location in New York City this summer, raising eyebrows for its $32 cup of coffee while also reportedly scouting for more locations in Austin, Chicago, and Miami. 

The emerging demand may be just much about luxury as it is about craft. I can’t help but wonder if the $200 cup of coffee is happening in Bushwick because the Girls-era Bushwickian has grown up and wants more exclusive experiences. Is this what happens when, as culture writer Raven Smith said on his new BBC podcast last week, the indie outgrows its sleaze? “For us, this is not about pushing prices higher for the sake of it,” Cohen says. “It is about challenging how we assign value to coffee in the first place. What is an extraordinary coffee actually worth?” 

As much as we may lament climbing prices for specialty coffee and espresso, why wouldn’t this happen to the category when so many other aspects of food and beverage — from cocktails to sushi to bread — are following the same trajectory? Higher consumer costs notwithstanding, the marketing of specialty beans does steer U.S. coffee culture away from its productivity-focused consumption and the work-optimized “coffee break.”

“Why do we accept that certain wines can command thousands of dollars, yet question whether coffee can have comparable value?” Cohen asks. It’s a fair question, albeit one that I’d answer with another: Once we acknowledge that coffee should command a higher price, who still gets to drink it?
