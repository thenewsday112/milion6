Southwest Denies A 787 Order. Remember Its Bag Promise?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Southwest says a widebody order has “no credibility.” Six months before it ended free bags, it said no work was underway. Denials from Dallas expire fast.

A Denial With A Short Shelf Life

On its October 22nd, 2026 earnings call, CEO, Bob Jordan, shot down reports of a widebody order as having “no credibility.” The reports came from a Reuters exclusive two weeks earlier, citing three unnamed sources who said Southwest was looking at Boeing 787s for a long-haul push, possibly leased or second-hand, with a launch window of roughly three years. At the time, Southwest’s only comment was that it had no news forthcoming on its fleet plans. We have heard this before.

His reasoning was mostly that it would require significant changes in the business model. Operating aircraft with more than 175 seats would send the airline back to its pilots, and the pilot contract likely opens next year. Another 737 variant would be “significantly easier” than a widebody. Long-haul flying would be upside “if we decide to pursue that.” My eyerolls are likely audible at this point.

We Have Heard This Before

I know how this goes because I have watched the free bag story (and every other unique characteristic that Southwest would “never” change) play out. In July 2024, Jordan told investors that bags flying free was the number one reason customers choose Southwest, behind only fare and schedule. On September 17, 2024, a Southwest spokesperson told The Points Guy there was “no work currently underway” to change the policy, and Jordan, pressed on the question, would only say never say never, as CBS reported.

Six months later, on March 11, 2025, Southwest ended the policy. The airline set fees of $35 for the first checked bag and $45 for the second on flights booked on or after May 28, 2025. Jordan’s explanation was that bags flying free was not part of the airline’s DNA, and that open seating was not either. What he should have said was that it wasn’t part of Elliott Management’s DNA and they were running the show – at least while they were still heavy investors. Analysts warned at the time that dropping the policy could damage the brand and turn Southwest into just another airline. It most certainly did that, but as reported in recent weeks, it has, in fact, helped the stock price and financials significantly.

But there is no demonstrable difference between Southwest and any of the top 5 US carriers except one, no long haul international service.

What Is Left That Makes Southwest Different

The bags went first, and assigned seating arrived on January 27, 2026, with eight boarding groups, four fare tiers and extra-legroom rows on every flight. Basic fares board last. Open seating, which was the other half of the cattle-call charm, was gone. This has all been covered over the last 18 months but it’s important preamble when considering the next logical step for the airline, and the claims it is denying. What remains uniquely Southwest is one fleet type, and a partially point-to-point map and a reputation for friendliness, and the fleet may be next. Jordan said Southwest is not building toward any one thing, but the addition of 11 lounges, a premium credit card, and first class tell a different story.

It’s a sea of sameness (to steal another industry trope.) Delta and United sell the same choices at similar prices, and Southwest sells them with a smaller map and limited international reach. Every concession after the bag fee was an admission that the discount model was not enough on its own, even though I personally believe that it was.

Why Global Is The Only Growth Left

The numbers explain why management keeps moving the goalposts. In the third quarter, revenue per available seat mile jumped a record 21.6%, but capacity fell 0.4% and unit costs excluding fuel rose 3.8%. Capacity for 2026 is up only about 1.2%. That mix tells me growth came from price and fees, not from flying more, but in order to keep up that frothy pace it will have to find new markets to enter.

Domestic markets are mature, and Southwest’s biggest rivals already own the best international partners. Southwest has partnerships with Icelandair, China Airlines, EVA Air and Turkish Airlines, and Jordan said international loyalty partnerships and partner redemption launch next spring (I can’t wait to see what the redemptions look like.) Partners only go so far, though. Will some EVA Air customers book connecting flights on Southwest for its customers, maybe, probably, but that’s not going to move the needle.

Alaska already saw this writing on the wall and made a move. The rest of the premium push doesn’t come close to working if Southwest customers still buy tickets to Rome on another carrier. Chicago Midway can technically accommodate a 787, as can Dallas Love, and at LAX and Baltimore that’s a very easy fit. But all of those markets have real competition from major carriers. Other Southwest hubs like Fort Lauderdale face serious challenges with nearby Miami, Houston Hobby against United at Intercontinental, Denver for both international carriers and United.

But Southwest wanted to assimilate the carrier to every other carrier so it has to compete with every other carrier too. And in that regard, I can’t see a lounge program as aggressive as 11 locations working without it. If the fleet was exclusively Airbus, the international case is easier with longer legs and a JetBlue-like model (though that’s not working out well for them at the moment and they already have premium cards, lounges, and international partners.)

Unless Southwest sees unprecedented growth from what it’s already doing – possible, but not probable – this is the last frontier. So why deny it? Doesn’t that indicate a lack of forward thinking?

Conclusion

I will take the other side of the denial. My prediction is that Southwest announces a widebody order or lease within eighteen months of the pilot contract opening, and that Jordan or his successor will explain it as a natural evolution of the brand. The pattern is set. Every sacred cow gets slain once the numbers demand it, and the statement of the day is always true until the day it is not. Travelers should treat Southwest’s promises the way they treat any airline’s, as information about the current quarter and not a commitment about the next one.

Impromptu Projects erects Bamboo Pagoda along Sai Van Lake

 

Sited on the shoreline of Sai Van Lake in Macau, Bamboo Pagoda is a 13-meter-tall temporary pavilion designed by local practice Impromptu Projects, led by Rita Machado and João Ó. The installation draws directly from Macau’s vernacular bamboo scaffolding, a building technique listed on the city’s Inventory of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2017, reinterpreting construction-grade craft into a contemporary waterfront pavilion for contemplation.

all images by Tang Kuok Hou, Gonçalo Lobo Pinheiro

 

 

OCTAGONAL SKELETON INTEGRATES TRANSLUCENT BAMBOO FABRIC SKIN

 

The design by Impromptu Projects studio references classical pagoda geometries and Taoist concepts of equilibrium, structured over an octagonal footprint that tapers as it ascends. Slender structural bamboo culms form the exposed load-bearing skeleton, bound using traditional scaffolding lashing methods. A semi-translucent skin of bamboo fabric wraps the framework, filtering natural light and diffusing the vertical profile. Grounding the structure, the floor surface is laid with composite panels manufactured by Macau-based firm Zence Object Technology (CHAZENCE), composed of recycled tea waste and discarded wood chips. Integrated solar panels power the project’s architectural spotlights off the municipal grid, combining intangible cultural heritage with circular material systems.

BMW Employs Professional Sniffers to Eliminate New Car Smell

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

That “new car smell” you love is actually a cocktail of chemicals, and BMW has a team of trained sniffers working to get rid of it. Why? It’s because that new-car smell comes from volatile organic compounds evaporating out of hundreds of synthetic parts in a freshly delivered vehicle.

BMW

How The Sniff Test Works

According to BMWBlog, the automaker’s “nose team” works alongside specialized labs to judge how cabin materials really smell. Odor labs have operated in Munich since 1999 and in Shenyang, China, since 2014. Materials go into heated chambers that mimic a hot cabin, and the air is analyzed for compounds, then judged by evaluators against reference standards. The checks come in three stages: single materials, larger parts such as seats and headliners, and finally the complete vehicle, since materials can smell different in combination, especially as temperatures rise. Nils Hesse, BMW’s VP of Product Sustainability, put it this way: “A car doesn’t exist in a lab, but with its occupants.”

What This Means For You

BMW

Unless you’re buying a BMW, it means nothing. But the science is interesting, and it claims that heat is the enemy. According to the working theory, summer sun raises cabin emissions, while overnight winter parking lowers them. BMW says newer microfiber materials cut emissions and avoid animal-derived proteins, which can cause odor as natural leather ages. Personally, I drive a W123 Mercedes, and the smell of old animal biologics in the upholstery is what sets the scene. In epic contrast, the Neue Klasse iX3 also gets a multi-stage Nano Filter to make sure you’re really only breathing the cleanest, crispest, and most premium air. As for the rest of us, if your car has a smell today, the usual advice applies: crack the windows and park in the shade. If you follow this advice with a new car, the compounds fade by roughly 20 percent a week, so the smell eases over the first few months.

Why Smell Matters

Some of those compounds, like benzene and formaldehyde, are classified as human carcinogens, though typical cabin levels aren’t considered a serious hazard. The U.S. has no federal rules for in-car air, while China requires chemical tests before sale. BMW’s pitch to new car buyers is that a cabin that smells like nothing is a cabin that’s working.

How to Turn an AI-Generated Track Into a Seamless Game Loop With FFmpeg, Godot, Unity, and Web Audio

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The short version: a generated track almost never loops on its own. To fix that, generate a steady instrumental and work from a lossless copy. Cut a whole number of bars on a downbeat, then crossfade the audio that follows the loop end into the loop start. Check the seam numerically and by ear, ship it as OGG or WAV (not MP3), and give the engine an intro plus a loop point. The rest of this post walks through each step with copy-paste commands.

The question that keeps coming up

On r/SunoAI, a thread titled "Loop music ?" (April 2026) collected the usual answers. One reply sums up the problem well:

"I've been trying to do this with some of my instrumentals, and it has been a struggle. The closest success I've had is adding in the style prompt to have 'outro matches intro for consistent loop' - but even then, the tonal quality and fade in/out can still be jarring." (u/KinkyHuggingJerk, r/SunoAI)

Game developers ask the same thing about music they wrote themselves. A February 2026 r/gamedev thread asks "How do I make music loop seamlessly when it has a slight tail of reverb", and the highest-voted reply is the classic answer: "Use two clips. One that starts the track and one that loops."

Prompting alone won't get you there. You need a small, repeatable editing step, and that's what this post covers.

Why generated tracks don't loop

Four separate things go wrong, and each has its own fix:

  1. Song form. Generators write intros, builds, and endings. If you loop the whole file, the ending crashes into the intro.
  2. The cut is not on the grid. If the loop isn't an exact number of bars long, the beat stumbles every time it wraps.
  3. Discontinuity and tails. If you cut in the middle of a waveform, you get a click. If you cut off a reverb or cymbal tail, the sound collapses audibly at the seam.
  4. The file format adds silence. MP3 encoders and decoders add delay and padding. The LAME technical FAQ describes a 528-sample decoder delay in standard decoders, plus an encoder delay on top. As Wikipedia's gapless playback article puts it, MP3 itself "defines no way to record the amount of delay or padding." Some encoders store it in a LAME tag, but only players that read that tag can remove it. Ogg Vorbis and Opus define the start and end exactly, so the padding is trimmed when the file is decoded.

Step 1: Generate something that wants to loop

Ask for material that stays in one place musically:

  • Instrumental only. Vocals have phrases with beginnings and endings, so they rarely survive looping.
  • A fixed tempo stated as a number ("100 BPM"), plus "steady groove, no tempo changes."
  • No dramatic ending. Leave out words like "finale," "build to a climax," or "fade out." If the tool has an exclusion field, put those there.
  • Longer than you need. A 2–3 minute render gives you several candidate sections to choose from.

I use EasyMusic for this. In its Advanced Mode you choose Instrumental, write the Style brief, list unwanted elements under Exclude styles, and set a suggested duration between 10 and 360 seconds. The EasyMusic Style brief guide covers how to structure that text. Any generator with an instrumental mode will do, though, and so will a bounce from your own DAW project.

One licensing note before you ship anything: check the terms of the tool you used. Many generators, including ours, limit free-tier output to personal use and include commercial licensing only on paid plans. A game you sell is commercial use.

Step 2: Work from a lossless copy

Download WAV if your tool offers it. If you only have MP3, decode it once (ffmpeg -i track.mp3 track.wav) and do every edit on the WAV. Because you're cutting a region from inside the track, the padding at the very start of the MP3 doesn't affect your loop points. It only causes trouble if you ship an MP3 and expect the engine to loop it gaplessly.

If FFmpeg is new to you, HackerNoon's audio handling basics post covers trimming and conversion from the command line.

Step 3: Find the bar grid

In 4/4 time, one bar lasts 240 ÷ BPM seconds:

Work in samples, not seconds, because rounding errors in seconds add up into audible drift. Check the file's sample rate with ffprobe -v error -show_entries stream=sample_rate -of csv=p=0 track.wav.

If you don't know the tempo or where the first downbeat falls, a beat tracker gets you close. This uses librosa's beat_track:

import librosa, numpy as np y, sr = librosa.load("track.wav", sr=None, mono=True) tempo, beats = librosa.beat.beat_track(y=y, sr=sr, units="time") print("tempo estimate:", float(np.atleast_1d(tempo)[0])) print("first beats (s):", np.round(beats[:8], 3))

Use the output as a starting point, not as the answer. The beat times are snapped to analysis frames, so they can be off by a few tens of milliseconds. Tracker tempos can also come out at half or double the real value. Confirm the downbeat by ear, and pick a loop start after the intro, where the arrangement has settled.

AI-generated audio isn't locked to a click track, so the tempo can drift slightly. For long loops, measure the loop length directly: find the downbeat 16 bars later and use the actual sample distance between the two downbeats, not the BPM formula.

Step 4: Cut and crossfade with FFmpeg

The approach: take exactly N bars starting at sample S. Then take the next X samples, the audio that naturally follows the loop end, including any reverb tail. Fade that tail out while fading the head of the loop in, using an equal-power curve. When the loop wraps, playback runs from the original audio just before the loop end into the original audio just after it, so the seam is continuous. The script also writes an intro-plus-loop file for engines that support a loop offset. It uses the atrim, afade, and amix filters.

#!/usr/bin/env bash # usage: ./cut.sh input.wav START_SAMPLE LOOP_SAMPLES XFADE_SAMPLES set -euo pipefail IN="$1"; S="$2"; L="$3"; X="$4" E=$((S + L)); T=$((E + X)) ffmpeg -hide_banner -loglevel error -y -i "$IN" -filter_complex " [0:a]asplit=3[a][b][c]; [a]atrim=end_sample=$S,asetpts=PTS-STARTPTS[intro]; [b]atrim=start_sample=$S:end_sample=$E,asetpts=PTS-STARTPTS, afade=t=in:ns=$X:curve=qsin[body]; [c]atrim=start_sample=$E:end_sample=$T,asetpts=PTS-STARTPTS, afade=t=out:ns=$X:curve=qsin[tail]; [body][tail]amix=inputs=2:duration=first:normalize=0[loop]; [loop]asplit=2[l1][l2]; [intro][l1]concat=n=2:v=0:a=1[introloop]" \ -map "[l2]" -c:a pcm_s16le loop.wav \ -map "[introloop]" -c:a pcm_s16le intro_plus_loop.wav

Example: a 100 BPM track at 48 kHz whose fifth bar starts at 11.6 s, looped for 16 bars with a 50 ms crossfade:

./cut.sh track.wav 556800 1843200 2400

For the crossfade length, start around 10–30 ms (480–1,440 samples at 48 kHz) for dry, drum-heavy material. For pads or anything with a long reverb, go up to roughly the length of the tail, 0.5–2 s. normalize=0 matters: without it, amix scales both inputs down and you'd hear a dip in level at the seam.

Step 5: Check the seam before you trust it

First, the numbers. This short script compares the jump at the wrap-around with the normal sample-to-sample movement in the file:

import soundfile as sf, numpy as np, sys y, sr = sf.read(sys.argv[1]); y = y.mean(axis=1) if y.ndim > 1 else y jump = abs(y[0] - y[-1]) # step at the wrap-around typical = np.percentile(np.abs(np.diff(y)), 99) # big-but-normal sample steps print(f"{len(y)} samples = {len(y)/sr:.4f} s at {sr} Hz") print(f"seam step {jump:.4f} vs 99th-percentile step {typical:.4f}", "-> OK" if jump <= 2 * typical else "-> likely click, move the cut or lengthen the crossfade")

The sample count should match LOOP_SAMPLES exactly. In a test on a synthetic 100 BPM file, a plain cut at the same bar line failed the check, and the crossfaded version passed.

Then listen. Render four repeats and play them back on headphones, paying attention to each join:

ffmpeg -stream_loop 3 -i loop.wav -c copy loop_x4.wav

A good loop sounds like one continuous piece. If you can tell exactly where each repeat starts, go to the troubleshooting list below.

Step 6: Export and set the loop points

Export to Ogg Vorbis: ffmpeg -i intro_plus_loop.wav -c:a libvorbis -q:a 6 intro_plus_loop.ogg. In the same test, the OGG decoded back to the exact sample count. Ship WAV if file size doesn't matter. Don't ship MP3 for anything that loops.

Godot 4. Select the OGG and open the Import tab. Enable Loop and set Loop Offset to the intro length in seconds (556800 / 48000 = 11.6 in the example), then reimport. The Godot audio import docs describe Loop Offset as the point "where audio will start to loop after playback reaches the end." For WAV files, Godot takes Loop Begin and Loop End in samples instead.

Unity. Use two AudioSources: one for an intro clip (export it as its own file with atrim=end_sample=$S) and one with loop enabled for loop.ogg. Start them with AudioSource.PlayScheduled, which the docs call "the preferred way to stitch AudioClips in music players":

double t0 = AudioSettings.dspTime + 0.2; introSource.PlayScheduled(t0); double introLen = (double)introSource.clip.samples / introSource.clip.frequency; loopSource.PlayScheduled(t0 + introLen); // loopSource.loop = true

Web Audio API. Decode the intro-plus-loop file into an AudioBuffer, then set loopStart and loopEnd in seconds:

const ctx = new AudioContext(); // start after a user gesture const data = await (await fetch("intro_plus_loop.ogg")).arrayBuffer(); const src = ctx.createBufferSource(); src.buffer = await ctx.decodeAudioData(data); src.loop = true; src.loopStart = 556800 / 48000; // 11.6 s src.loopEnd = (556800 + 1843200) / 48000; // 50.0 s src.connect(ctx.destination); src.start();

Use seconds rather than buffer indices here, because the browser may resample the decoded audio to the context's sample rate. Format support varies by browser, so test your target browsers. If any of them can't play OGG, ship WAV. For Web Audio basics, see HackerNoon's Getting Started with Web Audio API.

Troubleshooting

  • Click at the seam. Move S (and S + L) a few hundred samples to a nearby zero crossing on the downbeat, or lengthen the crossfade.
  • Rhythmic hiccup every repeat. The loop isn't a whole number of bars. Measure downbeat to downbeat instead of trusting the BPM.
  • Flam or smear on drums at the join. The crossfade is too long for transient material. Shorten it to 10–20 ms.
  • The reverb drops out at the seam. The crossfade is shorter than the tail. Make X longer.
  • A gap in the browser or engine. You're probably shipping MP3. Switch to OGG or WAV.
  • Level jump between intro and loop. Both files came from the same render, so don't normalize them separately.

Limits worth knowing

This method can't fix a section that doesn't repeat musically. If the chords at bar 1 and bar 17 differ, the crossfade will smear two different harmonies together, so pick another section. Short loops also get tiring: 30–60 seconds of material, or two or three variations you rotate, holds up better over a long play session. And the edit can only be as clean as the source. If the generated audio has artifacts, regenerate or fix the section before you cut it.

Wrapping up

Seamless looping is an editing problem, not a prompting problem. Generate steady material, cut whole bars in samples, fold the tail into the head with an equal-power crossfade, check the seam, ship OGG or WAV, and let the engine handle intro and loop points. Save the script, and the next track takes minutes. If you have a looping trick for adaptive or layered game music, I'd like to hear it in the comments.
