Apple’s ‘Write with Siri’ prompt was driving me crazy. Here’s how I shut it off

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There’s a lot to love about iOS 27 and macOS 27, the new operating systems released for iPhone and Mac last month. The Liquid Glass design language now has a fluidity and polish that it lacked when introduced last year in iOS 26 and macOS 26.

But as much as I love the new operating systems, there’s one thing that has driven me crazy ever since I updated my devices: the “Write with Siri” tool, which appears as a popover floating toolbar on the Mac and a big button above the keyboard on iPhone.

The tool, part of the new Siri AI, helps users generate text nearly anywhere—in a document, email, or messaging app. Unfortunately, it’s there even if you don’t want to use it. (And Apple, I never do. I don’t need help from generative AI in deciding how I should respond to my mom’s text inquiring when I’ll be over for dinner next).

Its presence is not only pointless for many who don’t need machine help with their writing, but it’s distracting, especially on the Mac, where a stray mouse click can easily expand the popover when you are trying to edit text. Thankfully, there is a way you can disable Write with Siri—though Apple doesn’t make it obvious.

How to disable ‘Write with Siri’ on an iPhone or iPad

On the iPhone, the Write with Siri tool primarily takes the form of a big glaring button above the keyboard on iOS 27. It’s there by default, whether you want it or not. And frustratingly, if you do want to disable it, you can’t do so where you would naturally assume you could: in the Siri section of the Settings app.

But there is a place where you can. It’s hidden under the Content & Privacy Restrictions of the Screen Time settings in iOS 27. Here’s how to find those settings and shut Write with Siri down:

  1. Open the Settings app.
  2. Tap Screen Time.
  3. Under the Restrictions heading, tap Content & Privacy.
  4. Toggle the Content & Privacy Restrictions switch to on (green).
  5. Tap Siri.
  6. Tap Writing Assistance.
  7. Tap “Don’t Allow.”

Now, when your iPhone’s keyboard next appears, the big “Write with Siri” button sitting atop the keyboard should be gone, replaced by the default predictive text choices that have been a staple of iOS for years (and which are easier to disable, by going to Settings>General>Keyboard).

How to disable ‘Write with Siri’ on a Mac

On a Mac, things are a little less straightforward. Common sense says you should be able to follow the steps above to disable Write with Siri. But in my testing, even after turning off the Writing Assistance toggle (in the Siri content restrictions in macOS’s Screen Time settings), the annoying “Write with Siri” floating bubble still appears wherever you type in apps, like Pages. I suspect that this is an unresolved bug.

Thankfully, there’s another option for turning off Write with Siri on the Mac (though it is more restrictive). You can do this by disabling the new Siri AI in one of two ways.

The first is by going to the Siri settings in the Settings app:

  1. Open the Settings app.
  2. Click Siri.
  3. Click Turn Off Siri.
  4. Click the next Turn Off Siri button, too.

Alternately, you can disable the new Siri AI and default to the old, less robust version, which does not support the Write with Siri popover floating toolbar.

To revert to the old version of Siri: 

  1. Open the Settings app.
  2. Click Screen Time.
  3. Under the Restrictions heading, click Content & Privacy.
  4. Toggle the Content & Privacy Restrictions switch to on (blue).
  5. Click Siri.
  6. On the next screen, under “Allowed Siri Version,” select “Siri Classic” from the drop-down menu.
  7. Click Done.

Apple doesn’t make disabling ‘Write with Siri’ straightforward—and that’s probably by design

Big Tech has spent hundreds of billions of dollars on artificial intelligence development over the past five years—and there’s no sign that spending will be slowing anytime soon. It’s a huge bet, and in order for it to pay off, the tech giants need everyday users to adopt their AI tools en masse.

For many consumers, that adoption has felt forced upon them. Google search now surfaces AI answers before search results, Meta has shoved its AI assistant into nearly every app it makes, and Microsoft has integrated AI across Windows.

Apple, of course, has spent far less on AI buildout than its competitors have, but that hasn’t stopped it from jumping on the AI train. The headline feature of its new operating systems this year was Siri AI, and its newest hardware product, the HomeView, relies entirely on artificial intelligence.

That’s not to mention the fact that investors seem to be demanding Apple “catch up” to its other Big Tech competitors to become a major player in the space. So it’s no wonder that Apple, too, wants to put its AI offerings within as easy reach as possible—even if its users haven’t asked for it.

Increasing Siri AI usage is surely a goal the company has, so it’s not much of a surprise that Apple has made disabling its individual features less than straightforward, but now you know you at least can. I wonder what Siri would say about that?

Software Engineer - AI Tooling Infrastructure at Supabase

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

About Supabase

Supabase is the Postgres development platform, built by developers for developers. We provide a complete backend solution including Database, Auth, Storage, Edge Functions, Realtime, and Vector Search. All services are deeply integrated and designed for growth.


About the Role

We’re looking for a Software Engineer, AI Tooling Infrastructure to join our AI Tooling team and help build and run the backend services behind Supabase’s AI products. The Supabase MCP server is one example of what the team runs in production, alongside the services that support agent skills and the dashboard assistant. You’ll work closely with the Platform, SRE, Auth, Billing and Edge Functions teams, and with the engineers on the AI Tooling team who build the product surfaces.

The AI Tooling team runs its own services in production and carries its own on-call. Traffic from AI clients and agents behaves differently from traffic from people: hundreds of calls a minute, retry loops, and no reading of deprecation warnings. This role makes sure the services they use are reliable, correctly authorized and observable. It is a systems and infrastructure role rather than a product AI role, and it is not an SRE role. You will build and operate these systems, not only advise on them.

This role is ideal for someone who thrives in async, fast-paced environments, owns what they ship in production, and is excited about building developer tools that scale to millions.


What You’ll Be Responsible For

In this role, you’ll:

  • Build and operate the production backend services behind Supabase’s AI products, including the MCP server: deployment, scaling, configuration and release.

  • Take ownership of how those services are structured, including splitting or reshaping a service when that makes it safer to run and easier to change.

  • Define, measure and meet reliability targets for the services the team runs. Set SLOs with the SRE team, track error budgets, and use them to decide what to fix first.

  • Participate in the AI Tooling team on-call rotation, and be a first responder for incidents in those services. Investigate, fix, coordinate with other teams during incidents, and write the follow-ups and runbooks.

  • Work the AI Tooling team support queue alongside on-call, and turn recurring issues into fixes rather than repeated manual answers.

  • Own authentication and authorization for AI and agent clients: OAuth apps, scoped tokens, and permissions that make sense for agents as well as humans, including how we change or deprecate a permission without breaking existing integrations.

  • Own observability for the services the team runs: metrics, traces, logs, dashboards and alerts, so we find problems before customers do.

  • Handle capacity and abuse for agent traffic: rate limits, cost and safety controls, and behavior under load or when a dependency degrades.

  • Improve the architecture of the services the team owns as they grow, including splitting or reshaping a service when that makes it safer to run and easier to change.

  • Work with the Platform team on shared code, review ownership and release process, and with the SRE team on infrastructure, alerting and incident practice.

  • Contribute to the code of the AI Tooling team’s tools, such as the MCP server, where it affects reliability and security, in partnership with the engineers who own the product surface.


You Might Be a Good Fit If You

Must have:

  • Have 5+ years of experience building and running backend services in production, and have carried on-call for them.

  • Write strong TypeScript or Go, and are comfortable working in the other.

  • Have designed and operated APIs with real consumers: versioning, error handling, rate limiting, and backwards-compatible change.

  • Know OAuth, OIDC, JWT and API authorization well enough to debug them in production and to explain where implementations commonly deviate from the spec.

  • Have a good understanding of PostgreSQL and relational databases.

  • Are comfortable with CI/CD, Docker and containerized deployments, and with infrastructure as code. Any tool is fine.

  • Can debug across services: follow a slow or failing request through several systems using metrics, traces and logs.

  • Communicate clearly in writing, work with a wide range of stakeholders, and thrive in async, globally distributed teams.

  • Are comfortable navigating ambiguity and moving quickly.

Pick your centre of gravity. We expect you to be deep in at least one of these, and to tell us which:

  • APIs and auth: You have designed permission models, token scopes or OAuth flows for third-party or machine clients, and you have migrated them without breaking users.

  • Operations: You have run production services yourself: carried the pager, written alerts and runbooks, used SLOs or similar to decide what to fix, and made shared systems less risky over time.

  • Gateways and distributed systems: You have built or run proxies or gateways, or debugged hard failures across services (retries, partial failures, upstream outages, fail-open vs fail-closed decisions), and you can reason about load and degradation.

Nice to have:

  • Experience with agent tooling (MCP or similar), or serving traffic from AI agents and LLM clients.

  • Experience moving a service out of a larger codebase while keeping existing clients working.

  • Experience with Grafana, OpenTelemetry, Logflare or a similar observability stack, and real SLO work.

  • Open-source contributions, especially to Node.js, Go, TypeScript or API tooling.

  • ESOP

    Every team member receives ESOP (equity ownership) in the company. We want everyone to share in the upside of what we’re building together.

  • Health Benefits

    Supabase covers 100% of health insurance for employees and 80% for dependents, wherever you are. Your wellbeing and your family’s health are important to us.

  • Annual Off-Sites

    Once a year, the entire company gathers in a new city for a week of connection, collaboration, and fun. It’s a highlight of our year.

  • Flexible Work

    We operate asynchronously and trust you to manage your own time. You know what needs to be done and when.

  • Professional Development

    Every team member receives an annual education allowance to spend on learning—courses, books, conferences, or anything that supports your growth.

About the Team

Supabase was born-remote and open-source-first. We believe our globally distributed team is our secret weapon in building tools developers love.

  • ~400 team members

  • 60+ countries

  • 20+ languages spoken

  • Over $1B raised (including our $500M Series F)

Alice Bucknell’s The Alluvials

 

In 2023, artist Alice Bucknell released The Alluvials, a video work filled with wolves, moths, and sediment that looks at a version of Los Angeles in the near future. In this California city, the sand that had once been characteristic of its beaches has taken over downtown, effectively turning the sprawl of highways and skyscrapers into a desert of dunes and dust. Through this speculative lens on an alternate reality, Bucknell presents both the history and consequences of this dried-up world. The work examines the history of the Tongva people of the Greater Los Angeles Basin while also imagining the water desalination corporation Qwench, a perpetually burning landscape, and an eternity in which the sky remains in its golden hour.

 

A year later, the project evolved into The Alluvials video game (2024), a multilevel game that explores the world inside the film. With each embodied exploration of its digital realm, the project presents the uncanny and nearly incomprehensible fallout of climate change. In their exploration of a not-so-far-off Los Angeles, Bucknell poses the questions: Is this how we will want to live? And if the answer is no, what modes of action do we have access to in order to reroute our course in the progression of climate change?

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video work, 2023 | all images courtesy the artist unless stated otherwise Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video work, 2023 | all images courtesy the artist unless stated otherwise

 

 

Embodied perspectives of NPCs

 

The word alluvial means of or relating to alluvium, which Merriam-Webster describes as ‘clay, silt, sand, gravel, or similar detrital material deposited by running water.’ In the game, Bucknell puts players in a kind of alluvial world, one that results from sand and silt accumulating where there should have been running water. Within it, there are four levels in which the player takes on the perspective of what are usually considered non-playable characters (NPCs). In doing so, Bucknell challenges traditional gameplay dynamics with what they refer to as ‘queer and ecological game mechanics.’

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024 Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

Exploring each level of The Alluvials

 

In one such level, Extinction Burst, the game opens with a bright red forest and a mountain lion sulking down a fuchsia path. The scene gives way to a dried-out basin filled with white particles. On closer inspection, these drifting flakes are revealed to be a form of moth. As you approach a desert tree growing near the center of the basin, a question in blue, hazy letters reveals itself in the sky: ‘ARE YOU MOTH OR ARE YOU TREE?’

 

Depending on whether you choose the moth or the tree path, you encounter two different perspectives of the desert landscape. In other levels, you walk through a forest fire, become a wolf in a barren, sand-ridden Los Angeles, or pilot an airboat speeding through the Los Angeles River in the evening. By presenting these alternative points of view, the game not only challenges the agency of the player but also offers an embodied understanding of things humans may take for granted: the knowledge of a moth, a yucca plant, or a body of water.

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

Exhibition history

 

The Alluvials has been exhibited in both film and video game formats in spaces across the US, Europe, and Asia. In each space, Bucknell’s work has come to life in different forms. When the film was shown at LUMINEX in downtown Los Angeles as a four-channel work, scenes of the forest fire lit up the building, creating an uncanny reflection of both the real and fictional city. At HEK Other Intelligences 2025 in Basel, Switzerland, pink camo beanbags were set up on the floor, presenting the game as a two-channel video game that visitors could sit down and play in a space awash in fuchsia.

Alice Bucknell, still from The Alluvials, video game, 2024

 

 

How to live in the world of The Alluvials

 

Within Bucknell’s work, the artist brings together a work of speculative fiction that speaks from an unknown future but carries the threatening tone of a parable lived many times over. True to the worlds they build in game engines, Bucknell makes real the promises, threats, and predictions that the CEOs and climate scientists of our time launch into the news cycle. Yet here, there is an opportunity to progress, level by level, through the very possible consequences of our present-day actions if left unchecked. Here, the posthuman utopia is one in which we leave humanity behind—one that discards the anthropocentric thinking that leads to artificial waters—and instead move forward with a future informed by the logic and processes of the natural world, before it is too late.

Jeff Ekhator has finally rejoined his Juventus teammates on the training pitch. The young striker, signed from Genoa in the summer, took part in a full group session at the Continassa on Friday for the first time since a run of muscle problems that has so far kept him out of every competitive game in black and white.

His return gives Luciano Spalletti one more name to consider in a week when the squad has been kept together at the training centre ahead of Sunday’s visit of Napoli.

A frustrating start in Turin

Juventus announced the permanent deal on 1 July, and Football Italia put the fee at €16m plus add ons. The 19 year old has barely been available since.

According to Gianluca Di Marzio, who credits Sky Sport Italia with the news, his troubles began in the very first friendly of pre-season against Basel. He recovered in time to be named in the squad for the second Serie A round against Parma, where he stayed on the bench, but broke down again a few days later and has spent roughly two further months on the sidelines.

Di Marzio describes the forward as fully recovered, and Friday’s session with the rest of the group was his first since that setback.

Where he fits in Spalletti’s attack

Nobody should expect Ekhator to be rushed into the starting eleven after so long without match practice. Football Italia sees him more as an extra option from the bench, giving Spalletti a third centre forward alongside Nick Woltemade and Randal Kolo Muani.

Adventurer to Climb 100 Peaks on 2,000Km Taiwan Traverse

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Tomorrow, November 1, Japanese adventurer Yoki Tanaka begins a four-month, 2,000km trek north to south down the length of Taiwan. Along the way, he will summit all 100 of Taiwan's celebrated Baiyue peaks.

Taiwan has about 268 peaks exceeding 3,000m, but only 100 of them made this special list, which is based not only on height but beauty, difficulty, prominence, and character. Prominent Taiwanese mountaineers compiled the list in the early 1970s, partly inspired by Japan's own list of 100 revered mountains.

Yoki Tanaka arrives in Taiwan earlier this week. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

The 43-year-old Tanaka has specialized in this unusual combination of long-distance trekking and mountaineering. In 2014, he summited Japan's 100 famous mountains while walking 7,800km over 208 days. (Japan, though far bigger than Taiwan, has only 23 mountains over 3,000m.) A year later, he extended that by climbing 200 mountains and walking 8,000km in 222 days. Finally, he increased that again to 300 mountains. This time, it took him three years and about 20,000km of walking.

Tanaka's projected route across Taiwan.

 

Tanaka will begin on Sunday from the northernmost tip of Taiwan, at the Cape Fugui lighthouse, and finish sometime in March at Eluanbi, the country's southernmost point. The first 150km will be along roads before he reaches his first trailhead. He'll spend the next week trekking. His first five peaks will be Xueshan (3,886m), Nanhudashan (3,742m), Hehuanshan (3,417m), Qilaishan (3,560m), and Nenggaoshan 3,262m).

Precise planning

Although he won't use motorized transport for the entire journey, he will have ample opportunity to resupply along the way. His expedition plan includes a charmingly precise breakdown of how he plans to spend his evenings: town stays, 48 days; mountain huts, 38 days; tent camping, 39 days. He has also budgeted 16 rest days and 20 contingency days.

Tanaka trains in Taiwan earlier this year. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

His most difficult section, scheduled for early January, will take him through the remote interior of the Central Mountain Range for over two weeks. During this time, he'll have to carry his own food, search for trails that may no longer exist, and gamble that he will be able to find enough water.

During a December 2025 reconnaissance trip to Xueshan, Taiwan's second-highest mountain, Tanaka discovered how difficult it can be to obtain water. Two water sources marked on his map were completely dry.

On a previous hike. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

By late January, the Japanese trekker should reach the historic Batongguan Trail, eventually leading to 3,952m Yushan, Taiwan's highest mountain. The Batongguan Trail was originally constructed in 1875 under the Qing dynasty to establish a route across the mountainous interior. Decades later, Japanese colonial authorities constructed a separate crossing through the region. Sections of the route survive as remote hiking trails and provide access to some of Taiwan's highest mountains.

Tanaka first visited Taiwan in the spring of 2025 and has made several reconnaissance visits since, including an east-west crossing of the island earlier this year. He had intended to begin his Taiwan 100 Peaks Traverse project this past spring, but local mountaineers advised him to avoid the rainy season and typhoon risks. Their advice prompted him to shift his starting date to November.

Yoki Tanaka on a training hike in Taiwan. Photo: Yoki Tanaka/Instagram

 

Interestingly, of Taiwan's 100 Baiyue peaks, only 99 are now recognized as surpassing 3,000m. The exception is Lushan at 2,981m. It was originally measured at 3,043m but subsequent measurements reduced its elevation. Nevertheless, it remains on the list.

Taiwan's representative in Japan told Japanese media that the fastest prior completion of the Baiyue by a foreign climber took three years and eight months, spread over multiple trips.

Once Tanaka begins, his expedition website will provide live tracking of his progress across Taiwan.

Backbone of a beast – charting Nvidia's rise to networking titan

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nvidia is more than just a leader in graphics and AI hardware. It has also become a titan of the networking industry with bigger datacenter Ethernet sales than any other vendor.

In Q2, Nvidia's networking revenues topped $17.5 billion. To put that in perspective, Cisco's revenue during that same period, across all products, was $17.3 billion.

GPUzilla's arrival as a networking power feels sudden, yet Jensen Huang started working towards this status in 2019 with the $6.9 billion acquisition of long-time networking partner Mellanox .

At the time, Mellanox wasn't a big name in Ethernet switching. The company was mostly known for its ConnectX NICs and InfiniBand switches. Outside of high-performance computing (HPC) and supercomputing clusters, and a big deployment at Microsoft, InfiniBand wasn't widely deployed in hyperscale datacenters.

Buying Mellanox allowed Nvidia to reposition itself as a system builder, which means something different in the context of distributed compute. Nvidia understood its datacenter customers weren't interested in buying GPUs or even servers; they wanted clusters of systems. For that, Nvidia needed interconnect and Mellanox's InfiniBand fit the bill.

Relentless co-design

Over the years, Huang has talked about co-design – designing components and software to work together for specific set of jobs rather than to be the best GPU, switch, or NIC. Its work with Mellanox is a prime example of that approach.

According to Dell'Oro Group analyst Sameh Boujelbene, Nvidia's decision to integrate compute and networking became key to the company's success.

"Customers choose Nvidia for its tightly integrated compute and networking architecture, optimized for AI workloads," she told The Register. That integration makes deployments easier and speeds time to market.

Ben Mabey, CTO of pioneering AI and machine learning company Recursion, told The Reg Nvidia's integration schemes won him over.

Founded in 2013, nearly a decade before ChatGPT made AI the subject of dinner-table discussion, Recursion sought to apply machine learning running atop GPUs to the problem of drug discovery.

This work involved churning through massive datasets which, Mabey tells us, now total more than 50 petabytes in size.

"When you think about our workloads, it makes LLMs workload training seem easy compared to the amount of data that has to be moved around," he told The Register.

"In the biological space and sciences in general, it's not well appreciated just how big the data actually is," he added. "The internet's big, but in the grand scheme of things, the text that people are training on is actually pretty small, versus the petabytes of data that we have of these images."

Recursion's first cluster was a system called BioHive Zero which it cobbled together from GTX 1080 graphics cards originally intended for gaming systems. It used Ethernet to connect GPUs and ran into the limits of conventional networking.

So, for its second cluster, an A100-based machine with around 300 accelerators, Recursion went with Nvidia's Quantum InfiniBand switches and ConnectX NICs.

As Mabey explains it, buying its compute and interconnects from the same vendor vastly simplified optimization and troubleshooting. When Recursion reached out to Nvidia for help tuning the system, they were able to consider it as a single system, rather than discrete compute and networking resources. This level of integration had only previously been offered by boutique supercomputer builders like Cray or Atos (Eviden/Bull/whatever it gets renamed next).

A wider Spectrum

Nvidia hasn't always listed networking revenue as a single line item in its financials. However, based on CFO commentary and a little back of the napkin math we can fill in the blanks.

Nvidia's networking revenues have grown steadily since the Mellanox acquisition. At two moments, its revenue shot up suddenly.

The first came in the 2023 calendar year (FY24) after OpenAI kicked off the AI gold rush with the launch of ChatGPT.

The second spread across 2024 and 2025, when Nvidia started selling Blackwell-generation GPUs and then started to ship them in large quantities.

"I see two major inflection points, both coinciding with the Blackwell platform ramp: the introduction of NVLink switches and the accelerating adoption of Spectrum-X Ethernet switches for AI back-end networks, driven by growing deployments of Nvidia GPU clusters and increased attach rates of Nvidia switches, NICs, optics, and cables," Boujelbene said.

It was around this time that users started deploy to Ethernet switches in large volume across Nvidia clusters. Elon Musk's Colossus supercomputer was one of the earliest examples.

As we reported at the time, the first half of the 200,000-chip H100 and H200-based cluster came online in 2024 and, at the time, was among the largest ever clusters deployed using Nvidia's Spectrum-X Ethernet switches.

Meta has also made extensive use of Nvidia's Spectrum switches in its own training clusters, including at its million-GPU Prometheus site in New Albany, Ohio.

There are a couple reasons Nvidia's Spectrum-X offering is doing so well.

First, through a combination of Spectrum Ethernet switches, superNICs, and some clever software optimizations, Nvidia was able to replicate many of the qualities that made InfiniBand so attractive while also benefiting from significantly higher radix and interoperability.

Ethernet switches, including Nvidia's own, have historically offered more ports. The higher the radix, the fewer switches are necessary to support a cluster at a given port speed.

This often gets misconstrued to mean that InfiniBand can't scale beyond clusters of a certain size, which isn't necessarily the case. But for clusters exceeding a certain number of GPUs, Ethernet is often more economical because it requires less equipment while achieving similar utilization.

That said, Nvidia's Spectrum-X isn't exactly unique. Broadcom, Cisco, AMD and others have managed to achieve utilization similar to InfiniBand through various investments in smartNICs and switches.

Nvidia's main differentiator, as Mabey and Boujelbene noted earlier, is the level of integration between the compute and networking.

The next frontier

Then Blackwell hit the market, enabling much greater scale and creating a surge in Nvidia's networking revenue.

Prior to Blackwell, Nvidia's NVLink and NVSwitch interconnect tech had largely been limited to single systems with up to eight GPUs. With the launch of the GB200 NVL72, these NVSwitches were broken out into dedicated appliances. (And yes, we remember GH200 NVL256 and NVL32, but from what we can tell these were never shipped in any meaningful volume, if at all.)

For every NVL72 rack system sold, Nvidia also sold nine NVSwitch trays, which enabled the system's six dozen GPUs to function as a single enormous GPU. This effectively guaranteed steady networking revenue growth: even if customers didn't buy Nvidia's scale-out switches, if they wanted NVL72 racks, they were automatically buying NVSwitch.

"We estimate that NVSwitch now accounts for roughly one-third of Nvidia’s networking revenue, up from virtually zero less than two years ago," Boujelbene told El Reg. "This underscores how quickly NVLink switching has become a major growth engine for Nvidia’s networking business."

Nvidia is now moving to grow its networking business even further with a new licensing program.

Until recently, Nvidia's NVSwitch tech was limited to its own accelerators. However, with the introduction of NVLink Fusion early last year, Nvidia opened the interconnect tech up to third parties to license.

Many chip designers, including Marvell, Qualcomm, Arm, Fujitsu, MediaTek, and d-Matrix, have announced plans to develop CPUs and XPUs using NVLink Fusion. The adoption of the interconnect opens the door to additional NVSwitch sales, as the chips using NVLink Fusion still rely on them for chip-to-chip communications, and there's no other place to get them, as Nvidia has yet to license out NVSwitch.

"NVLink Fusion allows Nvidia to extend its networking footprint beyond its own GPUs into the rapidly growing custom accelerator market. I see it as a strategic move to capture a larger share of AI networking spending, even in clusters that do not use Nvidia GPUs," Boujelbene said.

Nvidia's networking ambitions don't stop there.

"Nvidia continues to pursue opportunities in emerging networking domains as network requirements evolve to keep pace with increasingly demanding AI workloads," Boujelbene added. "The latest example is scale-in networking, designed to address the new requirements of agentic AI, while innovations such as CPO will help overcome growing bandwidth, power, and scalability challenges." ®

What’s Halloween without a little fun-gi. Arguably the most creepy of all fungi, Xylaria polymorpha can look like decaying zombie fingers creeping up from the forest floor. Commonly called dead man’s finger fungus, it and other similar sinister-looking fungi in the genus Xylaria grow throughout the United States. 

Take a look at at nine creepy pictures of this fungus—if you dare.

Dead man’s fingers are usually spotted at the bottom of decaying or dead trees. They break down deceased wood and release nutrients into the soil so that new plants can grow. Image: NPS/Bradburn. The finger-like shapes themselves are the fungi’s sexual reproductive structures. Image: Khlongwangchao via Getty Images. Like a real human finger, their exteriors can be rather stiff and strong. That firmness helps them to release spores and increase their chances of reproducing. Image: NPS Wheeler.
Xylaria are incredibly opportunistic genus and can release these spores for several months or even years. Image: NPS/Stiteler. The mushrooms grow from the forest floor among dry autumn oak leaves, with a mossy tree background in soft focus. Image: grechanyk via Getty Images. Simply plucking a dead man’s finger from the base of a tree doesn’t get rid of the fungi itself. It is merely the tip of a much larger organism.  Image: NPS. The creepy fungus is found on six continents. Image: Dr. John A Horsfall via Getty Images.
Some species of  Xylaria are known to cause black root rot, but that is typically a problem on trees or shrubs that were stressed already. Image: Riski Wayuningsih via Getty Images. Don’t worry. It’s completely harmless to humans, despite its walking dead-like appearance. Image: imageBROKER/Frank Leo via Getty Images.
