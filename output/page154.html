Vanja Matijevec, 79, March 3

Vanja Matijevec belonged to a generation of Slovenian alpinists who pushed onto some of the biggest walls in the Himalayas, but one of the defining moments of his climbing life came when he chose not to summit.

On his legendary 1979 expedition to Everest, Matijevec gave up his own planned summit attempt to help Stipe Božić, Stane Belak and Ang Phu, who had become stranded above 8,000 meters during their descent. Božić later remembered Matijevec’s selflessness as helping him survive after he lost a crampon high on the mountain.

Born in Ljubljana on October 14, 1946, Matijevec first encountered the Slovenian mountains as a child, tending cattle on pastures above Tržič. He came to alpinism later, after meeting climber Jože Hočevar through his field work, and began training as a mountain guide in 1975. In 1979, Matijevec, Lado Vidmar and Blaž Oblak made the first ascent of Lucifer (WI4+), a roughly 200-meter frozen waterfall in Gozd Martuljek. The climb, completed with equipment Matijevec later remembered as rudimentary, helped usher in the era of modern ice climbing in Slovenia.

But Matijevec’s ambitions stretched far beyond Slovenia. In 1981, Matijevec and Franček Knez climbed to the rim of Lhotse’s immense South Face, then considered one of the great unsolved problems of Himalayan alpinism. Over the course of his life, Matijevec accumulated more than 30 first ascents, including a direct new route up the West Face of Trisul in 1987. Despite his achievements, his peers remember his kindness more than anything. Fellow Slovenian alpinist Viki Grošelj, who had known Matijevec since their early climbing days, remembered him after his death as a distinctive personality and, simply, “the right man in the right place,” he told Slovenian newspaper Delo.

We Need to Move Past the “Shut Up And Die Like a Man” Mentality in the Mountains

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“Shut up and die like a man!” sounds like a line from a bad action movie, but we all heard it on the radio from the paragliding competition director. The director was yelling at a terrified pilot who thought he was falling to his death. The pilot had accidentally keyed open his radio’s shoulder mic, allowing his frantic screams to be broadcast across the competition frequency.

The unfortunate pilot begging to god to not die had collapsed his wing and plummeted in front of my eyes. Twenty-one years later, the memory of this sight and soundtrack is unforgettable, and so was the director’s barking, because there was clearly no sympathy for what the pilot thought was his final, horrific ride. Miraculously, the pilot recovered his wing and flew out to land and reflect on his life choices alone.

Most pilots didn’t think the competition director’s statement was out of line for the time. Many still don’t. I see terrible parallels between flying culture and some facets of climbing’s; in alpinism especially, some see death as an attractant, or an inevitability. Over my 40 years as an alpinist and 35 as a pilot, I’ve watched climbers experience gut-wrenching trauma—then do their best to ignore it, too scared or proud to deal with the ugly, messy truth. This avoidance sends a message to younger climbers, much like the competition director’s statement so long ago: Suck it up, move on, and die with honor if things go bad.

But living with trauma doesn’t have to be that way.

***

When my late friend Ken Wallator was 21, he climbed Mount Belanger in the Canadian Rockies with his girlfriend Heidi. They reached the top of the difficulties, unroped, and walked along a low-angle snow ridge to the summit of the peak. But the snow ridge was actually a massive, overhanging cornice of windblown snow, and it collapsed as they stopped to take a photo. Ken somehow managed to leap to safety but Heidi fell hundreds of meters down the face.

Ken, a brave bear of a man, downclimbed the face, found Heidi somehow still alive, and started carrying her out to a distant hut. She died before they reached it.

In the aftermath of Heidi’s death, I watched Ken receive a surprising amount of blame from grieving members of the climbing community. Didn’t he know better than to walk on a cornice? Why weren’t they roped up? Ken was already wracked with guilt from the accident. When his community piled on, their condemnation lit an internal firestorm.

Ken Wallator at a belay in the Canadian Rockies. (Photo: Will Gadd)

Thirty years later, in 2019, Ken helped me re-roof my house. He still had the strength of a bear and I watched him haul 100-pound loads of shingles up my rickety ladder. In a rare moment of emotionality, he told me how he still hadn’t gotten over Heidi’s death, or the climbing community’s blame. We talked more about trauma, and I realized only then how that day on Mount Belanger broke my friend in a way that never healed.

As two young, hungry alpinists, Ken and I didn’t know what mental “help” looked like 30 years ago, and Ken was too much a product of his culture to take it 30 years later. Despite his immense physical and mental strength, Ken died by suicide a few weeks after our talk.

***

More recently, Will Stanhope’s death has brought to mind this idea of “asking for help” once more. For years Stanhope had battled desperately with alcohol. He was facing various charges at the time of his death—including assault and sexual assault—while still succeeding on difficult routes as a professional climber and mountain guide.

In our last real conversation, Stanhope talked about needing to “harden up and stop letting the carnage get to me” one minute, then sobbed about the “death camp” of hard alpine climbing the next. Like Ken and me, he had lost many friends in the mountains.

“You and I [watched] our mentors and friends die, one after another, as if everyone around us was climbing their way to oblivion,” his friend Kieran Brownie wrote in an obituary. “We couldn’t return to the mountains, we couldn’t return to each other.”

Stanhope carried a heavy weight in the final months of his life, made even heavier by grief, guilt, and irreversible mistakes. After a lifetime of hard solos and even scarier roped climbs, it seemed impossible that Stanhope would ever fall off an easy 5.11. But when he did just that last April and succumbed to a head injury, to me and other friends, Stanhope’s death felt more related to the weight he carried in his head than the climb itself. His family and friends made truly profound and sometimes wrenchingly difficult efforts to help him, but, like falling, the weights of addiction and trauma develop their own momentum.

Will Gadd (left) and Will Stanhope (center, in white) surrounded by friends after a day of climbing in China.

Hard drinking, hard living, and celebrating dangerous acts—usually lauded as “bold”—are hallmarks of every mountain sport I’ve ever done, from kayaking to flying to skiing to climbing. Our literature is full of stories celebrating those who climbed hard, drank hard, and died silently.

I can’t help but wonder if our literature strives to make these deaths seem somehow valiant and necessary, like dying in the mountains is a noble sacrifice rather than the tragedy it really is. The classic mountaineering texts rarely explore the trauma that comes after seeing a friend die. They barrel ever onward to the summit.

***

Military veterans and first responders have a trauma-treatment framework that climbers should strive to adopt. Soldiers are not considered “weak” if they are affected by bloody combat. Paramedics are not ridiculed for taking leave after responding to a grisly car wreck. These are normal human responses, and professional groups typically have protocols for helping those involved in traumatic situations. These groups have no other choice; a traumatized team simply cannot function.

Those professional protocols weren’t there for Ken, Will, that terrified pilot, or many others, including me.

When I was 19 I tried with every tool I knew to save a fellow kayaker stuck just inches from the surface in his boat. I couldn’t reach him, and he drowned in front of me as I tried to remember how to rig a better haul system. I had nightmares for years of the paddler chasing me in the dark beside that river.

Only decades later did I seek help, after a divorce and a lot of behavior I understood even less than that terrible accident. I’m lucky I survived and sought belated help. Ken and Will and many others didn’t. Many more in our community—your friends and mine—are barely standing today, carrying crippling loads. They need help putting those loads down.

The pain of trauma does not disappear with six or 600 beers. Doing dangerous climbs or flights or other high-stimulus experiences temporarily distracts from the weight, but doesn’t solve it. There is no pack heavier than one filled with trauma you can’t put down.

One of the smartest things I’ve ever heard about trauma came after we failed to resuscitate a dying man at the base of an ice climb a few years ago. A professional responder on the scene could tell I was shaken, and looked me in the eye and said, “Post traumatic stress is normal; post traumatic stress disorder is optional—if you get help. Get help.”

I did.

***

I see real change today, from my wife Sarah Hueniken’s Mountain Muskox foundation to the American Alpine Club’s Grief Fund, two organizations built to support mountain-trauma survivors.

But I also still see a lot of the “Shut up and die like a man” attitude, and the celebration of “bold” yet physically unimpressive climbs that are often little more than Russian Roulette. Huge, low-angle alpine routes with immense serac and avalanche hazard are not that cool, in my book. And I see many climbers who still romanticize the wrong parts of climbing, whether that’s meaningless objective hazard or the very idea of “suffering silently.”

We need to recognize that a mountain death doesn’t end with the funeral; often that’s just the start of a very long and difficult process for everyone involved. In climbing more people die on the descent after the summit than on the climb itself.

At times I’ve felt deeply angry about our outdoor tribe’s celebration of “bold” hazards, and our denial of the after-effects of engaging in high-risk sports where participants die regularly. We often celebrate the bold free solos or dangerous alpine routes where everything clicked into place. We never celebrate the dead parent who doesn’t show up for their kids’ birthdays.

But the truth is that I laughed when the paragliding competition director said his infamous line. I promised myself that if I was in that situation in the future, I’d shut up. That became my normal. Today, I hope I’d speak up on the comment, and reach out to the guy who almost died that day. Because I’ve been him, and it sucks, and I’m trying to do better.

I’m less angry today when I think about Ken, Will, or others who have left the game, and just hope that our culture continues to evolve in the same way the first responders did, and that we ultimately celebrate survival more than dying silently. We can do better, if we want to.

Finally, if, like me, you’ve been through the trauma wringer, it often takes help to sort it out. We’re not tougher than the veterans or first responders even if our culture says we “should” be—we’re just less educated about intense trauma.

The goal is to return to full function and fly in life, not get stuck in the inevitable mental dust devils of mountain death. Peace to those hurting, it can get better with help. Substances and more dangerous living may distract us short term, but long term the combo is deadly despite the romantic words we historically place on both. I write these words for the future Wills and Kens, they are me, you, our friends.

Shutting up is not a solution, because silence is death.

What It Takes to Be Free

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

We spend our lives trying to find our power and our freedom. It is not easy: We live in a culture that has distorted the meaning and measure of power, confusing visibility with value, status with substance, attention with love; we live in a universe where free will may be an illusion, where chance deals the vast majority of the cards — the family and epoch we are born into, the body and biochemistry we live in, the people we cross paths with, the accidents of chemistry and happenstance we call love. The great double-edged question is how to keep power from becoming a prison and how much freedom we can find between the narrow parameters of our givens. But somewhere deep down, in the marrow of our language, we seem to know: The word power shares its Middle Latin root with possibility — its only measure is that which liberates the possible in us; our only power is our freedom.

Art from An Almanac of Birds: 100 Divinations for Uncertain Days, also available as a stand-alone print and as stationery cards.

“Nothing is more unbearable, once one has it, than freedom,” James Baldwin wrote. It is unbearable largely because it demands a shedding of the self with all of its attachments — to ways of being, to ideas about how the world ought to be, to particular interpretations of reality. Such attachments are shackles best broken by what Iris Murdoch has so splendidly termed unselfing, key to each of the five kinds of freedom she identified in literature and life. For Murdoch, freedom takes radical unsefling — only then are we truly free to both be ourselves and love each other:

Freedom is exercised in the confrontation by each other, in the context of an infinitely extensible work of imaginative understanding, of two irreducibly dissimilar individuals. Love is the imaginative recognition of, that is respect for, this otherness.

This is why, for Octavio Paz — who thought deeply about freedom — love is “a knot made of two intertwined freedoms.” And if love is our fulcrum of belonging, then belonging itself is woven of the same paradox — something Maya Angelou articulates in one of her finest interviews:

You only are free when you realize you belong no place — you belong every place — no place at all.

Art by Ofra Amit for The Dark Wood of the Golden Birds by Margaret Wise Brown

In this sense, freedom is the hinge between being and belonging that swings open the door to being ourselves — which, yet again paradoxically, must include the freedom to change, to confront the illusory solidity of the self, its ceaseless mutability. Psychoanalyst Karen Horney formulates this perfectly in her pioneering work on the fundaments of personal growth:

We do not need an inner strait jacket with which to shackle our spontaneity, nor the whip of inner dictates to drive us to perfection. There is no doubt that such disciplinary methods can succeed in suppressing undesirable factors, but there is also no doubt that they are injurious to our growth. We do not need them because we see a better possibility of dealing with destructive forces in ourselves: that of actually outgrowing them. The way toward this goal is an ever increasing awareness and understanding of ourselves. Self-knowledge, then, is not an aim in itself, but a means of liberating the forces of spontaneous growth… And as we lose the neurotic obsession with self, as we become free to grow ourselves, we also free ourselves to love and to feel concern for other people.

Then, and only then, can we contact the soul beneath the self, which is the only real locus of our freedom. Vassily Grossman — who spent a thousand days as a correspondent on the frontlines of the Holocaust — captures this beautifully in a passage from his almost unbearably poignant reckoning with life and death:

What constitutes the freedom, the soul of an individual life, is its uniqueness. The reflection of the universe in someone’s consciousness is the foundation of his or her power, but life only becomes happiness, is only endowed with freedom and meaning when someone exists as a whole world that has never been repeated in all eternity. Only then can they experience the joy of freedom and kindness, finding in others what they have already found in themselves.

If You Spot OTR on a Truck, Here’s What It Means

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Trucks are full of weird and useful features that most people overlook without knowing their real purpose. Some have long spikes on their wheels. Others might feature amber lights on top. You might even see diamond shaped signs. But what about OTR written on a truck?

What can look like a government code can have a specific meaning in the trucking industry. Learn what OTR means, how long-haul work compares with shorter routes, and why the context matters.

What Does OTR Mean When It’s Written on a Truck?

In trucking, OTR stands for “over-the-road.” It refers to long-haul trucking, where drivers transport freight across long distances, often through several states or into Canada. According to trucking company Schneider, OTR drivers can spend weeks away from home. Many drive trucks with sleeper cabs that provide a place to rest between shifts.

OTR describes the type of work, not a specific truck. It’s also not a required marking. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) requires certain identifying information, including a company’s name and USDOT number, on interstate commercial trucks, but OTR isn’t part of that requirement.

The letters can also mean something different depending on where they appear. In the tire industry, OTR stands for “off-the-road,” referring to heavy-duty tires used on construction and mining equipment. OTR Diagnostics is also the name of a maintenance tool that helps truck owners check engine problems and other vehicle information.

What Does an OTR Truck Driver Do?

OTR truck drivers transport freight over long distances, often making several stops along the way. Their jobs can also involve inspecting their trucks, handling delivery paperwork, and sometimes helping load or unload cargo.

Because these trips can last for weeks, drivers often sleep in their trucks or stop at other places along their routes. The FMCSA limits how long commercial drivers can drive without taking required breaks and rest periods.

Drivers who operate most full-size tractor-trailers need a Class A commercial driver’s license (CDL). Depending on the company and route, drivers may spend several weeks working before returning home.

What’s the Difference Between OTR, Regional, and Local Trucking?

OTR drivers cover the longest distances and may spend weeks away from home. Regional drivers work within a particular area of the country, often returning home weekly or more frequently. Local drivers generally stay closer to home and return after each shift, although they can still work long hours.

There’s also dedicated trucking, where drivers regularly transport freight for the same customer or between the same locations. These routes can be local, regional, or long-distance, depending on the company.

The exact distances and schedules vary between employers, so the terms don’t always describe the same mileage or amount of time away from home.

Proposal #1059 passed with 49.59% turnout as the chain counts down now to block 33,618,500 on October 28th, where validators are told to run the security-patched v29.0.1 binary, not the version named in the proposal.

The Cosmos Hub's latest upgrade drew almost no opposition. About 168.5 million ATOM voted on Proposal #1059, and only 2,335 ATOM voted No. Under that one-sided result is an unusual change: the Hub's stakers voted to remove the governance route that earlier generations of Cosmos proposals used to change the chain's own settings.

Proposal #1059 schedules the upgrade of the Cosmos Hub to Gaia v29.0.0. It finished voting on October 27 at 12:00 UTC with a PASSED result. The vote approves the upgrade, but the software has not run yet. The chain still has to reach the upgrade height, halt, and restart on the new binary. Until that happens, Gaia v29 is approved but not yet live.

Turnout Cleared Quorum By Nearly Ten Points

On-chain records show 49.59% of staked ATOM voted, against a 40% quorum requirement. Yes took 99.41% of the vote, or 167,514,965 ATOM. No With Veto took 0.58% (982,855 ATOM), Abstain took 0.01% (14,360 ATOM), and No rounds to 0.00% (2,335 ATOM).

The veto share is the main number to watch in a Cosmos governance vote, because a large enough veto rejects a proposal even if Yes wins. At 0.58%, the veto bloc was far too small to matter. That fits the proposal's nature. It is a maintenance release that removes deprecated code and updates dependencies, and it has no economic or political stakes.

The forum discussion covered one governance point: a delegator's own vote overrides their validator's vote. A few large validators did not decide the result on their own. Delegators could have overridden them, and very few chose to vote differently.

Why Validators Are Being Told To Run v29.0.1

The proposal names v29.0.0, but operators are not supposed to run that build at the upgrade height. On October 23, three days into voting, the Gaia team tagged v29.0.1 and updated the official forum post to recommend it.

The patch changes only one thing: it rebuilds Gaia with Go 1.26.10 instead of Go 1.26.7. That picks up the Go security fix tracked as CVE-2026-111943 (GO-2026-6809). The project's upgrade notes call v29.0.1 a non-state-breaking patch release, so it computes the same chain state as v29.0.0. Nodes that already installed v29.0.0 can switch at any time by replacing the binary and restarting.

Operators using Cosmovisor need to know one detail. The on-chain upgrade name stays v29.0.0, so the v29.0.1 binary still goes into the upgrades/v29.0.0/bin/ directory. If a validator names the folder after the patch version, Cosmovisor will not find the binary when the chain halts. The project's sample service configuration has automatic binary download turned off, which means each operator has to put the correct binary in place before the halt.

The Governance Route The Hub Just Voted Away

Gaia v29 removes the x/params module from the Cosmos Hub. That module is the original, centralized store for parameters, and it backed the legacy ParameterChangeProposal governance route. The release changelog lists the change under API-breaking changes: "The legacy ParameterChangeProposal governance route and cosmos.params.v1beta1.Query service are no longer available."

In practice, governance has already moved on. Each module now owns its parameters, and changes go through that module's own governance message. The legacy route remained as a fallback, and v29 removes it. The rate-limiting module was the last part of the Hub still wired to the old params subspace. In v29 it no longer depends on it.

The forum Q&A states who is affected. RPC providers, wallets, explorers, and governance dashboards should check whether they still call the params v1beta1 query service or create legacy parameter-change proposals. After the upgrade, those calls will fail on the Hub. For most users nothing changes. The proposal requires no token migration, and balances, delegations, and IBC assets are unaffected.

Multisig Signers Lose Automatic Gas Estimation

The second API-breaking item affects multisig accounts specifically. Gaia v29 moves the Hub from Cosmos SDK v0.53.4 to v0.53.8, and the changelog warns: "As of cosmos-sdk v0.53.8, --gas auto gas simulation is broken for multisig senders. Multisig transactions must specify a fixed --gas value until this is fixed upstream."

This matters in practice. The Hub's weekly update of October 22 said the problem applies to the Community Pool migration multisig. That multisig is handling a USDC.n migration spend that was moving toward an on-chain proposal during the same week. Treasury signers, custody desks, and DAO operators who build transactions with --gas auto will need to set gas by hand until the upstream SDK is fixed.

The same release also brings CometBFT from 0.38.23 to 0.38.25, the IBC rate-limiting middleware from 10.1.0 to 10.7.0, and runc from 1.2.8 to 1.3.6. It also moves the build toolchain from Go 1.25 to Go 1.26.

Interchain Security Leftovers Get Wiped

The upgrade handler, the code that runs once at the upgrade height, does more cleanup than the proposal summary describes. It first deletes everything in the deprecated provider store, the state left over from the Hub's time as an Interchain Security provider. Next it wipes the legacy x/params store. Last, it removes a receipt left behind by the earlier v28.3.1 balance migration.

The code also shows how cautious the Gaia team is about the upgrade. The stores stay mounted, and their contents are cleared as ordinary state changes. Unmounting them entirely could cause version-mismatch errors when nodes load the chain from disk. Gaia v29 has also run on the Hub's provider testnet since October 13, about two weeks before mainnet.

What Happens At Block 33,618,500 on October 28th

At 15:32 UTC on October 27, Mintscan showed block 33,618,500 as "not yet created". The chain was at block 33,604,199, with 14,301 blocks to go. At the recent average block time of 5.722 seconds, the Mintscan countdown for block 33,618,500 estimated the height for about 14:16 UTC on October 28. The forum's original estimate was 13:50 UTC. The real time depends on how fast blocks are produced over the next day.

When the chain reaches that height, block production stops. It restarts only after validators holding more than two-thirds of voting power are back online on the new binary. That makes the vote result only half the story. The other half is whether validators show up on v29.0.1 promptly, so that the halt lasts minutes rather than hours.

Michael Carrick has come under immense pressure as a disjointed Manchester United sit just two places above the relegation places.

Despite the scrutiny and the news of a possible sacking, Michael Carrick has been given another chance at Manchester United.

Michael Carrick will remain in charge of Manchester United for their Premier League trip to Chelsea on Saturday after the club decided to give their under-pressure head coach another opportunity to turn things around.

United have gone eight games without a win in all competitions, with Sunday’s humiliating 3-0 home defeat to Bournemouth leaving them 16th in the Premier League table, just one point above the relegation zone.

Carrick’s position looked increasingly uncertain after the Bournemouth loss. As a result, United chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox were at Carrington on Monday to discuss the situation. 

It has been reported by Sky Sports that United have considered other options internally as well to replace Micahel Carrick but for the weekend, they are sticking with Michael Carrick.

Carrick took training on Tuesday and the decision means he has at least one more match to convince the club’s hierarchy that he can get United back on track.

Gary Neville surprised by Michael Carrick decision

Gary Neville admitted he was surprised that Carrick had survived the immediate threat of losing his job, although he welcomed the decision.

“I am surprised, but I am happy because I was absolutely certain that what I witnessed on Sunday would be the end of Michael,” Neville said in Sky Sports.

The former United captain explained that Carrick’s actions after the Bournemouth defeat had made him think the manager was preparing to say goodbye to the supporters.

“It looked like he was walking round at the end on his own with the players behind him, like it was almost a goodbye lap,” Neville added.

Fans continued singing the manager’s name after the final whistle, while the rest of the team drew boos.

Everyone Goes to Havasu Canyon for the Waterfalls. Here’s What They’re Missing.

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Everyone’s seen them: the Gatorade-blue waterfalls surrounded by bright-red cliffs. They don’t look real. And in a world increasingly dominated by AI-generated images, it’s easy to look at a photograph of Havasu Falls and say, “Yeah, right.”

But I can tell you, they are real. And somehow, they’re even more beautiful in person.

Havasu Falls is on just about every backpacker’s bucket list. Hell, it’s on just about everyone’s bucket list, backpacker or not. And rightfully so. I’d rank the waterfalls among the most beautiful places I’ve ever seen.

But I expected that beauty from the falls. What I didn’t expect was a true backpacking adventure, a challenging hike in, a longer-than-expected adventure to the bottom of the Grand Canyon, an ice-cold beer 20 miles from the nearest paved road, and a herd of angry sheep blocking the trail.

I went for the waterfalls. But when I tell people about Havasu, I usually end up telling them about everything that came after.

Both the hike into and the hike past Havasu were challenging desert treks. Do not underestimate them. (Photo: Alex Moliski)

The Hike In: Hualapai Hilltop to Havasu Campground

Map distance: 10 miles (from parking to campground)
My distance: 11 miles
Elevation change: 800 feet

I won’t go into much detail about the permits, reservations, or logistics of visiting Havasu Canyon. The FAQ page on the Havasupai Tribe’s reservations website explains all of that. Permits open every year on February 1 at 8 a.m. Arizona time (with presale opening in late January). So if you want to go next year, now’s a good time to start planning. September to November are some of the best times to visit.

My first surprise was the hike to Havasu Campground from Hualapai Hilltop. From the trailhead, it was both harder and much more beautiful than I expected. As something of an “influencer destination,” I anticipated the approach to be easier than it was. Even if you pay to have your pack carried in by mule, you’ve still got roughly 10-11 miles of hiking through the canyon before camp.

Most stories I’ve read about Havasu glossed over the hike in. The trail winds through a rocky wash beneath towering walls of red sandstone, with bright-green trees and shrubs scattered along the canyon floor. I found it stunning, and that’s well before you come across any blue waterfalls. But it’s not easy: While the hike is mostly downhill, I don’t think many people realize how taxing 10 miles can be (the line for the helicopter ride out was proof of that).

The campground is first-come, first-serve. Some sites sit right along the creek, while others are tucked away in the oasis’s trees. With a four-day, three-night reservation, there was time to find a good spot as other backpackers left. I stayed somewhere different each night. They were all great.

The strikingly blue water of Havasu Creek cascades over several falls throughout the canyon. (Photo: Alex Moliski)

The Falls

From the campground, most visitors explore the main attractions: the 90-foot Havasu Falls, the 200-foot Mooney Falls, and, if they’re feeling adventurous, the stunning pools of Beaver Falls. Havasu Falls is right near the campground entrance, while Mooney is about a half mile downstream at the other end. Beaver Falls is another three miles beyond Mooney, following a rougher trail with rocky scrambles and multiple creek crossings.

Even at these Instagram-famous spots, I was surprised by how rugged the trail got. The descent to the bottom of Mooney Falls involves slippery ladders and chains bolted into the rock. Looking down at the drop below, I wasn’t feeling particularly confident. And that’s almost always where the real adventure begins.

The towering rock walls of Havasu Canyon are a beautiful place to explore. (Photo: Alex Moliski)

Hiking to the Confluence: Havasu Canyon Trail

Map distance: 12 miles out-and-back (from campsite to the confluence)
My distance: 22.11 Miles out-and-back
Elevation change: ~2,500 feet

My wife and I wanted to hike all the way to the Confluence, where the bright blue water of Havasu Creek meets the muddy Colorado River. My Gaia GPS and Strava both said the hike was six miles, one way. I’ve used those tools for years and generally trust them. So you can imagine my surprise when, three miles into the hike, we came across a sign pointing toward the Confluence.

“Six miles. ONE WAY.” All caps. Just like that.

Confused, I checked my GPS again. It showed only a few miles remaining, so we pressed on.

Past Beaver Falls, the crowds thinned out dramatically. The canyon narrowed, the trail got rougher, and the red walls rose higher around us. We skirted cliffs, scrambled over rocks, and crossed the creek repeatedly. A few sections made me wish I hadn’t worn water shoes. Other sections of the trail were in the creek itself. We got wet, but with temperatures pushing 100 degrees, that wasn’t necessarily a bad thing.

And the scenery? Havasu Canyon is every bit as stunning as the waterfalls. At one point, the canyon narrowed into what looked like a sea of green. Thick vines and foliage covered practically every inch of the canyon walls, emerald leaves spilling across the deep-red sandstone. It looked like someone had planted a vineyard in the middle of the desert. I’d never seen a photograph of it—never even heard someone mention it. And yet, there it was: a place every bit as beautiful as the waterfalls we’d come all this way to see.

The farther we walked, the more I realized how much of Havasu I’d been missing in all those photographs.

At one point we rounded a corner and found ourselves in a lush green canyon. (Photo: Alex Moliski)

And the farther we walked, the more I questioned my GPS. Eventually, my watch beeped at 10 miles, and we still weren’t at the Confluence. We were way out there. Several bends later, we rounded a corner and saw a huge group of people wearing swim trunks and life jackets.

“Where the hell did they come from?” my wife asked. They certainly didn’t look like they’d just hiked 10 miles through the desert. A few minutes later, we had our answer.

The Bottom of the Grand Canyon

Five large river rafts were moored at the canyon’s narrowest point. A leathery-skinned, deeply tanned river captain sipped a beer while his crew guided rafters toward a cool pool. The Colorado River ran brown, carrying its sediment downstream. Havasu Creek was still that impossible shade of blue. The two met and mixed, though not quite as dramatically as I’d expected. But that was it: The bottom of the Grand Canyon—the Confluence. We’d made it.

The captain raised a fist. “You coming from Havasu? That’s a helluva hike!”

We chatted for a while. Then he reached into a cooler and handed us a beer. Ice cold. There we were, deep in the Grand Canyon, 11 miles from our campground, drinking a cold beer with a group of people who’d arrived by raft. It wasn’t quite the Havasu experience I’d seen on Instagram—this was much better.

Two of the sheep whose herd wouldn’t let us pass until they were finished eating. (Photo: Alex Moliski)

A Long Way Back

The return trip from the confluence to camp brought our total to 22.11 miles, according to my GPS, with roughly 2,500 feet or more of elevation change over the day. And just when we thought the canyon had thrown everything it could at us, we encountered a herd of surly bighorn sheep. They’d apparently decided the trail was a fine place to stop for dinner and weren’t particularly interested in sharing it. We weren’t in much of a position to argue, so we waited until they finished feasting to finally get past.

A word of advice for anyone considering the Confluence hike from Havasu Campground: Don’t underestimate it. Bring plenty of water, plan for the heat, and give yourself an entire day. This is a serious desert hike, not a quick detour from the waterfalls. But it’s also extraordinary.

I came to Havasu for the waterfalls, and they were every bit as beautiful as I’d hoped—maybe even more. You could spend your entire visit swimming beneath them and leave perfectly satisfied.

However, beyond those famous blue pools is another Havasu. One with towering canyon walls, hidden green oases, river crossings, grumpy wild sheep, and miles of wild desert scenery that rarely make it into the photographs.

Check Gutters and Downspouts

Water that accumulates around a home during winter can cause foundation damage as it freezes and thaws. Before the first frost comes, clean leaves and other debris from the gutters and downspouts, check for leaks and extend downspouts so they carry water at least 10 feet away from the house.

Common mistake: “Don’t clean the gutters too early in the fall, before nearby trees have finished shedding their leaves,” says remodeling expert Michael DiMartino.

Disconnect Garden Hoses, Sprinklers and Outdoor Faucets

Many outdoor water systems fall victim to the first freeze, bursting as the water inside of them expands into ice. To prevent this:

  • Disconnect hoses from bibs.
  • Install spigot covers, if you don’t have frost-free models.
  • Drain garden hoses.
  • Turn off and drain the sprinkler system.

“It’s also a good idea to put the hoses and other garden equipment in a shed, so they’re covered and out of the elements,” says Tripp.

Protect Exposed Plumbing

Freezing water can split plumbing pipes. Then, once temperatures rise, the water leaking out can cause significant damage. To prevent this, use foam insulation sleeves, insulation tape or heat tape around pipes that run through unheated areas, such as garages, crawls spaces, basements, attics and exterior walls.

“Also, look for gaps around windows, doors, foundations and where pipes enter the home, and seal them properly, since cold drafts can make vulnerable plumbing even more susceptible to freezing,” says plumber Tim Fancher. As a bonus, this will also help keep mice and spiders out.

Pro-tip: know where your main water shutoff is, says Fancher. If a pipe does burst, quickly shutting off the water will help mitigate damage.

Prune Trees and Prep Lawn

Frost can cause weak trees limbs to fall, potentially damaging your roof or car, so trim trees before the winter season begins. That will also keep dead leaves from accumulating on your roof.

While you’re at it, prune shrubbery and cut back perennial plants. “Before the frost comes is also a good time to put in your winter fertilizer,” says Tripp. “Blend on your lawn and in your flowerbeds so that it will percolate down into the soil over the winter.”

Check Windows and Doors

Cracks or damaged seals can let in moisture, which will cause further damage, plus invite cold drafts. Before the first frost, caulk any gaps and fix damaged weather stripping. “This will also save you a lot of money on your heating bill, plus keep moisture and mold out of your house,” says Tripp.

Also, fogging between your window panes is often a sign that the window seal has failed, and the window may not be insulating your house as well as it once did, says DiMartino.

Inspect Your Roof

Any damage to your roof will likely get worse once temperatures drop. Before the first frost, visually inspect your roof for missing, loose or damaged shingles and signs of water intrusion. Also, check the attic for moisture.

Pro-tip: “If you notice moisture in the attic or leaks in the home’s living spaces, it could be a sign that the roof needs to be repaired or replaced,” says DiMartino.

Tune Up the HVAC

A freezing evening is no time to have the furnace fail. That could lead to burst pipes and even jeopardize your family’s safety. Home improvement expert Eric Goranson recommends hiring a pro to inspect your system every year, plus tune it for maximum efficiency.

While you’re at it, don’t forget to winterize your air conditioning system. “It’s not completely necessary to cover the outside condenser unit, but it’s a good practice to cover it or wrap it to protect it from frost,” says Tripp.

Safety check: Before using your furnace or fireplace in the fall, check your chimney for debris like spiderwebs and bird nests, which could cause it to not vent properly, or even catch on fire.

Get Your Car Ready for the Cold

Cold weather can reduce your battery’s cold cranking amps, making it hard to start on the first cold morning, says Goranson. Before the first frost, test your battery, check your tires and windshield wipers and bring other neglected maintenance up to date.

Pro-tip: Run a Top Tier fuel through your vehicle at least once a month. “Those have cleaners in them that help keep your engine’s fuel system clean,” says Goranson.

Winterize Lawn Care Equipment

Drain fuel or put fuel stabilizers in gas-powered lawnmowers, trimmers and blowers, before stowing them in the shed. “The best trick I was ever told, which has worked for over 30 years, is to drain the fuel out of your landscape equipment, then start it up and let it run until it’s completely out of gas,” says Tripp. “That gets all the fuel out of your carburetor so that it does not varnish or gum up, and it will start in the spring every time.”

For electric-powered equipment, charge the batteries to the manufacturer-recommended levels, then leave them in a temperature-controlled space.

Check the Garage

If your garage isn’t insulated, chances are there are items in there that won’t fare well when temperatures drop. Especially check the storage instructions on paints and other liquids, plus battery-powered equipment. Also, don’t forget to check the seals on your garage door.

Pro-tip: Move shovels and ice melt to a place where they’re easy to access, leave a clear walking path through the garage and designate somewhere to put wet boots and tools, says garage expert Alan Bernau Jr. “You want to be able to use the garage in winter, not just cram everything into it,” he says.
