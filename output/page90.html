When you think of the fall season, chances are that gorgeous foliage, brisk sweater weather, and Halloween decorations come to mind. Deer, on the other hand, think of sex. 

Deer breeding behavior, known as the rut, aligns with the changing length of daylight. As daylight decreases, buck (male deer) and doe (female deer) hormones increase. While in southern states the rut can last several months, it only runs for a short period of time during the fall in the Northeast. 

During the rut, bucks seek out does that are ready to breed, and the amorous pursuit can be intense. Because some bucks are so obsessed with breeding, they can eat little and burn through up to 20 percent of their body weight during the season. And just like love-crazed high schoolers, deer during the rut are more distracted and less cautious, as well as more active during the day than they usually are. 

“During this period, many yearling bucks leave their birth areas in search of new territory, sometimes dozens of miles away. At the same time, adult bucks are actively roaming their home ranges in pursuit of doe, often chasing those they encounter,” according to Raven Ridge Wildlife Center in Pennsylvania. 

What’s more, “The end of daylight-saving time means more vehicles will be on the road during dusk and dawn, which are the peak hours for deer movement.”

In fact, most deer-vehicle accidents take place in October through December. While there are things you can do to lessen your chances of hitting one—such as staying alert and paying close attention when you see deer crossing signs—sometimes accidents are unavoidable. So what should you do if you hit a deer?

Safely pull over and turn your emergency flashers on. While your instinct might be to go see the deer or move the body off of the road, resist the temptation. Care for any injured passengers, and call 911. The dispatchers will send the appropriate authorities to help. 

And if you narrowly avoided one deer, remember that, “deer often travel in groups and walk single file,” per the Raven Ridge Wildlife Center. “So even if one deer successfully crosses the road in front of a driver, it doesn’t mean the threat is over. Another could be right behind it.”

A free flight is rewarding, but so is knocking $100 off your credit card bill. Cash back is less flashy, but far more straightforward: you can use it wherever your budget needs it most. The question is whether your purchases earn a forgettable 1% back or enough to make a noticeable dent in your next statement.

By matching cards to your spending and layering in welcome offers, bonus categories and card-linked deals, you can earn more cash back on purchases you were already planning to make.

Understand your spending habits

Knowing how you spend your money is an important step before applying for a new cash-back credit card. Do you spend heavily on specific types of purchases, such as dining and travel? Or does your spending shift from month to month?

This is also a great time to consider what you want from your rewards credit card and how you would maximize cash-back rewards.

To understand your spending habits, take some time to review charges on any existing credit cards. Issuers like Chase assign categories to card charges, making it easy to identify what types of transactions dominate your budget.

Whether you're spending on ride-hailing services or on groceries, mapping out your spending habits will help you pick the best card.

Once you've identified how you spend, you can select a cash-back credit card that complements your typical spending habits. No single cash-back card will earn the highest rate on every purchase, but knowing this can help you compare options that coincide with your particular needs.

This Sunday, conservative pastors will preach against Democrats’ claims to Christian faith

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

When Robert Jeffress takes the pulpit at First Baptist Dallas on Sunday, he’ll give his 16,000 congregants a not-so-subtle message: Beware of false faith.

Jeffress will preach about the Tower of Babel, a topic that shares “some parallels to what's happening in the election,” he said in a recent interview with POLITICO.

“My goal as a pastor is not to get people in the voting booth, but to keep them out of hell,” the archconservative megachurch pastor, televangelist and longtime Republican backer said. Texas Democratic Senate nominee James Talarico, a Presbyterian seminarian, “seems harmless,” Jeffress continued, “but the toxic faith he is preaching will lead people away from heaven, not toward it.”

On Sunday morning, conservative evangelical pastors across the country will be sharing a similar message, with the hope of galvanizing their congregations to support right-wing candidates and causes on Election Day.

But the big difference from recent years: They’ll be preaching against Democrats in four battleground U.S. Senate and House races who’ve made their faith a key part of their campaign message.

Talarico is a few classes short of becoming an ordained minister. Kansas Senate Democratic nominee Adam Hamilton is pastor of the largest United Methodist church in the country. In Iowa, Presbyterian pastor Lindsay James and Lutheran minister Sarah Trone Garriott are running for Congress. All four are in tight races in red territory, according to polls.

They’ve all made their faith an explicit part of their closing message.

“Jesus said, ‘Do unto others as you'd have them do unto you.’ This is really important. All of these things guide us,” Hamilton says in his stump speech.

“I approve this message because my faith teaches me that caring for my neighbor is not optional,” James says in a face-to-camera ad.

It’s a marked deviation from decades of the Democratic Party’s gradual march toward a less religious tone that has matched their increasingly secular voting base, and an effort to win over politically moderate believers who have grown disgruntled with Trump and the Republicans.

These Democrats' campaigns have put the country’s conservative evangelical pastors in unfamiliar territory: needing to convince their congregants that self-described fellow believers deserve their condemnation, not their vote.

“There are some Christians that don’t know how to respond to it, because [the candidates] are using all the same language that we do, but they are advocating for a lot of things that we are not for,” said Josh Bingaman, lead pastor of the First Church Open Bible in Des Moines, Iowa.

Many conservative pastors contend these Democrats are wolves in sheep’s clothing, pointing out disagreements on abortion, same-sex marriage, transgender rights or even the nature of God.

In Wichita, Kansas, Rob Rotola of Word of Life Church will lay out the stakes of the election in his Sunday sermon.

He said he has “no problem” calling Hamilton a false prophet, and imagines when his sermon is through, his congregants will “feel sorry for Adam Hamilton and would pray for him, but would tell their friends where he stands.”

In Iowa, Mike Demastus of Fort Des Moines Church of Christ plans to remind his congregants of “the importance of every single one of us, as believers, to go and vote and be engaged in this process.” He doesn’t include Trone Garriott among those “believers”: “She's a poser and a fake, and most conservative evangelical Christians see through that as quickly as she can say it.”

Bob Vander Plaats, an influential evangelical leader in the state, called Trone Garriott a “dangerous politician” and dismissed James as straying from the true path.

“To hijack Scripture to make it fit your agenda, versus to embrace the comprehensive nature of Scripture to fit God's agenda, that's a very, very risky thing to do,” Vander Plaats said in an interview.

A spokesperson for Talarico pointed to the candidate’s past statements on faith, including his assertion that “when I take on the politicians who are serving themselves and not the people, I’m doing it on biblical grounds.”

Trone Garriott spokesperson Zach Schenkman said that “while others try to divide Iowans and dictate how every Christian should practice their faith, Sarah is focused on showing up to make a difference for her neighbors.”

James spokesperson Jackson Smith said: “No politician gets to define or dictate how any Iowan practices their faith, and Iowans are disgusted by this kind of politics.”

Hamilton’s campaign declined to comment.

Trump has long enjoyed overwhelming support from white evangelical Christians: In 2024, exit polls found more than eight in 10 white evangelical Protestants voted for the president. Even now, as his approval rating among other demographic groups has declined sharply, Trump’s support remains relatively strong with white evangelicals, according to polls.

Some conservative evangelical leaders have long mixed faith and politics, criticizing Joe Biden for how he practiced his Catholicism, questioning whether Hillary Clinton was really a practicing Methodist, and attacking Barack Obama’s sincerity as a Christian (Jeffress once said Obama was "paving the way for the future reign of the Antichrist”).

Other pastors feel newly empowered by the Trump administration’s moves to loosen restrictions on tax-exempt churches engaging in partisan political activity.

Joseph Hall, lead pastor of Crossroads Church in Council Bluffs, Iowa, said that for weeks he has dedicated a portion of his Sunday sermon to talking about the midterms: “I am promoting ‘vote red.’ Most every Republican stands closer to our beliefs and people of faith’s lifestyle than obviously the Democrats.”

Jeffress, a longtime Trump booster, says he will not be explicitly endorsing Republican candidates this cycle. He argues his dalliance into the election is a moral engagement, not a political one.

“As a pastor, I'm reacting to what James Talarico is saying about his faith more than his politics,” Jeffress said. “Look, James Talarico's religious beliefs don't disqualify him from being a senator, but they do disqualify him from being a Christian pastor, or even a Christian.”

Heads up: The clocks are about to change. The evening of Halloween gets an extra hour as the clocks fall back an hour, meaning you can sleep in on Sunday, November 1. 

Complaints about daylight savings are as old as daylight savings itself, and pretty much everyone has an opinion about the practice. As with any such topic, there are all kinds of myths and misunderstandings out there. 

Farmers didn’t ask for daylight savings

This one comes up a lot: the idea that farmers are the reason we have daylight savings. In this version of the story, farmers, who work outside and need to align their workdays with the daylight, were the ones who made the argument for the practice. 

In truth, farmers were the main group opposed to daylight savings during World War I. According to the Library of Congress: “The agriculture industry fervently opposed the measure because farming schedules are based on sunrise and sunset not the clock.” 

After the war there was a bill to repeal daylight savings, which passed through both houses of Congress. President Woodrow Wilson vetoed that bill, and his statement about the veto acknowledged this wouldn’t make farmers happy. 

“I realize the very considerable and in some respects very serious inconveniences to which the daylight-saving law subjects the farmers of the country,” he wrote. Wilson sided with the industrial sector. The veto was overridden, leading to a long period where daylight savings was practiced only in certain areas. For example: New York City observed daylight savings, but rural areas in New York State did not (not what you’d expect if farmers broadly supported daylight savings). 

None of this is to say that some farmers don’t support daylight savings time today—it’s just that farmers aren’t the reason the practice exists in America. If anything, they tried to stop it. 

Benjamin Franklin didn’t invent daylight savings

There are some “facts” repeated so often that hardly anyone questions them, and “Ben Franklin invented daylight savings time” remains one of them. The problem is that it’s not true. 

I’d understand if you’re skeptical—I’m a Canadian journalist trying to steal an achievement from one of America’s founding fathers. But you don’t have to take my word on this one—The Franklin Institute, a museum dedicated to Franklin’s legacy, states that “Daylight saving time is one thing that Franklin did not invent.” The United States didn’t even implement daylight savings until 1918, well over a century after Franklin passed away. 

So where did the idea that he invented it come from? Probably from a satirical Journal de Paris article Franklin penned back in 1784. 

In that piece he jokingly encouraged the residents of Paris to wake up earlier in the morning, in order to spend less money on candles. He also proposed a tax on shutters, rationing of candles, a prohibition of carriages on the streets after sunset, and church bells ringing at the crack of dawn every day. He, it goes without saying, wasn’t being entirely serious. 

So where did the idea for daylight savings come from? New Zealand entomologist George Hudson first proposed intentionally shifting the clock in an 1895 talk to the Wellington Philosophical Society, then followed up with a paper in 1898. 

The US already tried year-round daylight savings

This isn’t a myth, exactly, but it is something that rarely comes up in contemporary discussions about daylight savings. Multiple US states have proposed doing away with standard time entirely. Nationally, the Sunshine Protection Act, which passed the House over the summer, would institute the change from sea to shining sea. 

Here’s the thing, though: this isn’t the first time. The US tried making daylight savings permanent during the height of the 1970s energy crisis. The problem was that people hated it. What was supposed to be a two-year experiment lasted less than a year because of a massive change in public opinion. 

Support for the change started high, as an October 1974 New York Times article lays out: 79 percent of the public favored the idea in December of 1973, but by February of 1974, that dropped to 42 percent—a 37-point swing. A bill to restore standard time in winter passed the House by a 362 to 16 vote. 

What caused the backlash? Dark winter mornings. Particularly prominent were complaints from parents, who didn’t like their children walking to school in the dark. 

A 1975 study by the US Department of Transportation concluded that a year-round switch possibly isn’t a great idea, proposing instead a schedule change. “We conclude that modest overall benefits might be realized by a shift from the historic 6-month DST system (May through October) to an 8-month DST system (March through October),” the paper concludes. This, notably, is pretty much what the US has been doing since 2007. 

The past isn’t a prediction of the future—it’s possible switching to year-round daylight savings would go differently today. Walking to school is less common, for one thing, and streets are better lit. All the same, it’s worth realizing this has been tried before.
