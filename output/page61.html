Aman At Sea: Amangati “Superyacht” Sets Sail Spring 2027, From $6K+ Per Night

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In 2027, we’re going to see the launch of a new ultra-luxury cruise line, as hotel group Aman is getting into the cruising industry, with the Aman at Sea concept, and its Amangati “superyacht,” as it’s called.

I’d like to provide an update about this concept, as sailings are now bookable well into 2028, so we have a sense of both the summer sailings in Europe and winter sailings in the Caribbean. If you think the recent entrants into the market are expensive, wait until you learn the details of this one.

It’s interesting to see the increasing trend of luxury hotel groups launching cruise lines, or perhaps more accurately, licensing out their names to partner on new cruising concepts. For example, in 2022 we saw the launch of Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection, and in 2026, we’ve seen the launch of Four Seasons Yachts and Orient Express Sailing Yachts.

Aman gets into ultra-luxury cruising industry

Amangati (“peaceful motion” in Sanskrit) is expected to set sail in the spring of 2027, as Aman’s first superyacht. Yes, this marks the Aman brand getting into the luxury cruising industry, on a ship that’s being described as a “floating sanctuary.”

The 600-foot ship will be around 23,000 gross tons, and will have a hybrid propulsion system, using battery power to reduce emissions and improve efficiency. It’s currently being built in Italy’s T. Mariotti shipyard, and it will be delivered to Neptune Co., which is a joint venture between hotel group Aman and Cruise Saudi.

Amangati superyacht rendering Amangati superyacht rendering

The ship is expected to feature a total of 47 suites, each with a balcony and floor-to-ceiling windows. The suites will be among the largest at sea.

Amangati suite rendering Amangati suite rendering

When it comes to amenities, the ship will have four restaurants, a jazz club, a 52-foot pool, a beach club and marina, a spa with a Japanese garden, two helipads, and much more. Sinot Yacht Architecture & Design is behind the design, and the intent is to take cues from traditional ryokan architecture.

Amangati pool rendering Amangati beach club rendering Amangati beach club rendering Amangati boat access rendering

For those not familiar, Cruise Saudi is owned by Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund, and is intended to promote the cruise and tourism industry in Saudi Arabia. However, it doesn’t appear that the plan is actually for the ship to cruise in Saudi Arabia, at least for the foreseeable future. So it seems to primarily be part of the country’s larger investment in the cruising industry.

Here’s how Aman CEO Vlad Doronin describes the idea behind the ship:

“The vessel will embody the very essence of our brand’s DNA, providing exceptional privacy, best-in-class amenities, now synonymous with Aman, and our signature unparalleled service not yet experienced on the water. As construction progresses behind the scenes, our vision remains clear: to create a vessel that not only represents the highest standards of maritime engineering and design but that mirrors the tranquility of our land-based sanctuaries.”

Aman at Sea itineraries & destinations

Aman has published the inaugural voyages for Amangati, for sailings between May 7, 2027, and March 31, 2028. Unsurprisingly, the ship will spend summers in Europe and winters in the Caribbean, with transatlantic sailings in November and March.

In both summer in Europe and winter in the Caribbean, sailings will be five to eight nights each, and they’re roughly what you’d expect in terms of itineraries. You can either book directly through Aman at Sea, or through a travel advisor.

An eligible travel advisor will be able to add a minimum of a $500 shipboard credit, while paying the standard rate. You can generally expect pricing parity between all booking sources, so you won’t find discounted sailing prices anywhere.

Aman at Sea voyage inclusions

Before we talk about the pricing for Aman at Sea, what’s included with journeys? It’s an interesting topic, because historically ultra luxury cruises have been all-inclusive. However, Four Seasons Yachts is countering that trend, by not including lunch, dinner, or alcoholic drinks.

With that in mind, Aman at Sea will include the following with the cost of sailings:

  • Luxury suite accommodation
  • In-suite breakfast
  • On-demand Suite Host
  • Unpacking and packing assistance
  • Daily laundry and pressing
  • Twice daily housekeeping
  • All-day dining at Alira
  • Light bites available throughout yacht
  • Gelato Bar
  • Soft drinks, coffees, and beer
  • Fitness Studio access
  • Marina platform and water sports
  • Pools, sun loungers and cabanas
  • Onboard entertainment and enrichment programming
  • Cinema
  • Youth Lounge
  • Starlink Wi-Fi for reliable connectivity
  • All onboard gratuities

As you can see, Aman is sort of taking a middle ground approach here. Alcohol aside from beer isn’t included, and neither is dining at the specialty restaurants.

Dining will only be included at the main restaurant

Aman at Sea per-suite pricing

How much are Aman at Sea rates? Pricing is per suite rather than per guest. As it currently stands, I see five-night journeys starting at $32,500, while I see seven-night journeys priced as high as $54,600. So we’re looking at somewhere around $6,000-8,000 per night for an entry level suite. You’ll generally find that Caribbean sailings are a little cheaper than European sailings.

Admittedly these suites are massive by ship standards, but that’s definitely on the very high end of the market, and easily 50%+ more than Four Seasons Yachts is charging (though with some more inclusions).

Aman at Sea voyages start at $6K+ per night

How does the Aman cruise concept compare to the competition?

Prior to 2026, I hadn’t taken a cruise since I was a kid. However, this year I’ve given all three of the luxury cruise lines associated with hotels a try, and I’ve shared my thoughts on Four Seasons Yachts, Orient Express Sailing Yachts, and Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection. I’ve even done a direct comparison of the three concepts, based on my firsthand experience.

Let me of course acknowledge that these cruise lines are all at the very top end of the market, so I’m not claiming or suggesting that they’re the best value options out there. Instead, I’ve taken such an interest in them because I find the crossover between hotels and cruise lines to be so interesting, and also find it noteworthy how these are all cruise lines targeted at people who historically haven’t considered cruising.

With that in mind, here’s how I’d broadly sum up the experiences offered by the three existing players:

  • Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection is the all-around best value of the three, given all that it includes, and it also has some interesting itineraries beyond just Europe and the Caribbean; admittedly this isn’t as exclusive as the other cruise lines, as the ships are the largest, the standard suites are the smallest, etc.
  • Four Seasons Yachts definitely takes design to the next level, and the ship is gorgeous, with large suites, an incredible pool, and a very family friendly experience; however, compared to its two competitors, the lack of inclusions is a bit disappointing, especially given that there’s room for improvement with food quality
  • Orient-Express Sailing Yachts is really the current top end of the market, with unbelievable design, amazing service, and the most inclusions of any of the existing player; if you can swing it, this is definitely the concept to go with

In general, I’d expect Aman at Sea will probably be closest to Orient-Express Sailing Yachts in terms of exclusivity, while probably being more like Four Seasons Yachts in terms of design (since it’s not a sailboat). I will say, given the price point, I do find the lack of inclusions to be a bit frustrating. Then again, the standard suites will be absolutely massive.

Is the demand there for this amount of luxury cruising?

The concept of luxury cruising is nothing new. However, there’s no denying that the industry is evolving a bit:

  • We’re seeing luxury pushed to new levels in terms of the exclusivity of ships, the pricing, etc.
  • We’re seeing cruising positioned differently; these luxury cruise lines are no longer just for rich retirees, but these cruise lines are also now targeting a younger crowd that might not typically consider cruising

Ritz-Carlton Yacht Collection is the only one of the concepts that has now been in service for some time, and based on what we know, the financial performance has left a bit to be desired. And that was before the cruise line expanded to three ships, and it’s before three competitors entered the industry.

Ultimately the success of these ventures comes down to whether they can convince people that cruising is “cool.” I don’t think these cruise lines will be cannibalizing brands like Silversea, but instead, I think they’re going after those who would otherwise vacation on land.

The other thing worth keeping in mind is that at some point, these cruise lines are competing with the concept of just chartering a yacht, which is a concept that will have more appeal for many people.

Obviously $6K+ per night is a massive amount to spend on a cruise. But the reality is that people easily pay that kind of money for peak season rooms in place like Saint-Tropez. Can these hotel-branded cruise lines convince people who want to “see and be seen” that it’s as cool to be on a ship as it is to be in the South of France? It seems like a tall order, but I guess time will tell.

Ironically, I think the volume of new entrants into the market is actually quite helpful in building demand, since cruises are finally being talked about more among non-cruisers. While they’re all competing for the same types of guests, I know a fair number of people have tried one of these cruise lines, then rebooked on another cruise line, etc.

Bottom line

Aman will be getting into the luxury cruising space in the spring of 2027, with the launch of its 600-foot Amangati superyacht. The ship looks incredible, and it seems like we’re seeing the luxury hotel groups all one-up each other.

The pricing is at the very top end of the market, which doesn’t come as much of a surprise. Will there be enough people willing to pay $6K+ per night for Aman’s cruising concept to keep these ships full, especially without most alcohol included? Only time will tell.

The Art of Being Alone: May Sarton’s Stunning 1938 Ode to Solitude

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

“There is no place more intimate than the spirit alone.”

“The best things in life happen to you when you’re alone,” artist Agnes Martin reflected in her final years. “Oh comforting solitude, how favorable thou art to original thought!” wrote the founding father of neuroscience in his advice to young scientists. The poet Elizabeth Bishop believed that everyone should experience at least one prolonged period of solitude in life. For in true solitude, as Wendell Berry so memorably observed, “one’s inner voices become audible [and] in consequence, one responds more clearly to other lives” — an intuitive understanding of what psychologists have since found: that “fertile solitude” is the basic unit of a full and contented life.

But in the neutral state of aloneness, the psychoemotional line between solitude and loneliness can be as thin as a razor’s edge and as lacerating to the soul. How to draw it skillfully in orienting ourselves to the world, exterior and interior, is what poet, novelist, and memoirist May Sarton (May 3, 1912–July 16, 1995) explores in a beautiful poem she penned ten days after her twenty-sixth birthday, decades before she came to contemplate solitude in stunning prose. Originally titled “Considerations,” the poem was slightly revised and published the following year as “Canticle 6” in Sarton’s second poetry collection, the altogether sublime Inner Landscape (public library).

CANTICLE 6
by May Sarton

Alone one is never lonely: the spirit
              adventures, waking
In a quiet garden, in a cool house, abiding single there;
The spirit adventures in sleep, the sweet thirst-slaking
When only the moon’s reflection touches the wild hair.
There is no place more intimate than the spirit alone:
It finds a lovely certainty in the evening and the morning.
It is only where two have come together bone against bone
That those alonenesses take place, when, without warning
The sky opens over their heads to an infinite hole in space;
It is only turning at night to a lover that one learns
He is set apart like a star forever and that sleeping face
(For whom the heart has cried, for whom the frail hand burns)
Is swung out in the night alone, so luminous and still,
The waking spirit attends, the loving spirit gazes
Without communion, without touch, and comes to know at last
Out of a silence only and never when the body blazes
That love is present, that always burns alone, however steadfast.

Complement with Louise Bourgeois on how solitude enriches creative work, Virginia Woolf on the relationship between loneliness and creativity, and Olivia Laing’s masterwork on the art of being alone, then revisit other readings of beautiful poems of existential radiance: Derek Walcott’s “Love After Love,” Wisława Szymborska’s “Life-While-You-Wait,” Jane Kenyon’s “Having It Out With Melancholy,” and Adrienne Rich’s “Planetarium.”

In a possible future version of this football world, Lewis Hall is winning the World Cup for England at left-back, while still a Newcastle United player.

At right midfield in the same team is Lewis Miley, Hall’s Newcastle teammate.

They have transferred their unique understanding at club level to the international arena.

However, in another not-so-impossible future, it’s easy to see them both as the next players that Newcastle United cash in on.

So, let’s forget about Fantasy Island, and in the interim, let’s see what the two of them could do in a Newcastle side reinvented tactically and positionally – while we’ve still got time.

I’ll start with Lewis Hall.

For a couple of games last season, Newcastle’s absolute best player, even with our former midfield talisman Bruno on the pitch, was Lewis Hall. From left-back, he ran the game. He moved forward into what would have been called a half-back position – just ahead of the central defenders but relatively narrow. From there his ability to tackle and pass gave a new dimension to the creative thrust of the midfield.

The central defenders would split left and right and one of the midfield trio would drop back into a back three temporarily to accommodate this switch.

All football tactics come down to freeing up some potential so you have more or better options than your opponents. It was obvious that Eddie Howe allowed this move or was even encouraging it.

It gave Newcastle a new dimension, allowing the attackers to concentrate on attack, and to make defence a much more attacking option, without necessarily sacrificing solidity.

I noticed Howe also seemed to be favouring Miley at right-back, and that he seemed to be slowly pushing him into a similar right-back/right-half hybrid.

In the world of number sixes, number eights and number 10s, it’s worth explaining what a half-back used to be.

The half-back was a player who tackled hard, broke up opposition attacks and linked defence to attack. At Newcastle, Jimmy Scoular was a legendary example, while Billy Bremner at Leeds offers a more familiar illustration of the combination of aggression, intelligence and passing ability. Modern football has divided many of those responsibilities between holding midfielders, central defenders and inverted full-backs.

Photo by The Mag

Last week, I polled my mates in our NUFC WhatsApp group as to who is Newcastle’s current best player and the resounding answer was: Lewis Hall.

He is strong in the tackle but he also has the attacking instincts of a midfield player. If he’s Newcastle’s best player, it would be pointless to limit him to full-back when he can do so much more without losing any of what he gives at left-back.

Lewis Miley might be less adaptable to – or even less keen – to play full-back, but he also has the tackling, vision, passing range and movement to make an excellent right-half – breaking up opposition play and recycling the ball in numerous, deft ways.

Since we lost our four best players – Isak, Gordon, Tonali and Guimarães – and then our manager, Newcastle have been left with a weakened squad and an inexperienced coach. It’s a monumental challenge to get anything out of the current group.

Some would go so far as to say the midfield has a gaping hole in it and that the attack is not much better.

The Bruno-Tonali-Joelinton midfield – the so-called ‘best midfield in the Premier League’ – was broken up and two thirds of it sold. But trying to play the same way with fewer, less gifted and less experienced players – two out of Joelinton, Nico, Ramsey, Steur or Miley – is doomed to fall short of the players and formation we lost.

Further forward, trying to replace the combined thrust and technical adroitness of Tonali and Bruno with Willock or Wissa also seems like no more than wishful thinking.

Matthias Jaissle needs to find some positional and tactical innovation based on the players he has got at his disposal. It’s not going to work just to impose a system he likes – be it 4-2-3-1 or the Red Bull vertical football – just because he likes it.

The Newcastle United players in this squad have strengths as well as weaknesses. Nico has an eye for a pass and is not as easy to get knocked off the ball as many might have thought. Dedić is a traditional overlapping full-back with strength in both attacking and defending. Osula is like lightning and has boundless confidence. Barnes has a few tricks and a neat way of finishing. Wissa has an excellent recent international record even if the weight of replacing Isak at Newcastle seems still to be weighing him down. And so on.

So let’s play to our strengths.

Give our best player (Hall, remember?) permission to push forward to left-half and dictate the game. Coach the central defenders and midfielders to accommodate that – Burn or Botman goes further left to cover the space behind him.

Let Miley operate at right-half, with Amar Dedić behind him at right-back, providing the overlapping threat. Miley can drop back to cover when Dedić advances, or move centrally to help control possession. Again, coach the right central defender to cover Dedić as well.

Photo by The Mag

With a midfield three of Joelinton, Nico and Miley, Joelinton would have the responsibility to fill the central position in a back three – to tackle, hustle and move the ball forward.

Give Nico more time to express his range of passing and even drift forward into a number 10 hole.

Let whoever is picked out of our multiple wide men have the time and space to break down the wings or cut inside, knowing they have Hall and Miley ready to come in to tackle, pass, dribble or push forward themselves.

The innovation would be having multiple players doubling up on responsibilities. No one gets the luxury just to be a striker or a winger or a defender; everyone has at least two things they do well.

These movements wouldn’t necessarily happen simultaneously. The point is to give Newcastle different ways of creating an extra man in midfield while maintaining cover behind the ball.

It’s not based on the coaching playbooks, but on the players we have, and it will take drilling and practice to get it right.

Let’s be first hard to beat and then hard to stop.

‘The best thing that you can do for your career is to make yourself redundant’: Inside Stellantis’ AI push after the ‘starting gun went off’

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Grand Cherokee is lined with wood paneling, its windshield is cracked, and chunks are cut out of its side-view mirrors like pierced ears. That’s where the cameras go, a grinning Joydip Saha tells me from behind the wheel. The manager of Stellantis’ hands-free driving project lovingly taps what he calls “the most broken-up car,” jerry-rigged with a yellow box sitting on the dashboard that lets a human grab control. It’s an industry-standard “kill switch” used in many prototype vehicles. In technical terms, the safety driver can hit the kill switch to disconnect power to the advanced driver assist system module, stopping the ADAS system from controlling the vehicle. Then, the driver has full control over the vehicle. 

Another piece of equipment connected to the kill switch — the STLA AutoDrive L2++ system embedded in the vehicle — beeps when it needs him to take control, a bit like a dog wearing a smart collar.

And it’s confused. We are in the bowels of the Chrysler Technology Center in suburban Auburn Hills, Mich., navigating through the rabbit warren of a parking structure on the campus built by Lee Iacocca, the former Fortune posterboy who broke ground on the 500-acre site in 1986 and dedicated it in 1991, at a cost the company puts at about $1 billion. With the headquarters tower added in 1996, it spans 5.4 million square feet, the second-largest building in the U.S. by floor space, behind only the Pentagon.

Stellantis still runs its North American engineering out of Iacocca’s building, and the people who work there still say “all under one roof.” Inside, it’s a pure shot of early ‘90s design. Skinny escalators climb skyward around an octagonal skylight, like a Clinton-era MC Escher drawing. 

The interior of the Chrysler Technical Center. courtesy of Stellantis

The diamond-shaped parking garage attached to it is unique and confounding. Saha’s development mule heads toward the wrong exit and Saha has to put his hands on the wheel Even he finds the garage complicated. Auburn Hills’ suburban roads are much better for a test drive and I watch Saha’s mule smoothly navigate an intersection where two Michiganders decide to both make illegal multi-lane changes within seconds of each other. 

Saha, the global engineering manager who serves as the owner of Stellantis’ L2++/hands-free door-to-door supervised automated driving system, has been at this longer than most. He worked on the Chrysler Pacifica that Stellantis supplied for Waymo’s first robotaxi in Arizona, then managed a group that integrated Level 4 autonomy with partners including Aurora and Lyft. He still test-drives every new software build himself, as often as twice a week, because he considers any problem a customer faces problems  to be his responsibility. He said his team built the Cherokee’s hands-free system in six weeks with the help of AI tools, when a similar project used to take a year or even 18 months. 

He has a rule for AI, though: “I will not let people use AI to architect stuff because they will forget how to debug stuff,” he said. In a safety-critical business, he adds, engineers have to be able to answer every question.

Joydip Saha courtesy of Stellantis

Across two days of interviews at the technology center, engineers told Fortune that AI has turned yearlong projects into weeks and overnight simulations into minutes. Stellantis wants that speed to cut development time on its new computing and cockpit software, STLA Brain and STLA SmartCockpit as part of a general push to speed vehicle development from four years to 24 months, starting with two models in 2027. But nearly everyone who described a speed-up also described checking the machine’s work. Stellantis also has problems bigger than time savings. The company has 14 brands, a European business that is losing money, and a second-quarter adjusted operating margin of 1.8%, while CEO Antonio Filosa has set a margin target of 7% for 2030.

Wall Street isn’t waiting. Stellantis is the member of Detroit’s Big Three most exposed to Europe, which accounted for about 38% of its 2025 revenue. (GM sold its European brands, Opel and Vauxhall, in 2017 to PSA, one of the companies that later merged to form Stellantis.) Europe also leans much harder toward electric vehicles than truck-happy America: : battery-electric cars made up 17.4% of new EU registrations last year, according to ACEA, compared with 7.8% of U.S. sales, according to Cox Automotive. 

Stellantis had also bet heavily on EVs in North America, and it took the biggest hit in Detroit after the $7,500 federal EV tax credit ended on Sept. 30, 2025. In February, Stellantis wrote down roughly €22.2 billion (about $26.5 billion), more than Ford’s $19.5 billion charge in December or the $7.6 billion GM took across two charges. Stellantis shares fell 25% that day, and the reverberations are still being felt, with the stock down nearly 60% year-to-date. 

Mel Roberts, the director of North American infotainment, who plans the features inside Stellantis vehicles five to eight years out, said AI was not her biggest surprise of the last several years. “What I couldn’t anticipate is just the complete collapse of the electrification ambitions and how that really did affect all of our future plans,” she said. “It trickled into everything.”

Detroit’s European giant

Stellantis is the only one of Detroit’s Big Three to be legally headquartered abroad, in the Netherlands. It’s the result of a pandemic-era merger in 2021 between Fiat Chrysler and the PSA Group that brought iconic European brands such as Peugeot and Citroën together with Fiat, Jeep and Ram. That gives it a distinctly international air, as Fortune met executives born in France, Mexico and Bangladesh within minutes of each other, along with a Michigander back from a few years stationed in Paris. 

The mergers also left behind a machine built by committee. Ben Zawodni, manager of software engineering, is working to fold the hundreds of electronic control units, each a small computer with its own job, into a central “brain” called, naturally, STLA Brain. Over the decades, he says, the car’s architecture had evolved over time to really mirror the supply chain. “We were bolting on features.” 

Amanda French, who leads the company’s platform teams, is tackling the same problem one layer down. Stellantis historically built separate platforms, each to a fixed size. Its new platform, STLA One, is built to stretch from small B-segment cars to midsize D-segment vehicles. The company says it is designed to grow into a mega platform supporting 30+ models and targeting more than 2 million units by 2035. Asked whether building it felt like playing with Legos, French responded, “Yeah, with a lot more people and emotions involved.” 

‘Not there yet‘

Upstairs at the technical center, Chief Engineering & Technology Officer Ned Curic is a self-described “intense” but talkative and warm presence. He came to Stellantis by way of Amazon, with previous stints at Microsoft and Toyota. He explained that Stellantis is not where it needs to be. Some teams show “phenomenal successes,” he said. In building that culture, he said, “as a whole organization, we are not there.”

Curic sounds exasperated with an industry that has piled on complexity. Cars now come with “five screens, like digital noise, like 350 features,” he said, and automakers have historically tended to “checklist the heck out of the features” without measuring how people actually use them. He said he learned from Toyota that “the highest quality part that you have in a car is the part that you don’t have in a car.” In Stellantis’ research on drivers, for instance, he said most people preferred a mechanical stick to a button on a screen.

Ned Curic, chief technology officer at Stellantis. courtesy of Stellantis

At Amazon, Curic received Jeff Bezos’ notorious emails forwarding on customer complaints. The body of each email was a single question mark. “I never found it stressful,” Curic said. “Never.” One executive would handle the problem, he explained, and the others would each write a couple of paragraphs to help. 

From Amazon and Toyota, Curic also brought small teams from the “two-pizza rule” and single owners for each system, so engineers who once sat in separate groups can’t blame each other when something breaks. “If you see something that’s wrong, you just pick it up,” he said. “I own it.” 

The hardest idea he’s selling is that “the best thing that you can do for your career is to make yourself redundant.” He has done it himself several times by getting a team in good shape, leaving, and starting something new. For that to work, Curic said, the company has to make sure there is always a new opportunity for those people, and that leaders need maturity and confidence. Asked whether Detroit’s long run of hard times makes people defensive about change, he replied that while there is always a bit of a fear, change is a lot more accepted, ”and actually sought after.”

The man telling engineers to make themselves redundant is also hiring them. At the Paris auto show in October, Curic told Bloomberg that Stellantis would add “multiple hundreds” of engineers in Europe and the U.S. this year.

The starting gun

The pressure lands on engineers like Zawodni and French. Both of them are tackling Stellantis’ tricky margins issue by simplifying the wiring for each vehicle to bring the manufacturing into alignment, cutting costs and boosting Stellantis’ profits back to where they need to be. “The AIs aren’t going to tell us what the new platforms are,” Zawodni said. 

Zawodni said he started working on consolidating the cars’ central computing during COVID, when engineers went fully remote and Stellantis set up labs in India, Poland and North America that could be logged into from anywhere. A proof of concept for a global observability platform, which monitors software deep inside the car’s computers, took a week. Before agentic coding tools, he remembered something like that would take six months. And defects that took two to three weeks to fix shrank to two to three days. “We knew that we had to really reimagine all of our time,” Zawodni said. Did that scare him? “How exciting, right?”

French, a cheerful engineer from southern Indiana, lit up when the conversation turned to AI, though hers was a more measured take than Zawodni’s. She said human judgment “cannot be replaced,” but AI helps her pull together requirements and bills of materials stored in regional systems that were never built to talk to each other, because Stellantis is “made up from a number of different companies.” She compared AI to an interactive form of Cliffs Notes she wished for in school. 

Zawodni said his team was not all as excited about the AI breakthroughs as he was. “I think there was a starting gun, and that starting gun went off,” he said. “Some people heard it, and some people didn’t.” Of his own engineers, he calls it a mixed bag. “Engineers are really afraid of losing their core skills,” he said. They want to know why a semicolon in a coding language does what it does. He called that “a valid curiosity,” but argued that it doesn’t get Stellantis to its goals. He wants them to become architects, and eventually chief architects. 

The tools haven’t lightened Zawodni’s own load. “Agentic tools, they’ve made our work so much easier,” he said. “So why am I working so much harder?” He estimates he works at least twice as hard as before. “I feel a tremendous sense of urgency.” He said, “I really hope that we can transition responsibly … especially our people.”

Choosing battles

Anne Laliron, a French engineer who runs Stellantis’ roughly 400-person tech research group, gave up her office after COVID so her team can always find her, and she lights up talking about their energy. Her job is deciding which big bets to make. Stellantis can’t afford to do everything, so it designs battery cells in-house and partners with startups for the rest. “If you want to embrace everything,” she said, “you are at risk of spreading your effort.”

Anne Laliron, SVP of Tech Research at Stellantis. courtesy of Stellantis

For Stellantis, partnering is a way to punch above its weight. The company brings in Wayve, a British startup, for the perception software in its hands-free driving, works with the French startup Tiamat on sodium-ion batteries, with Zeta Energy on lithium-sulfur batteries, and owns a stake in the Chinese EV maker Leapmotor, selling its cars outside of China through the joint venture Leapmotor International. Laliron said the habit runs deep at Stellantis and is less common at other automakers. Curic wants a culture where “it doesn’t have to be invented here,” and he said the learning with Leapmotor runs both ways: “We learned a lot… we also taught them a lot.” Stellantis runs batteries the same way, Laliron said: design in-house, partner with startups, and leave mass production to its gigafactory partners.

Their biggest bet sits on a stage nearby: a battery-electric Dodge Charger prototype carrying a solid-state battery from Factorial, a Massachusetts startup Stellantis has worked with since 2021. The two companies run it as one team, and Factorial co-founder Siyu Huang says the startup has done it with $250 million in capital, a fraction of what some competitors have spent. The car worked the first time they turned the key. But Huang puts the technology three to five years from automotive production, and Laliron cuts about 10% of her group’s projects every year when they don’t pan out. “It’s always painful,” she said.

Still one car a day

Elsewhere, the speed-ups are real, but still small next to Stellantis’ problems. Arturo Guzman was among the first to bring AI into the company’s aerodynamics work, about five years ago, after a startup struggled to explain it to him in a side conversation at a conference.

Now he explains it to vice presidents, and he starts by taking the mystery out. “There’s no miracles. There’s no magic,” he said. “For us being engineers, all this is a Python code.” Run through his team’s model, a design change to a Ram pickup’s front splitter returns a drag estimate in about three minutes. The full simulation used to take 14 to 24 hours. He tells his directors it’s like an intern. His younger engineers get a different metaphor: R2-D2 from Star Wars. “You just give it a task and come back and see what it did.”

Arturo Guzman, manager for aerodynamics. courtesy of Stellantis

Guzman said the time his team saves goes to “other stuff.” His team now generates new guidelines, takes requests from across the company, and mines its simulation data for patents. “Now I tell them, just send me the data,” he said.

Through the doors to the wind tunnel, the giant equipment runs at pretty much the same pace it always has. It still tests “typically, one car a day,” said Steve Kubiak, a deadpan Michigander who started at Chrysler in 1992 and has supervised the tunnel for about 10 years. Asked whether the process has changed over time, he said, “No, it’s always been the same.” The analysis is speeding up even if the testing isn’t. A colleague, Todd Lounsberry, gives AI the data from the last 20 runs of a test and asks what it sees. Asked how someone becomes the manager of a wind tunnel, Kubiak responded, “First you get a wind tunnel.” 

Steve Kubiak, wind tunnel supervisor. courtesy of Stellantis

Other engineers are keeping AI on a short leash. Mohammed Malik, who runs Stellantis’ driving simulators from the Automotive Research & Development Centre in Windsor, Ontario, said his group is “keeping it in the box,” but even so, technology has transformed his work. Malik at work is like a life-size, immersive game of Mario Kart, the classic racing game where Nintendo characters pick their car and track then try to dodge obstacles to the finish line. 

His job has been transformed by his simulator more than by AI. Engineers used to tune a vehicle on a computer, build a prototype and only then find out how it felt. Now a driver can feel the computer model before anything is built, and Malik can swap springs on the fly. He can test 10 tires in an hour, something no prototype can do. His team has scanned about 20 roads, from racetracks to local streets, in 10-centimeter squares, and it trades models with colleagues in Brazil and Italy instead of shipping cars across oceans.

Malik plays me a video of a full-size Stellantis body that lacks any wheels — instead it’s suspended on what appear to be mechanical spider-legs, bobbing up and down, back and forth in front of a giant TV screen with a track. Asked whether he ever played Mario Kart, he said he has a track of his own, built by a student. “You can drive the Rainbow Road if you want,” he beams from behind his bushy mustache.

Mohammed Malik in action in Windsor, Ontario. courtesy of Stellantis

AI’s role is narrower. A simulator run produces thousands of channels of data, and when a car hits a bump and “swirls a little bit,” Malik asks AI which channel to look at. His ambition is much bigger: feed an agent every test driver’s scorecards from every region, ask it “what does it take to make a Jeep,” and let it drive the simulator itself until the answer feels right. For now, he said, the company is moving at a very slow pace and wants some form of control. “Some of the stuff we want to do, we can do today,” he said. “And some of the stuff we want to do, we cannot.”

The bigger surprise has been people’s willingness, or lack thereof, to adapt. Veteran test drivers who spent 10 or 20 years in physical prototypes were slow to move to the simulator. Young engineers with racing setups in their basements took to it right away. Laliron has seen the same thing. Experienced engineers carry old failures with them, she said, and sometimes “their brain is closing doors.”

Some people have made peace with work that is better but not faster. Roberts said her AI research tool returns 10 to 15 sources, and she has to check every one. “I don’t think it saves time,” she said, though it makes her work more “robust.” 

One more cycle

Alison Rahm, the chief vehicle engineer for midsize and large SUVs, has seen this before. She is moving back to Michigan after two years running Stellantis’ European car lineup from Paris, and she remembers a staff meeting in 1997, when her boss told the room they were getting something new called email. “All of us were in a total panic,” she said. They went back to their desks and tried it anyway.

AI now runs checks that people used to do by hand, Rahm said, like going down a list of about 1,000 engineering design practices. That frees engineers to ask whether the rules themselves are right. But she isn’t ready to stop double-checking. For years after virtual crash testing arrived, she notes, engineers still crashed real cars into the walls to convince themselves the situations were right. 

Asked how long it will take to trust AI the same way, Rahm paused. “I think we need another life cycle to prove to ourselves that our assumptions were right.” While cautioning that different products have different life cycles, she deliberated. “I would say four years.” That would land in 2030, the year Filosa has promised a 7% margin.

Saha drives us back into Iacocca’s garage, and puts his hands back on the wheel of the Cherokee as the diamond-shaped parking structure looms. The robot needs a guiding hand coming home. 

Saha takes a long view on when the AI game will shake out. As we roll into a smooth parking job, he turns to me and says he’s a Game of Thrones fan (his favorite character is Robb Stark). While he works in logic, he explains, he likes fantasy because he finds he needs “to be a little bit philosophical” with the other side of his brain. “Creativity is very important.”

Saha says he’s a Game of Thrones fan, more into fantasy than science fiction. courtesy of Stellantis

Saha takes a long view on when the AI game will shake out. As we roll into a smooth parking job, he turns to me and says he’s a Game of Thrones fan (his favorite character is Robb Stark). While he works in logic, he explains, he likes fantasy because he finds he needs “to be a little bit philosophical” with the other side of his brain. “Creativity is very important.”

Humans have adapted and thrived to date by figuring out creative solutions, he said. “We were never the strongest animal in the world,” he said, riffing a bit in the style of Game of Thrones writer George R.R. Martin. He suggests that several hundred years of industrialization led people sometimes to get used to process-oriented thinking. “My understanding is we’re going to leave behind the process-oriented world because AI is going to help us. And my personal belief is that’s actually good.” 

“There will be some loss,” Saha said, as there is with any technological shift. But he pointed out that humans have adapted to the natural world through creativity over and over again. “People are going to figure out how to tame AI, too.”
